The critical essay

W*nking Off the Dog to Feed the Cat: Four Ways of Getting The Fall Wrong

By Roman Totale XVII

The Fall made a career out of rendering the question “which is the better song?” faintly ridiculous. Not because all their songs are equally good—some are glorious, some are dreadful, and a few manage both within four minutes—but because the band repeatedly changed the terms on which a song could succeed. An immaculate pop construction might be spoiled, improved or detonated by Mark E. Smith's voice. A riff might be a brazen theft and a startling invention simultaneously. A recording could sound unfinished because nobody knew what they were doing, or because somebody knew exactly when to stop doing it. The eight selections in this first F.A.L.L.O.U.T. bracket offer four particularly perverse tests.

Elves / Grammy Friday: what a riff remembers

Start with the most notorious borrowed furniture in The Fall's house. “Elves,” opening The Wonderful and Frightening World of The Fall (1984), takes a guitar figure that makes the Stooges' “I Wanna Be Your Dog” impossible to ignore. Calling it plagiarism is both fair and insufficient. The Stooges' original is a ritual: one riff, one demand, a piano note hammering away like a nail being driven through the floor. The Fall turn the same primitive insistence into something more fidgety and crowded. The keyboards begin to worry at the guitar; the rhythm section pushes rather than merely pounds; Smith sounds less like a man pleading for submission than a provincial prophet reporting hostile activity beyond the parish boundary.

That difference matters. Iggy Pop's repetition reduces language to a bodily impulse. Smith's repetition tends to multiply implications. His landscape is full of names, insinuations and half-seen adversaries. Even when the source riff is unmistakable, “Elves” doesn't feel like the Stooges in fancy dress. It feels like the Stooges' riff has been taken to an unfamiliar town and made to give a statement to the police. Brix Smith's arrival in the group is often told as a story of new accessibility; here, accessibility means an instantly graspable guitar hook serving a lyric that remains stubbornly inhospitable.

“Grammy Friday,” by contrast, cannot lean on a riff that arrives already carrying twenty years of rock history. Its more peculiar appeal asks for patience, and the pairing reveals a recurrent problem with Fall rankings: the obvious classic has the advantage of being obvious. A song that declares itself in its first bars will generally beat one that needs to rearrange your hearing. That doesn't mean the less immediate recording is a hidden masterpiece. It means that instant recognition is a criterion, not a verdict.

Bill Is Dead / Tough Life Booogie: tenderness and machinery

“Bill Is Dead” is still disarming because it refuses the familiar caricature of Smith as a permanently irritable man barking over a permanently irritable band. On Extricate (1990), after Brix's departure, the song proceeds with extraordinary restraint. The accompaniment doesn't underline the sentiment with an enormous chorus or a sudden eruption. It leaves Smith room to approach a melody almost shyly. The voice is cracked, unvarnished and recognisably his; its effect comes from hearing those limitations used as expressive resources rather than concealed.

It is tempting to declare this the moment when Smith reveals the sensitive man beneath the monster. That would be a disservice to the rest of the catalogue. Tenderness is already present in The Fall, but commonly disguised by grotesquerie, speed, jokes or deliberate misdirection. “Bill Is Dead” is unusual not because it discovers emotion but because it permits emotion to remain audible without immediately sabotaging it. Even the title resists a neat reading. The apparent intimacy is shadowed by a name, a death, an absence: a love song with a trapdoor in the floor.

“Tough Life Booogie,” from The Real New Fall LP (Formerly Country on the Click) (2003), approaches the body from the other direction. Its pleasure is in propulsion and the friction of repetition: an ensemble locking into a pattern and worrying it until the pattern develops a personality. That album's production is crucial to its effect. The band sounds forceful and defined, rather than merely chaotic, while Smith's interventions scrape against the machinery. Compare the two records and you hear how misleading the phrase “typical Fall” can be. One recording creates space for a vulnerable voice; the other makes the voice another moving part in a tough, persistent mechanism.

The two songs also belong to different versions of survival. Extricate sounds like a band proving it can emerge from an upheaval with its identity intact. The Real New Fall LP sounds like a later band discovering that discipline can be every bit as abrasive as disorder. The sentimental song is not the soft option, and the boogie is not merely the hard one. Both depend on a precisely judged relationship between Smith and the people who have to keep playing while he does whatever he likes.

The Quartet of Doc Shanley / Shut Up!: when awkwardness becomes form

The Fall's most exasperating records often contain the clearest evidence that the group were not simply making things up as they went along. Levitate (1997) is a case in point: percussion, electronics, abrupt edits and seemingly incompatible textures jostle for possession of the same track. “The Quartet of Doc Shanley” belongs to that strange territory where the listener keeps waiting for the recording to settle into a conventional song, only to realise that its instability is the arrangement. The busy drumming is not an ornamental flourish. It is one of the principal arguments of the music, continually preventing the track from finding a comfortable resting place.

“Shut Up!,” from Middle Class Revolt (1994), makes a different kind of awkwardness. That record has long suffered from being treated as an ungainly interval between more easily mythologised Fall eras. But the ungainliness is worth hearing rather than explaining away. Where Levitate often sounds like the pieces of a record have been flung into the air and caught in the wrong order, the earlier album can sound like a band wilfully refusing the payoff its own arrangements promise. Neither approach is automatically superior. The important question is whether the irritation becomes productive—whether a track keeps opening up after the first shock has worn off.

This is where a knockout contest is at its least reliable and most revealing. Songs built on withholding resolution are badly served by the demand for an immediate winner. But they also force a listener to say what, precisely, counts as musical development. The Fall's long history of two-drummer propulsion, lurching repetitions and arrangements that appear to ignore their own best ideas makes the question unavoidable.

Greenway / Senior Twilight Stock Replacer: the late-period refusal

Late Fall can be misheard as a gradual subtraction: fewer hooks, less agility, more rasp, more stubbornness. “Greenway,” from Ersatz G.B. (2011), argues for a more interesting account. Its grinding attack has a physical density that would be diminished by greater tidiness. The guitars do not need to sparkle; they need to occupy space. Smith's deteriorated voice is neither an accidental blemish nor an excuse to romanticise decline. It is one of the abrasive materials from which the record is built.

“Senior Twilight Stock Replacer,” from New Facts Emerge (2017), is an even later proposition. By this point the band had travelled so far from the nervous economy of Live at the Witch Trials that continuity was no longer a matter of timbre or personnel. It lay in the insistence that a song could be organised around a phrase, a pulse and a refusal to behave. The title itself sounds like a misfiled job description from a bureaucracy that exists only after dark. Smith was extraordinarily good at making English seem as if it had been translated into English from some more suspicious language.

There is a temptation to hear these last recordings principally as documents of a failing body. That can obscure the actual players, whose precision and endurance make the racket possible. The late group does not sound like an early Fall lineup running out of energy. It sounds like musicians with a different set of strengths, using weight, repetition and a deliberate lack of polish to hold the music together. In that respect the distance between “Elves” and “Senior Twilight Stock Replacer” is enormous—and the family resemblance remains unmistakable.

The wrong instrument for the right band

The four pairings don't produce a definitive map of The Fall. They expose why any such map would be suspect. A stolen riff becomes an original song; a love song refuses to become a confession; a rhythm section turns awkwardness into architecture; a late-period racket proves that deterioration and invention need not be opposites. The group had a talent for making the same critical vocabulary fail repeatedly. “Raw,” “catchy,” “experimental,” “tight,” “sloppy”: each term describes one recording accurately and misses something essential in the next.

That is the peculiar virtue of ranking Fall songs against one another. The result may be absurd, but the effort of explaining it makes the ears work harder. The best Fall criticism should do the same. It ought to tell us not merely that a record is strange, but exactly where the strangeness lives: in a guitar figure that carries the wrong history, a drum pattern that won't yield, a melody delivered against the grain of a voice, or a phrase that sounds as though it has escaped from an administrative nightmare. Anything less is just another way of saying that Mark E. Smith was a character. We knew that already.
