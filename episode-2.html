Episode 2 · Critical essay

THE WRONG KIND OF PERFECT

By Marquis Cha-Cha

On Can, department-store comedy, the treachery of a good chorus and the noise of The Fall refusing to behave

The Fall could make an apparently simple question — which of two records is better? — feel like an inquiry into what music is for. In this second F.A.L.L.O.U.T. episode, the bracket places eight tracks in four collisions. The results are illuminating less because the winners are surprising than because the reasons for choosing them are often incompatible. One listener admires precision; another wants the record to sound as though precision has been declared illegal. Someone hears a horrible synthesiser patch; someone else hears the entire point of the song. The interesting thing about The Fall is that both listeners can be right.

The opening pairing, “I Am Damo Suzuki” and “Put Away”, provides a particularly nasty trap for anyone hoping to define originality as the absence of borrowing. “I Am Damo Suzuki”, from This Nation’s Saving Grace (1985), wears its debt to Can in the title and the music. The pulse is not an incidental krautrock flavour sprinkled over an otherwise British post-punk record. It is the structural proposition: a repetitive, bodily insistence around which Smith’s voice can stalk and splinter. Ezra hears a beloved Can drum pattern reconstituted by another beloved band and is thrilled that such open appropriation produces something with its own authority. Phil hears a more technical achievement: a group routinely mistaken for incompetents controlling the minute dislocations of a rhythm that sounds as if it might come apart.

The comparison is useful because Can’s repetition and The Fall’s repetition do different jobs. In Can, the groove can create an almost utopian suspension of time: musicians sharing a pulse so thoroughly that individual exertion disappears into the machine. In The Fall, the machine always has an irritated foreman. Smith’s delivery does not float above the beat; it heckles the beat, worries at it, reminds you that a human being with a grudge has wandered into the mechanism. That is not a claim that The Fall improved on Can. It is a description of how a borrowed method acquires a different social temperature.

“Put Away”, from Dragnet (1979), makes the argument harder. Its attractions are less monumental: a grimy, bustling performance, a guitar part that refuses to behave like mere accompaniment, and the strange comic debris scattered through the arrangement. Phil hears a guitar solo; Brendan hears Scanlon’s playing and the double-tracked vocal but cannot stomach the kazoo-like intrusions and scatting. The podcast temporarily becomes a tribunal on the physical effects of kazoos. It is funny precisely because this is not an argument about a record’s abstract significance. It is about an actual noise, and what that noise does to one person’s nervous system.

The two records are not primitive Fall versus accomplished Fall. “Put Away” has its own severe intelligence: a production so compressed and scuffed that the embellishments seem to emerge from a room too small to contain them. “I Am Damo Suzuki” opens that room into a tunnel and gives the musicians a terrifying amount of space in which to repeat themselves. The unanimous vote for Damo is understandable, but the loser reveals a useful fact: The Fall’s supposedly marginal irritations are not incidental flaws to be edited away. Sometimes they are the event.

The next match is stranger still: “Shoulder Pads” against “Victoria”. Both are accessible; both are funny; neither fits the old caricature of The Fall as a band that can only communicate through hostility. Yet accessibility turns out to be a poor description of what either record is doing.

“Shoulder Pads”, from Bend Sinister (1986), has a keyboard figure that lodges itself in the head with the persistence of a television ident. The rhythm moves with a deliberate, almost relaxed confidence, and Smith delivers the words as though the entire business were mildly beneath him. The transcript takes a splendid detour into Are You Being Served?, Grace Brothers and the programme’s cash-register theme. This is not merely an amusing digression. It points to a peculiarly English form of pop grotesquerie: synthetic brightness, retail banality, clothes as social camouflage, a melody that sounds cheap until you realise how expertly it has colonised your brain.

The detail that matters is the lyric’s distinction between Lou Reed and Doug Yule. Smith is not simply displaying Velvet Underground knowledge. The line makes a tiny act of cultural sorting into an absurd social accusation. The song’s “Harlequin kecks” and shoulder-padded image belong to the same universe: people identified by clothes, taste, affiliation, and the minute humiliations of getting those things wrong. The keyboard is cheerful; the social world it illuminates is petty and sinister. That friction is the hook.

“Victoria”, the Kinks song that The Fall took into the charts in 1988, presents an inverse problem. Ray Davies’s original already makes patriotic exuberance sound suspect: the imperial singalong has a rotten core. The Fall do not need to invent the satire, only find a way of occupying it. Ezra makes the strongest case for their version through the video, where Smith’s sullen expressions supply a second, visual vocal line. The record’s pop competence is real, and the hosts are not immune to it. Yet the vote goes unanimously to “Shoulder Pads”. One reason may be that the Kinks cover offers the listener a recognisable object to compare against an original. “Shoulder Pads” seems to have arrived from a parallel department store where the tills ring in an unfamiliar key.

The third contest is where any tidy opposition between melody and abrasion falls to pieces. “Feeling Numb”, from Cerebral Caustic (1995), is discussed through the contrasting voices of Brix and Smith. The transcript reaches for Belly, Throwing Muses, Pixies and the Breeders, and these are more helpful comparisons than a general remark about The Fall becoming ‘pop’. The guitar sound has a particular mid-nineties grain; Brix’s melodic line does not soften Smith’s voice so much as expose its coarseness. Phil hears the production’s balance as a triumph. Alistair finds it too polished. They are responding to the same decision in the mix.

That matters because Smith’s voice is not always a disruptive ingredient added to a finished song. Here the prettiness needs the interruption. Remove the growl and the track risks becoming merely accomplished; remove the melody and you lose the pleasurable shock of hearing the two occupy the same space. The comparison with the Breeders is especially suggestive: melodic economy and distorted texture need not cancel each other. The Fall’s version of that bargain, however, refuses to resolve the conflict into harmony.

Then “Masquerade”, from Levitate (1997), arrives in an outfit apparently purchased from the least fashionable corner of a dance-music warehouse. One speaker hears the Shamen, another Utah Saints; Ezra hears gloriously cheesy presets and an immediate MDMA flashback. There is a serious critical question beneath the abuse. What happens when The Fall use sounds that already seemed second-hand when they were new? In a conventional pop production, the dated synthesiser is a liability. Here it can become a form of exposure: the machine does not pretend to be timeless or tasteful. It announces its cheapness and demands that Smith perform inside it.

Brendan’s defence of the song turns on the possibility that uncoolness itself becomes useful in Smith’s hands. It is not a universal principle — plenty of bad records are simply bad — but it describes why “Masquerade” remains more interesting than the charge of bad taste allows. Smith’s shifting pronunciation of the title becomes another rhythmic disturbance, a mouth refusing to respect the sleekness of the backing. The group votes for “Feeling Numb”; Brendan invokes the host’s veto and sends “Masquerade” through. An arbitrary decision, certainly. Also a fitting demonstration that Fall fandom is not a court of objective musical law.

The final pairing makes the earlier arguments sound almost polite. “Mister Rode”, from the late period, is heard as an overextended, relatively functional groove. Ezra wonders whether a guitar figure recalls Captain Beefheart’s “Pachuco Cadaver”; another listener hears the Levellers or, worse, Stereophonics. The comparisons are not interchangeable. Beefheart suggests rhythmic wrong-footedness, a guitar line assembled from angles. The other names suggest the danger of a riff becoming a kind of sturdy, unexamined rock furniture. The objection is not simply that “Mister Rode” lasts too long, but that it fails to make its duration feel necessary.

“Pledge!”, by contrast, makes repetition sound like an emergency. The discussion turns immediately to the enormous analogue-sounding electronics, the pounding drums, the bass and keyboards locked together, and Smith’s bubbling, corroded vocal performance. One host remembers seeing Silver Apples and imagines the synthesiser as a machine operated with gigantic levers. Another reaches for Shellac. These are unusually precise associations: early electronic music as dangerous physical apparatus, and post-hardcore minimalism as a method of applying pressure without decorative relief. The synthesiser is not a tasteful colour laid over the band. It is a blunt instrument competing with the drums for ownership of the room.

The late Smith voice is essential. By this point, his familiar snarls and vowel distortions risked becoming recognisable gestures, even a parody of themselves. But “Pledge!” makes that familiarity productive. The voice sounds as if it has been left too close to the machinery and begun to rust. A track about kidnapping Bono, as the conversation gleefully notices, scarcely requires an elegant narrator. It requires someone who can make a threat sound like a malfunctioning public-address system. “Pledge!” wins decisively because its ugliness has a purpose. The electronics are not bad taste redeemed by irony; they are the sound of the song thinking.

Across these four matches, the bracket has accidentally assembled a small history of how The Fall make music out of material that other bands might consider unusable: an openly borrowed Can rhythm, the keyboard jingle of an imaginary shop, a polished alternative-rock chorus interrupted by a rasp, a dance preset that has passed its sell-by date, a synthesiser that sounds capable of causing structural damage. The question is not whether the group could play properly. The recordings repeatedly demonstrate that they could. The question is why playing properly was so often the least interesting option.

That is also why the tournament works when it works. It forces someone to say not that a song is ‘essential’ or ‘underrated’, but that the guitar resembles Beefheart, the keys resemble a cash register, the scatting makes them ill, or the synth produces a flashback they would rather not explain. Such responses are partial, personal, occasionally absurd — and far more revealing than a reverent verdict. The Fall were not a monument to consistency. They were a series of arguments conducted with drums, guitars, cheap electronics and one man’s increasingly unreasonable voice. Episode Two catches those arguments in the act.
