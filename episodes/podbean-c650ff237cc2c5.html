THE ADMINISTRATIVE SUBLIME: A CHECKLIST FOR THE END OF THE WORLD

On a shopping list that became a masterpiece, an unsuitable cover version, the horror of the everyday and a guest appearance by the entire British comedy underground.

By Dr. J. Temperance, Department of Applied Grotesquery

I. FOUR MOBILE PHONES AND NO WAY HOME

“Dr Buck's Letter” is one of the strangest great records of the Fall's later career. Its premise is barely a premise at all: a list of personal possessions, consumer preferences and minor logistical concerns, delivered as though the inventory were evidence in a criminal investigation. Sunglasses, music formats, electronic organisers and mobile telephones are subjected to the kind of scrutiny normally reserved for political manifestos. Nothing of consequence appears to happen, and everything feels catastrophically important.

This is not quite surrealism, although the effect can be surreal. The material is too ordinary. Smith's genius is to expose the weirdness already contained in the language of lifestyle journalism. The celebrity questionnaire promises intimacy through possessions: what do you carry, what do you wear, what can you not live without? It offers the comforting illusion that identity can be reduced to a manageable list. The Fall turn the list into a nervous breakdown.

Georges Perec's *An Attempt at Exhausting a Place in Paris* offers a useful literary parallel. Perec records the seemingly insignificant movements and objects of ordinary life until the act of noticing becomes an obsession. Smith's catalogue operates in the opposite direction: the details accumulate so insistently that the person they supposedly describe begins to disappear. The list does not reveal the self. It replaces the self with its accessories.

Musically, “Dr Buck's Letter” makes the trick irresistible. The low, loping rhythm, clipped percussion and electronic textures establish an atmosphere of subdued menace. Smith's delivery oscillates between reading aloud and performing an accusation. The song never needs a conventional melodic climax because the tension resides in the mismatch between its trivial source material and the gravity of its presentation.

It is a masterpiece of administrative horror. Kafka's bureaucracy has acquired a personal digital assistant.

II. THE RIGHT TO MAKE A TERRIBLE NOISE

At the other extreme stands “Bombast”, the opening assault from *This Nation's Saving Grace*. Its introductory declaration, “This is new,” is a wonderful piece of provocation from a band whose music already had a reputation for obstinate repetition. The guitars arrive in a thick, aggressive mass, while the drums give the performance the momentum of an argument that has escaped its original participants.

The recording makes a useful companion to “Joker Hysterical Face”, whose unsettling title and early-1980s severity suggest the same fascination with threat, misrecognition and emotional instability. The difference lies in musical architecture. “Bombast” is a direct physical event, its riff designed to occupy the room. “Joker Hysterical Face” has a more oblique, shadowy quality, leaving space for the listener to imagine what exactly is so frightening.

Both challenge the comforting assumption that repetition is a form of simplicity. In the Fall's hands it becomes a means of intensification. The repeated figure is not merely played again; it returns with slightly altered implications because everything around it has shifted. The principle recalls the work of the Stooges, particularly the brutal economy of *Fun House*, but also the motorik experiments of Neu! and the collective improvisations of Can.

The important distinction is between repetition as a commercial formula and repetition as a machine for producing difference. A conventional pop chorus reassures the listener by returning to something familiar. The Fall's repeated figures can make the familiar increasingly suspect. By the fifth or sixth cycle, the riff begins to sound as though it knows something you do not.

There is also a democratic quality to this method. The rhythm section matters as much as the vocalist. The Fall are sometimes reduced to the story of Smith and a revolving collection of employees, but the records themselves make nonsense of that account. “Bombast” requires a band capable of making force sound structured rather than merely loud. The noise is collective, even when the personality is singular.

III. THE WRONG KIND OF SMOOTH

The existence of *Shift-Work* complicates any tidy account of The Fall as permanent enemies of polish. Its relatively smooth production, synthesised textures and relaxed vocal passages can sound startling after the harshness of the earlier records. Yet the group's willingness to change surface without abandoning its deeper methods is one reason the catalogue remains so difficult to classify.

“Shift-Work” itself is not simply an exercise in softening the edges. Its cyclical arrangement and peculiar atmospheric effects retain the group's obsession with repetition, even as the sound moves towards a more spacious, almost nocturnal register. Comparisons with Space 3—or rather Spacemen 3—are useful insofar as both groups understand the hypnotic possibilities of apparently simple patterns. But the emotional temperature is different. Spacemen 3 frequently seek transcendence; The Fall tend to find an administrative dispute waiting at the other end of the tunnel.

“War”, their interpretation of the Henry Cow and Slapp Happy song, makes the distinction clearer. The original emerges from the radical European avant-rock tradition, in which political language and difficult musical structures were deliberately combined. The Fall do not preserve the original as a museum piece. They seize it, roughen its surfaces and subject it to their own rhythmic instincts.

This is what a good cover version should do. It should reveal a previously unsuspected possibility in the source material. The Fall's covers are most successful when they behave like hostile takeovers: the original melody remains somewhere on the premises, but the management has been replaced overnight.

The relationship with Henry Cow is especially intriguing because both groups resisted the ordinary conventions of rock professionalism, albeit in very different ways. Henry Cow developed a highly disciplined form of musical complexity; The Fall often cultivated the appearance of undisciplined simplicity. Yet each made music that challenged the listener's expectations about how a band should function.

To prefer one approach is reasonable. To pretend they have nothing to say to each other is not.

IV. THE COMEDY OF UNWELCOME VISITORS

The appearance of Paul Vickers introduces another strand of British outsider culture. His work with Dawn of the Replicants, the Leg and the character Mr Twonky occupies a territory in which musical eccentricity and absurdist performance are not easily separated. That territory has a long history: the Bonzo Dog Doo-Dah Band, Vivian Stanshall, the Residents and the stranger edges of British television comedy all suggest that the distinction between experimental art and deliberate stupidity may be largely administrative.

The Fall's own comedy is frequently overlooked because Smith rarely signals that he is joking. A phrase can be vicious, ridiculous and oddly moving at once. His enthusiasm for some minor piece of daily experience may sound like parody, but the enthusiasm is real enough to animate the song. This creates a peculiar emotional uncertainty. Are we laughing at the narrator, with him, or because the world has become too strange for any other response?

Samuel Beckett's characters confront a similarly unstable relationship between repetition and meaning. They continue speaking, waiting and performing their little rituals long after those activities have ceased to promise a satisfactory outcome. Smith's characters are more likely to complain about the buses, but their predicament is not entirely different.

The title “The Fella from Tears for Fears Breathing Down My Neck” captures this sensibility perfectly. A minor social anxiety is inflated into an existential situation, while the precise identity of the threat remains comically beside the point. The sentence is almost a short story already. It needs no explanation, only an increasingly uncomfortable room.

V. CHEETHAM HILL, OR THE GEOGRAPHY OF INSULT

Place names in The Fall are rarely neutral. Manchester and its surrounding districts provide a vocabulary of class, accent, history and local antagonism. “Cheetham Hill”, from *The Light User Syndrome*, draws on that vocabulary while pushing the music towards an ungainly, theatrical kind of dance-rock. Its shifts in vocal tone and musical texture are not universally persuasive. At moments, the record seems to be parodying itself, and the parody has stayed longer than invited.

Yet the song's awkwardness is revealing. The Fall's relationship with their own regional identity was never a simple matter of northern authenticity. Smith distrusted sentimental celebrations of place as much as he distrusted metropolitan condescension. A district name could become a joke, a warning, a badge of recognition or an excuse to deliver a particularly ugly vocal performance.

“Contraflow” turns another mundane spatial experience into a song. Its subject seems almost aggressively unpromising: the frustration of disrupted movement, the road that refuses to behave like a road. But traffic is a perfect Fall subject. It combines bureaucracy, physical inconvenience, local geography and the infuriating presence of other people. The song's guitar-driven movement converts an everyday obstruction into a form of rhythmic resistance.

J. G. Ballard understood that the modern road system was not merely infrastructure but a psychological environment. *Crash* made the motorway into a landscape of desire and violence; *Concrete Island* imagined a man stranded within the machinery of urban mobility. Smith's vision is less grandiose. He does not need a spectacular collision. A badly arranged diversion will do.

VI. THE HORROR THAT LEAKS THROUGH

“Touchy Pad”, from *The Unutterable*, is a reminder that the Fall's electronic experiments were not always sleek or coherent. Its competing textures and strange vocal interventions can sound like several different songs attempting to occupy the same studio at once. The result is ungainly, but the ungainliness is part of its identity.

The temptation is to describe this as failure. Sometimes it is. Yet the track also demonstrates how the band's best and worst qualities could coexist without settling the argument. A promising rhythmic figure might be interrupted by an irritating noise; an absurd vocal gesture might suddenly become the most memorable element in the recording. The listener is forced to decide whether the collision is productive or merely accidental.

The uncanny atmosphere invites comparison with the fiction of H. P. Lovecraft, whose monsters frequently appear as evidence that ordinary reality has concealed a much larger and less hospitable order. The Fall's monsters are usually smaller, closer and more embarrassing. They emerge from telephones, television programmes, local newspapers and the corners of familiar rooms. A cosmic horror that requires an expensive expedition to Antarctica is less useful than one already waiting at the bus stop.

This is why “Dr Buck's Letter” remains such a powerful centre of gravity. It achieves without spectacle what the more obviously grotesque recordings sometimes struggle to accomplish. The ordinary inventory becomes terrifying because the record refuses to acknowledge that anything unusual is happening.

VII. THE CHECKLIST IS NEVER COMPLETE

These recordings offer no single answer to the question of what The Fall were supposed to sound like. One is an aggressive guitar assault; another a comparatively polished nocturnal groove; another an avant-rock cover; another a fractured electronic experiment; another an inventory of personal possessions delivered as if it were a message from the underworld.

The unity lies not in genre but in method. The Fall repeatedly take the familiar and make it untrustworthy. A riff becomes an accusation. A shopping list becomes a personality disorder. A place name becomes a social conflict. A road diversion becomes a metaphysical insult. A cover version becomes an argument with its own ancestry.

This method could be exasperating, and the results are uneven. The Fall were not a band that reliably distinguished between their brilliant ideas and their terrible ones. That unreliability is precisely what made the next record worth hearing. The possibility of failure was always present; so was the possibility that a song about four mobile phones would turn out to be one of the finest things they ever recorded.

The checklist remains open. The batteries are running low. Somebody has borrowed the organiser. The man from Tears for Fears is still behind you.

And the voice on the record has just remembered another item.
