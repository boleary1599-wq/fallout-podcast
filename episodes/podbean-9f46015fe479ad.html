THE PHARMACIST, THE PHANTOM AND THE OFFICE JUNIOR

On bodily malfunction, institutional stupidity, class anxiety and the recurring suspicion that The Fall were never quite fit for purpose.

By Dr. M. Hexen, Senior Correspondent for Unlicensed Metaphysics

I. THE HUMAN BEING AS DEFECTIVE EQUIPMENT

There is something sinister about the phrase fit and working again. It suggests recovery, but also a repaired machine, a reinstated employee or a defective appliance returned to service despite unresolved electrical problems. One is not required to be happy, merely operational. Mark E. Smith understood this distinction. Throughout The Fall's catalogue, bodies malfunction, language breaks down, institutions misfire and ordinary social interactions become exercises in administrative humiliation.

The episode's opening contest between “Fit and Working Again” and “God Box” immediately establishes this territory. The former's jaunty arrangement suggests recovery while its imagery implies an organism that has endured something considerably worse than a difficult Monday. The latter belongs to a universe where even transcendence arrives as another mysterious object requiring interpretation.

Kafka's Metamorphosis offers a comparison. Gregor Samsa wakes as an insect, yet his family's immediate concern is his employment. In Smith's England, the transformation might be less spectacular, but the obligation to report for duty would remain. A Fall protagonist could lose a lung, develop supernatural abilities and be possessed by an eighteenth-century magistrate, only to discover that none constitutes an acceptable excuse for lateness.

II. THE CHEMIST, THE ACCOUNTANT AND THE OCCULTIST

The Fall's Britain is populated by people claiming authority over systems they barely understand. Chemists dispense remedies, businessmen manufacture expertise, bureaucrats classify the unclassifiable and mystics offer access to hidden knowledge. Frequently these occupations appear interchangeable.

“Mr Pharmacist” and “Birmingham School of Business School” represent different ends of the same transaction. The former demands a chemical solution to an immediate personal problem; the latter promises institutional solutions to problems that may not have existed before the institution was established. The episode's excursion into chemists' counters, proprietary remedies and dubious preparations is especially revealing. Smith's imagination thrives on material specificity: products, buildings and actual men whose job titles become faintly threatening.

Michel Foucault described modern authority as operating partly through the classification and regulation of bodies. Smith's version is less theoretical: somebody wants pills, somebody controls the pills, and neither party appears entirely trustworthy. Meanwhile the repeated school suggests an establishment devoted to producing further establishments, each awarding certificates to the others. One can imagine a senior consultant explaining that the absence of coherence represents an exciting opportunity for stakeholder engagement.

III. THE DIFFICULTY OF BEING RESPECTABLE

Respectability is one of Smith's persistent enemies, although his relationship with it was more complicated than simple working-class defiance. He despised pretension, but also distrusted those who made opposition to pretension into a professional identity.

“Middle Class Revolt” and “Last Chance to Turn Around” explore this territory from different directions. One suggests rebellion transformed into a cultural accessory; the other an approaching personal reckoning. Together they create a Britain where everyone is furious about their position and terrified of losing it. Smith's characters might be revolutionaries, landlords, office juniors, failed poets or people who have mistaken a modest salary increase for admission to the ruling class.

Jonathan Swift's A Modest Proposal weaponises the voice of rational administration to expose moral obscenity. Smith often makes an apparently ordinary social voice reveal its own grotesque assumptions. Yet his suspicion extends to self-appointed rebels as readily as to their targets. The enemy is not merely a class, but the manufacture of importance: the person who acquires fashionable vocabulary and mistakes it for a personality. A middle-class revolt might involve refusing to attend a committee meeting while insisting the minutes be circulated.

IV. THE BEAUTY OF AN UNSUCCESSFUL EXPERIMENT

The Fall's catalogue encourages a peculiar critical generosity. Because Smith so often produced brilliance from unsuitable ingredients, admirers become reluctant to acknowledge occasions when those ingredients simply remained unsuitable. The garage-rock directness of “Mr Pharmacist” and the electronic complications of “Birmingham School of Business School” illustrate the problem. The former seems assembled from urgency and repetition; the latter from departments forbidden to communicate.

Neither approach is inherently superior. A primitive riff can gain sophistication through repetition; a synthetic sound can make conventional rock feel alien. Jean-Luc Godard's editing provides an analogy: colliding images can generate meanings neither possesses independently. But collision alone does not guarantee revelation. Sometimes a tinny keyboard is simply a tinny keyboard.

The possibility of failure matters. The Fall were remarkable partly because they took risks, and risks are meaningless if every outcome is retrospectively declared a triumph. Their best recordings do not sound inevitable. They sound like accidents somebody had the good sense to preserve.

V. THE CROOKED FAMILY TREE

The episode's excursion into The Yummy Fur raises another question: what does it mean to inherit The Fall's influence? The Glasgow band's miniature explosions of wiry guitar and absurdist energy resemble The Fall without functioning as respectful imitations. Their name, borrowed from Chester Brown's comic, places them in a wider culture of uncomfortable humour.

Their engagement with “Fiery Jack” shows that influence need not mean obedience. A song can be inherited, accelerated, distorted and returned with different fingerprints. This is what The Fall did with “Mr Pharmacist”, originally recorded by Californian garage band The Other Half. Its frantic energy survived, but its cultural location shifted from an American drugstore to the grimly comic possibilities of an English high street.

John Peel's programme was an ideal environment for such mutations. A forgotten garage single, a Scottish post-punk miniature and a Manchester group's latest reinvention could occupy the same imaginative space. Musical history became a disorderly conversation, not a procession of authorised masterpieces. The Fall's legacy is not a specific guitar sound or vocal mannerism. It is permission to misuse inherited materials.

VI. THE ELDRITCH MANAGEMENT CONSULTANT

By “Mountain Energei” and “No Respects Rev”, the familiar division between early and late Fall becomes almost useless. “Mountain Energei” demonstrates how a spacious arrangement can generate enormous pressure: pounding drums, clean guitar and Elena Poulou's electronic contributions create an environment both physical and unreal. “No Respects Rev” comes from another incarnation, yet shares the sense that ordinary experience conceals something abnormal.

The episode's discussion of eldritch opens a route into Smith's imagination. H. P. Lovecraft used cosmic horror to suggest that human civilisation rests on an appalling misunderstanding of reality. Smith's version is more localised and considerably funnier. His supernatural disturbances occur in recognisable places: a pub, a housing estate, an argument about someone's employment history.

Where Lovecraft's narrators collapse before incomprehensible knowledge, Smith's would demand to know why the revelation arrived late and whether its expenses had been authorised. The music makes the inversion possible. Repetition establishes a stubborn material reality while the words undermine confidence in it. The rhythm insists everything is normal; the voice supplies mounting evidence to the contrary.

VII. FIT FOR PURPOSE, UNFIT FOR CIVILISATION

What connects these recordings is not chronology or genre, but a fascination with systems failing to deliver what they promise. Medicine offers recovery but introduces dependency. Education promises expertise while manufacturing empty credentials. Class identity supplies belonging but encourages vanity. Technology promises progress while producing new incompetence. Even musical influence becomes productive misunderstanding.

The Fall inhabit these contradictions rather than resolving them. Their characters attempt to function within structures apparently designed by somebody with a personal grievance against humanity. Yet the songs are rarely simply pessimistic: their humour makes everyday irritation seem ridiculous and genuinely threatening at once.

This makes fit and working again an appropriate emblem. It might describe a patient, an employee, a repaired machine or The Fall themselves, endlessly reconstituted through changing personnel and incompatible ambitions. Fit for what? Working for whom? Again after which catastrophe?

Somewhere the chemist opens his shutters. A business school awards a certificate to itself. A middle-class revolutionary complains about the parking. A Scottish band plays too quickly to be arrested. A mysterious mountain emits an eldritch invoice. Nobody has solved anything. The Fall have simply reported for another shift.
