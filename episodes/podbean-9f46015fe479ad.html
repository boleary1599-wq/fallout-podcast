THE PHARMACIST, THE PHANTOM AND THE OFFICE JUNIOR

The Fall's great subject is not alienation but the absurd labour of remaining a person while institutions, chemicals, fashions and other people keep attempting to turn you into something else.

By Dr. M. Hexen, Senior Correspondent for Unlicensed Metaphysics

A Body Is a Workplace

There is something unusually sinister about the words fit and working again. They resemble the reassuring phrase on a doctor's note, the announcement of a repaired machine, or a personnel manager's delighted discovery that the employee's nervous collapse has concluded within the permitted period of absence. In The Fall's universe, health is never merely health. It is an industrial category. The body is an unreliable branch office; its organs are argumentative shop stewards; the boss has arrived to demand greater productivity from the lungs.

On Slates (1981), “Fit and Working Again” performs an extraordinary trick: its brisk, almost jaunty arrangement gives the impression of recovery while Mark E. Smith's words suggest an appalling recent history. The image of lungs encrusted with blood, remembered in the episode's discussion, is not exactly the language of a successful wellness retreat. The song sounds as though a man has escaped hospital, stolen the doctor's coat and returned to work without informing the doctor. Its piano and little acoustic inflections are not decorative concessions to musical civility. They make the performance more troubling, suggesting an entire social world determined to keep functioning around an individual who may not be functioning at all.

This is the Fall's peculiar kinship with Franz Kafka. Kafka's protagonists discover that institutions are simultaneously omnipotent and administratively incompetent; Smith's discover that they can be similarly deranged inside their own bodies. Gregor Samsa wakes as an insect, but a Fall character would probably have to catch the 8:17 to work anyway. The industrial North supplies the material background, but the metaphysical horror is portable. You could have this experience in a bank, a supermarket, a council office or the upstairs room of a pub whose landlord has begun talking about 'restructuring'.

A Brief History of Pharmaceutical Civilization

“Mr Pharmacist” is an especially revealing case of the Fall's art of adoption. It was originally recorded by the Californian garage band The Other Half in the 1960s. The Fall's version, released in 1986, is not a delicate act of historical preservation. It drags an already agitated garage-rock object into a world where the request for medication feels like both a joke and a threat. The familiar riff is a motor, and the motor has no particular interest in whether its passenger is medically fit to travel.

The original song belongs to the gloriously disreputable family of mid-sixties American records that sounded as though the singer had spent three days in a garage without locating the garage door. The Seeds, the Sonics and the Count Five made this sort of racket into an alternative national anthem. Its social world is suburban, but its emotional weather is distinctly medieval: obsession, hunger, persecution, miraculous remedies. The Fall found a way to make the old language sound freshly local. An English high street chemist is not a Californian drugstore, but both can become an altar to the little god of immediate relief.

The episode's discussion of “Mr Pharmacist” even wanders into the practicalities of chemists' counters, tonics, foot remedies and the kind of proprietary cure whose packaging suggests that it can fix your athlete's foot and possibly the entire twentieth century. This is an ideal Fall subject. The comic specificity matters. Smith's world is never just 'drugs' or 'illness'; it is products, brand names, transactions, instructions and the small indignities of asking a stranger behind a counter for assistance.

Michel Foucault's account of modern power emphasises its tendency to operate through the management of bodies rather than merely through spectacular punishment. A Fall record, less politely, might present this as a bloke in a dirty shirt demanding pills while an incompetent authority figure checks the stockroom. Neither is particularly reassuring. The record doesn't preach about chemical dependency. It makes the dependency rhythmic, funny and alarmingly catchy.

The School of Business School

The most useful distinction in The Fall is not between experimental and accessible music. It is between arrangements that seem inhabited and arrangements that seem assembled. “Birmingham School of Business School,” from Code: Selfish (1992), puts that distinction under a fluorescent light. There are samples, programmed surfaces, dance-music gestures and the sense of a band trying to operate a piece of equipment that has arrived without the correct manual. The track's odd title repeats 'school' as though a bureaucratic error has become a constitutional principle. It sounds like an institution that trains people to establish other institutions that train people.

The discussion in the source transcript is unusually concrete about why its disparate parts can feel resistant to one another: the sampled material, synthetic flourishes and Smith's vocal presence do not necessarily produce the same emotional temperature. This is a more interesting criticism than the familiar complaint that The Fall should never have touched a synthesiser. They had been using electronics, repetition and studio mischief for years. The question is whether the friction is productive. At their best, the band's incompatibilities create a third thing, neither rock nor dance nor spoken-word theatre. At their least convincing, the parts remain in separate rooms, sending each other internal memoranda.

There is an accidental parallel with Jean-Luc Godard's film editing: the collision between images can create meaning that neither image possesses alone. But a collision can also just be a collision. You cannot automatically promote every unfinished transition into a dialectical triumph. A tinny keyboard may be a deliberate affront to good taste, or it may simply be a tinny keyboard. The Fall's admirers have occasionally mistaken their favourite band's indifference to polish for a supernatural inability to make mistakes. The band, to its considerable credit, never promised such a thing.

The title's endless institutional loop also recalls the British management culture of the early 1990s, when the language of enterprise and efficiency invaded spaces that had previously survived perfectly well without a mission statement. In Smith's England, the salesman and the mystic are frequently the same man. One sells courses, the other offers transcendence, and both insist that the cheque be made payable immediately.

The Art of the Wrong Cover

Cover versions expose what a band believes a song actually is. A conventional tribute attempts to preserve the original's essence. The Fall's better covers behave like a hostile takeover. They retain the address and replace all the furniture. This is why their version of “Mr Pharmacist” feels like a Fall song rather than an antique displayed behind glass, and why the band's relationship with older rock and roll is more complicated than nostalgia.

The Scottish group The Yummy Fur provides a complementary case. Their ferocious miniature songs, wiry guitars and cartoonish velocity are audible relatives of the Fall, but not obedient descendants. Named after Chester Brown's comic, they took the comic's uncomfortable, absurdist sensibility into a Glasgow indie setting where brevity was practically a moral philosophy. The episode's excursion into the band's recordings, including “Colonel Blimp” and their version of “Fiery Jack,” offers a wonderfully crooked family tree. The Yummy Fur do not simply reproduce Smith's sneer. They accelerate the machinery until the singer has barely enough time to establish what he is sneering at.

Their relationship to the comic is important. Chester Brown's work can be intimate, embarrassing and unnervingly precise; its grotesquerie is not merely a decorative flourish. The best short Fall songs work similarly. A line arrives, seems ridiculous, then reveals that it has described an entire society in the time it takes a drumstick to strike a snare. You do not require a twelve-minute epic to suggest civilisation's collapse. A minute and a half and the right complaint about a chemist will do.

John Peel's radio programme was the ideal distribution system for these crooked inheritances. It encouraged a sense of musical history in which a garage record, a Scottish post-punk miniature and a Manchester group's third or thirtieth mutation might occupy adjacent positions without apology. This was not a genre. It was a method of listening, and its deepest principle was that no artist owns the last word on a sound.

Class Warfare in the Staff Canteen

The Fall's treatment of class is often misunderstood because Smith was suspicious of both the powerful and the people who announced themselves as their official opponents. “Middle Class Revolt,” the 1994 title track, is not a manifesto for a neatly defined political constituency. Its attack operates through tone, repetition and an almost theatrical impatience with respectable self-description. “Last Chance to Turn Around,” from The Light User Syndrome (1996), approaches the same cultural territory from another direction: a sense that the ordinary social self is always one mistaken decision away from a particularly humiliating revelation.

A Fall character might be a revolutionary, a landlord, a failed poet, a confidence trickster or a man who has been banned from three branches of the same pub. The categories are unstable because Smith's satire is less interested in assigning a correct political label than in detecting vanity and fraud. This puts him in an uneasy relationship with the English satirical tradition, from Swift to Alan Sillitoe and beyond. Swift's A Modest Proposal weaponises an administrative voice to expose moral monstrosity; Smith weaponises a voice that seems already to have been dismissed from the administration for insubordination.

The record's class comedy is not soft. There is a real anger beneath the absurdity, directed at privilege, imitation, cultural careerism and the sort of person who mistakes a fashionable vocabulary for an original thought. Yet Smith was equally capable of making his own side of the street look absurd. That is what prevents the songs from becoming campaign literature. Their enemy is not merely a class. It is smugness, wherever it is found, including in the mirror.

A Mountain, a Machine, an Eldritch Invoice

Late-period Fall records could still sound remarkably strange without resorting to the old idea of 'experimental' music as a heap of difficult noises. “Mountain Energei,” from The Real New Fall LP (Formerly Country on the Click), builds its peculiar authority from space, pressure and the interaction of simple elements. The source conversation notices the pounding drums, the comparatively open recording and the way the guitar's clean, tremulous bite cuts through. These are not vague compliments. They describe a band that knows how to make a few gestures feel enormous.

Elena Poulou's keyboards are central to this phase of the group's sound. They can illuminate a track without making it polite, adding a bright synthetic counter-world to the physical grind of bass and drums. This is an important alternative to the lazy idea that a Fall song is just Smith plus interchangeable accompaniment. The music often thinks in ways that the singer does not, and the friction between those thoughts is the art. A great Fall arrangement has more than one author, even when one of those authors is yelling over everybody else.

“No Respects Rev,” from Your Future Our Clutter (2010), belongs to a different decade and a different arrangement of bodies, but it shares the sense of an ordinary object opening onto an abnormal universe. The episode's late discussion turns on the word 'eldritch' and Smith's Lovecraftian interests. The connection is more than a convenient adjective. H. P. Lovecraft's cosmic horror depends on the terrifying suggestion that the familiar world is a provisional arrangement, held together by forces that do not acknowledge human importance. Smith's supernatural imagination often works from the opposite end of the telescope: the cosmic disturbance has been spotted in a chip shop, a football ground or a suburban front room, and somebody is going to have to fill in the incident report.

There is a magnificently daft difference between the two approaches. Lovecraft's narrators collapse before unspeakable knowledge. A Smith narrator might complain that the revelation was badly organised and demand to know who had authorised the transport. The music makes this inversion possible. Its repetition insists on a stubborn material world even as the words begin to question whether that world can be trusted.

The Future Has Already Been Recorded

The temptation when listening across four decades of The Fall is to construct a tidy history: early aggression, mid-period sophistication, electronic experimentation, late-period austerity. This is a useful filing system and a terrible explanation. The Fall did not develop in a straight line. They doubled back, mislaid their inventions, recovered them in altered form, discovered unexpected uses for old tricks and occasionally produced a glorious mess. An early song can sound more modern than a later one; an apparently conventional cover can be more radical than a track festooned with equipment.

The group's real continuity lies in an attitude to work. They return to the task, rearrange the personnel, argue with the tools and refuse to accept that the job has been finished merely because a record has been released. This is why “Fit and Working Again” makes such an appropriate emblem. It is both a boast and a question. Fit for what? Working for whom? Again after which disaster?

And somewhere in the background the chemist is opening his shutters, the business school is awarding a certificate to itself, a Scottish band is playing a song too quickly to be arrested, and a mysterious mountain has begun emitting an eldritch invoice. The world has not been repaired. The Fall have simply turned up for another shift.
