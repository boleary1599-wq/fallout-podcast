THE TAXI IS LATE AND THE UNIVERSE IS ON FIRE

The Fall, Can and the art of making repetition sound like a nervous breakdown: a small history of bodies, borders, cosmic time and one very important missing cab.

By Dr. M. Hexen, Correspondent for Unnecessary Emergencies

There is an old problem in experimental rock: how do you distinguish a radical rejection of conventional songcraft from a musician simply failing to finish the song? In The Fall's case the question is made worse by the suspicion that Mark E. Smith would regard the distinction as an affront to his civil liberties. An unfinished idea could be a masterpiece; a perfectly executed one might deserve to be thrown down a flight of stairs. Somewhere between the two lies the peculiar artistic economy of a band whose greatest instrument was not the guitar or the drum kit, but the ability to make a repeated phrase seem more alarming on its seventeenth appearance than on its first.

The German group Can offer a particularly useful point of comparison. Not because The Fall were a northern-English version of Can — a claim which collapses under the weight of both groups' considerable differences — but because both understood that repetition could produce change without the comforting business of a chord progression. Can approached the problem with an ensemble of startling rhythmic intelligence: Jaki Liebezeit's drumming, Holger Czukay's bass and tape work, Irmin Schmidt's keyboards, Michael Karoli's guitar, and the radically different voices of Malcolm Mooney and Damo Suzuki. The Fall made an apparently related discovery by considerably less consensual means. Where Can could sound like a single organism, The Fall often sounded like five organisms disputing custody of the same nervous system.

THE MACHINE THAT LEARNS TO SWEAT

The Can story is sometimes told as a tidy line from Stockhausen to the dance floor. Schmidt and Czukay studied with Karlheinz Stockhausen; Liebezeit brought jazz experience; the group then produced Monster Movie, Tago Mago, Ege Bamyasi and Future Days, an astonishing sequence of records which treated rock as a substance to be pulled apart and reassembled. But the records themselves resist the neatness of that account. “Yoo Doo Right” takes the idea of a song and stretches it until the distinction between musical duration and bodily endurance becomes uncertain. “Halleluhwah” is a machine that appears to have acquired appetites. “Vitamin C” has one of the most irresistible drum-and-bass relationships in recorded music, and its sense of forward movement is achieved with remarkably little conventional harmonic reassurance.

The familiar term motorik is helpful up to a point, but too easily turns a whole musical culture into the image of a man driving down an Autobahn. Liebezeit was not simply playing a metronome. His beat could be exact and elastic at once, apparently unchanging yet continually redistributing the listener's attention. Malcolm Mooney's vocal performances added another important ingredient: the repeated word as incantation, complaint, threat and involuntary twitch. Smith did not need to copy Mooney's phrases to recognise what they made possible. A voice could sit inside a groove and still sound as though it was trying to escape.

“Eat Y'self Fitter” is the most grotesquely appropriate British application of that discovery. The rhythm is at once a dance instruction and a disciplinary regime. Its keyboard figures jab at the listener like defective office equipment; the bass refuses to surrender its position; Smith turns self-improvement into a form of self-consumption. In the early eighties, the language of bodily optimisation was already creeping into advertising and popular culture. The title sounds like an instruction from a government pamphlet whose author has become dangerously excited about dietary fibre. Michel Foucault's account of disciplined bodies is relevant, though the song achieves in several minutes what a committee of social theorists might take a semester to explain: the modern citizen is commanded to improve himself until there is nobody left to improve.

Its opponent, “To Nk Roachment”, inhabits a different kind of rhythmic logic. The awkward title is itself a minor act of sabotage, a linguistic obstruction placed at the entrance to the music. Where “Eat Y'self Fitter” is a machine that recruits the body, “To Nk Roachment” suggests a system that has lost track of what its own instructions mean. Neither requires a conventional chorus to produce tension. Both depend upon the listener's increasing awareness that the rhythm has taken possession of the room.

PUBLIC TRANSPORT AND PRIVATE APOCALYPSE

One of Smith's most remarkable gifts was the ability to transform ordinary logistics into existential drama. “Cab It Up!” turns movement through the city into an argument between rhythm, voice and environment. “H.O.W.” is stranger still: a title that seems to promise explanation while supplying a record whose atmosphere is more important than any stable account of events. The comparison is not merely between two songs but between two ideas of travel. One is practical, immediate and bodily; the other seems to move through a landscape of verbal disorientation.

Walter Benjamin's Arcades Project imagines the modern city as a labyrinth of commodities, memories and interrupted journeys. Smith's urbanism is less Parisian and more likely to involve an irritated telephone call, but the underlying insight is surprisingly similar. A journey is never simply a passage from one point to another. It exposes the hidden bureaucracy of everyday existence: schedules, strangers, waiting rooms, promises, prices, the miserable possibility that the driver has gone to the wrong address.

Which brings us, inevitably, to “Where's the Fucking Taxi? Cunt”. A lesser critic might dismiss the piece as a novelty, and a lesser artist might have treated the title as the entire joke. Yet the recording belongs to a long and distinguished history of linguistic reduction. Samuel Beckett spent much of his career removing conventional explanation from human speech until repetition and waiting became the principal dramatic events. In Waiting for Godot, the absent figure never arrives. In The Fall's transport apocalypse, the missing figure is a cab driver, and the metaphysical anguish has been compressed into a series of increasingly urgent obscenities.

This is not to suggest that Smith consciously set out to produce a Beckettian miniature. It is to recognise that an apparently stupid record can produce a surprisingly sophisticated effect. A phrase loses its ordinary informational purpose through repetition and becomes pure emotional pressure. At a certain point the listener no longer asks where the taxi is. The listener has become the taxi's absence.

“Calendar”, a collaboration involving Smith and Badly Drawn Boy, points in the opposite direction: time itself, not transport, becomes the organising difficulty. Calendars are supposed to impose rational order on experience. They divide an otherwise intolerably continuous existence into days, appointments and anniversaries. Henri Bergson distinguished measurable clock time from lived duration; The Fall repeatedly found ways to make the two disagree. A three-minute song could feel interminable. A ten-minute groove could disappear in an instant. A missing taxi could last until the heat death of the universe.

EUROPE, AMERICA AND OTHER IMAGINARY COUNTRIES

The Fall's geography was always unstable. Smith could write about Iceland, America or Manchester without producing anything resembling conventional travel writing. Place names became imaginative triggers, not coordinates. “Portugal” is an especially instructive example: the location promises a national portrait, but the pleasure lies in the way Smith's words, delivery and musical setting refuse the dignity of the travelogue. The song is funny without being merely a joke. Its humour comes from a voice capable of making a foreign country sound like a personal inconvenience.

“Midnight in Aspen”, by contrast, belongs to the haunted, unexpectedly beautiful end of the catalogue. Aspen is a place of American wealth, mountain scenery and celebrity mythology, but Smith's language opens it towards astronomy, memory and the possibility of a landscape possessing its own secret history. The appearance of Orion is particularly telling: the hunter in the night sky turns a familiar constellation into a figure watching over a very specific earthly location. The song's comparatively restrained, jazzy movement gives its images room to settle. It is one of those Fall performances in which Smith's refusal to explain himself produces something closer to tenderness than menace.

The contrast is not between a comic song and a serious one. Both demonstrate the same imaginative procedure. A location is named, then gradually detached from its expected meanings. “Portugal” becomes a peculiar verbal object; Aspen becomes a place whose emotional weather cannot be reduced to the meteorological forecast. W. G. Sebald's landscapes offer a distant analogy: geography as an archive of associations that may never resolve into a coherent narrative. Smith's version is less elegiac, more likely to be interrupted by an insult, and considerably less interested in footnotes.

It is tempting to regard the later Fall as an endless process of recycling. Certainly the band revisited rhythmic patterns, vocal gestures and old obsessions. But this is also true of much of the music that mattered most to Smith: garage rock, rockabilly, dub and Can. The significant question is what the repetition does. In the right hands, a familiar figure becomes newly unstable because the surrounding context has changed. A song about a taxi can suddenly appear to illuminate the entire history of modern alienation. A song about a mountain town can reveal an unsuspected capacity for wonder.

THE GREAT UNFINISHED BUSINESS

The old accusation that The Fall were incapable of conventional musicianship misses the point as thoroughly as the opposite claim that every scrap they recorded was an unrecognised masterpiece. The band could be brutally effective, genuinely inspired, careless, funny, tedious and magnificent — sometimes in consecutive minutes. The value of the experiment lies partly in the risk that it will fail. If every musical decision were guaranteed to work, there would be no experiment, only an increasingly elaborate quality-control procedure.

Can's achievement was to make collective improvisation sound like an alternative physical law. The Fall's was to make it sound like an alternative social order, one in which nobody had been informed of the rules and the man apparently in charge kept changing them. Both groups understood that music can mean something before its words have been deciphered. A rhythm can communicate dread, joy, impatience or the sensation of being caught in an administrative trap. A voice can become an instrument without losing its personality. A phrase can be ridiculous and profound, provided it is delivered with sufficient conviction.

What remains after the comparisons, the votes, the grand theories and the unprintable demands for transportation is a remarkably simple idea. The best Fall records do not merely repeat themselves. They alter the listener's experience of repetition. The bass keeps moving. The drums insist. The singer supplies a fragment of language that may be a threat, a joke, a prophecy or an address written incorrectly on the back of an envelope.

Eventually the music stops. Nothing has been resolved. The body has not become fitter. The calendar has not made time comprehensible. Portugal remains Portugal, although perhaps not quite the same Portugal. Aspen is still there beneath the stars.

And the fucking taxi has still not arrived.
