Truckin’ n Squeakin’ in Torquay

By Dr Buck Ruxton

A tournament of incompatible pleasures: four Fall matchups, competing interpretations, and the unexpected virtues of a vote that goes the wrong way.

A bracket that refuses to behave

By its third instalment, F.A.L.L.O.U.T. has found the basic trick that makes its seemingly ludicrous project work: put two Fall songs in direct competition, then spend long enough talking about them that the result begins to feel both inevitable and completely unjust. The premise is a head-to-head tournament of hundreds of tracks, divided across eras. But the pleasure is not really in producing a champion. It is in watching four listeners discover how little a supposedly straightforward choice can tell us about why they love The Fall.

CB radio versus time itself

The opening match pits “I’m Into C.B.” against “Various Times”, and it immediately exposes the format’s fault line. The former inspires an affectionate account of a mixtape, memories of Wigan and the peculiar way Mark E. Smith’s half-glimpsed narratives can become part of a listener’s own history. The panel dwells on its scuffed, abrasive energy rather than treating the song as a novelty about CB radio. Yet “Various Times” makes a different demand: the discussion turns to its strange overlapping timelines, literary references and the early band’s ability to make an almost skeletal arrangement sound disconcertingly mature. One contributor describes the guitar and Smith’s squealing delivery as a kind of mating ritual. That is the sort of observation this show is good at: funny, faintly grotesque and genuinely attentive to the sound.

The wrong song wins — or does it?

When voting begins, the emotional attachment to “I’m Into C.B.” proves insufficient. The conversation itself has changed minds. “Various Times” goes through, and the host’s mock anguish supplies the first of several reminders that a bracket is a machine for generating regret. These are not objective rankings disguised as banter. The participants argue from memories, recordings, lyrical interpretation, production choices and sheer bodily delight. Their votes are contingent on what they have just heard and on what someone else has made them notice.

Australians, football and the limits of taste

“You Haven’t Found It Yet” faces “Australians in Europe” next. Here the discussion wanders through the song’s lyrical frustrations, the virtues of alternate performances and a brief excursion into Torquay football. One participant hears a stronger, almost Kraftwerk-like quality in a Peel-session version of “Australians in Europe”. The result is not unanimous: the argument becomes briefly, hilariously incredulous when someone chooses “You Haven’t Found It Yet”. The vote for “Australians in Europe” is a reminder that the podcast is as interested in the differences between listeners as it is in similarities between records.

Puritanism, mysticism and a greasy synthesiser

The third pairing, “Sons of Temperance” against “Surmount All Obstacles”, pulls the conversation into one of The Fall’s richest territories: the gap between Smith’s apparent pronouncements and the meanings a listener can plausibly extract from them. The speakers speculate about temperance movements, punk puritanism, mysticism and the name of Damo Suzuki, while also finding room for the music’s snarling textures and a particularly greasy synth passage. Interpretations collide without needing to be settled. A joke about confusing two different Damo Suzukis becomes a neat miniature of the whole enterprise: scholarship and mischief are not competing methods here. They continually interrupt each other.

The outsider looking in

Finally “Cowboy Gregori” takes on “Irish”. The conversation moves from rockabilly and psychobilly associations to late-period Smith, his relationship with the music business and his talent for occupying the position of the permanent outsider. The hosts consider the pleasure of hearing a changed voice that remains recognisably Smith, and the oddity of an artist condemning imitation while borrowing freely himself. “Irish” wins the final vote. The concluding tally is “Various Times”, “Australians in Europe”, “Sons of Temperance” and “Irish”: four survivors that make an oddly persuasive, deeply personal miniature anthology.

Why this episode matters

What distinguishes this instalment is its willingness to leave the apparatus visible. The host forgets where the voting belongs, the panel digresses, the transcript catches false starts and interruptions, and the verdicts sometimes sound like affronts to common sense. That looseness is not an obstacle to criticism. It is the method. The songs are played against one another, but the real drama lies between ways of listening: nostalgia against close reading, groove against lyric, early-period mythology against the awkward vitality of the later records. The bracket keeps score; the conversation makes the case for listening again.
