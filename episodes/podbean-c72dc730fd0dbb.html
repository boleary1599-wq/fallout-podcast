THE ART OF GETTING IT WRONG

Rockabilly, Baywatch, historical fraud and The Fall's war on cultural authenticity

By Dr. M. Hexen, Senior Correspondent for Unlicensed Metaphysics

I. THE WRONG KIND OF ROCK AND ROLL

There is a peculiar indignity in hearing The Fall perform conventional rock and roll. A band capable of making three notes sound like an attempted military coup suddenly behaves like the entertainment at an unsuccessful wedding reception. Rollin' Dany, their excursion into Gene Vincent territory, presents precisely this problem. The clipped guitar figures, brisk momentum and tidy production know which historical costume to wear. The solos are competent, energetic and almost offensively appropriate. Everything functions. Which is the problem. The Fall were rarely most interesting when functioning correctly. Their greatest records resemble machines assembled by somebody who understands engineering but disagrees with its objectives. Compare this cover with the primitive propulsion of New Face in Hell. Both depend on repetition and early rock energy. But one reproduces a familiar vocabulary while the other invents a new language using three broken instruments and a police report. The Cramps understood that rockabilly was already grotesque: sexual panic, cheap horror, exaggerated masculinity and theatrical delinquency. They turned the genre into an EC horror comic with a rhythm section. The Fall's Vincent cover leaves the machinery intact. New Face in Hell rebuilds the elementary components into a paranoid apparatus. One song knows where it comes from. The other appears to have escaped from somewhere not yet discovered.

II. LEE PERRY PRODUCES THE VELVET UNDERGROUND IN A SHED

New Face in Hell is remarkable for how much information its rudimentary arrangement carries. The keyboard figure evokes the Velvet Underground's devotion to repetition, particularly John Cale's ability to turn simple material into psychological pressure. Yet the guitars are more intricate than their abrasive surface suggests. They weave around one another, changing emphasis and generating movement without abandoning the pulse. Later the arrangement approaches dub. Smith's voice rises into peculiar high-register cries while cheap-sounding delay suggests Lee Perry producing the Velvet Underground in a shed outside Prestwich. Somebody has stolen the proper tape machine. The remaining equipment belongs to a school drama department. The result is nevertheless extraordinary. This is more than a joke. Dub and minimalist rock demonstrate how repetition destabilises perception. In conventional pop, the chorus returns and everybody feels reassured. Here musical figures recur until the listener suspects something is wrong with the world they describe. Smith's narrative of accusation and institutional corruption intensifies the sensation. Kafka's Trial offers a parallel, though Smith's bureaucracy is more distinctly British. Kafka's authority seems infinite. Smith's officials appear incompetent, vindictive and employed by the local council. Compare this intricacy with Pearl City, where a guitar vamp and competing instrumental details do not always form a convincing whole. The Fall's greatness was not simply that their parts disagreed. It was that disagreement sometimes created an entirely new order.

III. HANK WILLIAMS AND THE PROBLEM OF SINCERITY

Country music presents a different difficulty. Hank Williams could transform a simple observation into loneliness or spiritual exhaustion. His melodies carried emotion directly; his performances invited identification. Mark E. Smith was not naturally inclined towards either procedure. Just Waiting consequently sounds like an experiment in incompatibility. The country inheritance remains recognisable, but Smith introduces emotional distance. Rather than inhabiting the original sentiment, he seems to inspect it from across the room. The backing is casual; the vocal refuses the conventional gestures of yearning. It can be heard as failed interpretation, but also as a test of what happens when an artist devoted to undermining convention encounters a song built upon direct expression. I'm Frank constructs an unstable atmosphere instead. A repetitive rhythm, synthetic textures and periodic swooshes approximate dance music, although the production seems to have been left overnight in an airing cupboard. The groove has comic physicality. It suggests a dancefloor populated by people ordered to enjoy themselves without being told what enjoyment involves. Smith moves between detached observation and exaggerated sexual suggestion, turning the vocabulary of popular music faintly unpleasant. This is not carefully staged rock eroticism. It resembles a man imitating a seductive entertainer after receiving his instructions from a defective answering machine. The thin production and peculiar snare might be disastrous on a polished pop record. Here they help create a slightly incorrect version of the early nineties. Hank Williams and I'm Frank represent opposing poles: one offers direct feeling; the other makes communication itself a misunderstanding.

IV. ZAPPA'S MISSING INSTRUCTIONS

Frank Zappa occupies an unusual position in rock history: a composer of formidable technical ambition whose humour could be extraordinarily juvenile. He treated a rock band as a miniature orchestra and a studio as a laboratory, then interrupted proceedings with a joke apparently written on the back of a school exercise book. The Early Life of Crying Marshal is a strange, fragmentary piece, its odd instrumental gestures and abrupt presentation suggesting an interruption rather than a developed composition. Synthesised textures and peculiar sounds arrive as though dropped into the album from a different session. It belongs to The Fall's tradition of refusing to distinguish reliably between a finished work and something discovered during recording. Yet its apparent spontaneity differs from Zappa's meticulous constructions. Zappa used the appearance of chaos to conceal preparation. The Fall sometimes used the appearance of preparation to conceal chaos. A fragment might be an inspired miniature, an intentional disruption or something nobody could be bothered to finish. Occasionally all three. Pearl City provides a substantial counterweight. Bass and guitar establish a rock foundation, but the details pull in different directions. Smith introduces another rhythmic argument. There are interesting ingredients without necessarily a persuasive recipe. A song can contain several good ideas and still fail to become one good song. The easy critical response praises all roughness as a challenge to bourgeois values. The harder response notices when an arrangement simply doesn't work. Sometimes an unpolished diamond is a diamond. Sometimes it is gravel confidently labelled by the dealer.

V. THE INVENTION OF THE PRE-MDMA YEARS

Mark E. Smith understood the absurdity of historical classification. Popular culture is divided into periods: pre-punk, post-punk, Madchester, Britpop, wilderness years, comeback years and the inevitable period when musicians insist none of these categories means anything. Music journalism depends upon such divisions. Without them, thousands of retrospective articles would need replacing with descriptions of actual music. Pre-MDMA Years pushes this habit towards absurdity. Its title sounds like an academic classification devised by a sociologist explaining British youth culture through pharmaceutical history. The music is brief, electronic and deliberately insubstantial. Smith talks over squelchy synthetic textures, delivering fragments that parody the language of generational division. Rather than mounting a grand argument about nostalgia, the piece makes nostalgia's vocabulary ridiculous. It treats the calendar as a suspect document. The absurdity lies in assuming human experience can be divided into coherent periods according to whichever phenomenon interests the historian. It resembles describing the English Civil War as occurring during the pre-Baywatch years. The electronics connect it with I'm Frank, though the two approach the same cultural territory from opposite directions. In one, the body becomes ridiculous; in the other, the calendar does. The former turns dance music into uncomfortable physical comedy, while the latter makes rave culture a subject of mock historical scholarship. The piece barely stays long enough to establish itself. Its brevity is essential: it delivers its joke and disappears before anybody can organise a symposium.

VI. BAYWATCH AND THE COLLAPSE OF WESTERN CIVILISATION

The suggestion that Remainderer resembles the Baywatch theme permanently damages the listening experience. Once proposed, the connection is difficult to remove. The television theme represents manufactured optimism: enormous choruses, heroic uplift and the promise that attractive people will always rescue you from the sea. Remainderer inhabits another universe. Rough guitar and bass parts struggle to establish a coherent relationship. Smith's vocal consists of growls, interruptions and grotesque noises. He sounds less like a lifeguard than a malfunctioning Dalek abandoned beside a municipal swimming pool. The comparison is illuminating because it is ridiculous, not because an actual borrowing has been established. Traces of conventional rock momentum remain, but the arrangement undermines its accessibility. The guitars pursue a riff. The drums attempt order. The vocal behaves as though neither has authority. Moments of coherence appear and disappear again. This raises the uncomfortable question of late Fall criticism: when does artistic refusal become an excuse for poor execution? Earlier recordings transformed roughness into urgency and invention. Yet roughness is not automatically interesting. A badly played song is not necessarily a revolutionary critique of respectable musicianship. Sometimes it is just badly played. Remainderer occupies that territory. Its vocal grotesquerie is distinctive; the musical foundation is less persuasive. Imagine a beach rescue performed by people who cannot swim, have never seen the sea and are arguing about the ownership of a synthesiser. The programme would not survive its pilot. The soundtrack might conceivably be released by The Fall.

VII. THE AUTHENTICITY TRAP

Across these eight recordings The Fall repeatedly confront authenticity. Rockabilly demands fidelity to an imagined past. Country music demands sincerity. Experimental rock demands compositional ambition. Dance music demands pleasure. Television pop demands accessibility. The Fall can reproduce elements of all these traditions, but reproduction is rarely where their most interesting work begins. Rollin' Dany demonstrates the limits of competent imitation. Just Waiting exposes the difficulty of inherited sincerity. The Early Life of Crying Marshal questions the boundary between experiment and unfinished material. Pearl City shows that interesting components do not guarantee a coherent machine. Elsewhere, refusal to behave correctly produces something magnificent. New Face in Hell turns primitive rock into a paranoid political nightmare. I'm Frank transforms dance music into uncomfortable physical comedy. Pre-MDMA Years dismantles the language through which popular culture explains itself. And Remainderer demonstrates that artistic freedom does not guarantee artistic success. This distinction matters. The Fall were not great because everything they recorded was great. They were great because they attempted things respectable musicians considered unreasonable, unnecessary or professionally damaging. Their catalogue contains magnificent failures, terrible successes, accidental masterpieces and recordings that seem to have escaped quality control through a hole in the studio wall. Smith understood that authenticity was frequently another performance. The rockabilly singer dresses like 1957. The country singer performs sincerity. The experimental composer performs seriousness. The television entertainer performs happiness. The Fall's greatest achievement was to expose the machinery beneath those performances. Sometimes they dismantled it with extraordinary intelligence. Sometimes they kicked it until it stopped working. Either way, the resulting noise was unmistakably theirs.
