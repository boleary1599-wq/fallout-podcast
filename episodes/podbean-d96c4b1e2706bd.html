BY JAMES TEMPERANCE, MD

THE SQUID, THE RABBIT AND THE METRONOMIC DEATH OF THE UNIVERSE

An excessively learned account of several people attempting to decide whether The Fall were any good, and accidentally reopening the entire problem of modernity.

There is a particular species of English intellectual catastrophe in which five men spend the better part of eighty minutes arguing over a record made in circumstances that might charitably be described as provisional. This is not a criticism. It is a cultural achievement. On the fifteenth instalment of F.A.L.L.O.U.T., Brendan, Phil, Ezra, Al and guest Steve of The Fall in Fives conduct four bouts of musical adjudication, ranging from the band's first public declaration of intent to its later fascination with a rabbit, a serial murderer and a surprisingly competent rhythm section. In the process they arrive, without quite announcing it, at a working theory of The Fall: a group for whom repetition is essential, but repetition done properly is almost invariably suspicious.

The evening begins with a description of a familiar spiritual itinerary. He loved The Fall in the eighties, fell away in the nineties, then returned to find that the group had quietly produced an industrial quantity of music during his absence. His response was to acquire the records and write something in the region of half a million words about them. This is either a scholarly vocation or a very polite nervous breakdown. It also gives the episode a splendidly lopsided dynamic. Steve possesses the terrible authority of someone who has listened to everything; the hosts retain the even more terrible authority of people prepared to disagree with him.

I. REPETITION, OR THE DIALECTIC OF NOT DOING THE SAME THING

“Repetition” is the ideal opening exhibit, not because it supplies a tidy manifesto but because it makes a nuisance of the idea of manifestos. The obvious historical story is that Mark E. Smith, emerging from the wreckage of punk, announces his allegiance to the mechanical pulse of Can and the hypnotic pleasures of the same thing again. But The perversity is immediately apparent: the record is barely as repetitive as its reputation. The drums wander, the arrangement shifts, the thing opens up and changes its mind. It is worth remembering that, for all the posthumous talk of Krautrock and motorik, The Fall were rarely an orthodox motorik outfit. Their music was not a Düsseldorf engineering demonstration. It was closer to a machine constructed in a Salford shed by a man who distrusted both the instructions and the concept of engineering.

Then there is the song's moral instability. Smith's cast of repetitive enthusiasts includes Chairman Mao and Tommy Cooper, a pairing that suggests either a uniquely comprehensive cultural theory or the contents of a particularly upsetting pub quiz. What is repetition here: aesthetic discipline, authoritarian conditioning, compulsive behaviour, the consolation of a working-class routine, or the hideous prospect of doing the same gig forever? The reference to psychiatric treatment and electrodes brings Prestwich Mental Hospital into the conversation, while the song's lurch towards Richard Hell's “Blank Generation” turns a possible statement of punk allegiance into a sneer at punk's rapidly standardising revolt. Even rebellion can become a uniform. Especially rebellion.

The ending risks the ending, hearing something dangerously close to Sham 69; but its humour deserves a defence. The Casiotone chords invite speculation about Baader–Meinhof, beatnik black clothing and the possibility that Smith is mocking his own group as much as everyone else's. These are not competing solutions to a crossword. Their incompatibility is the point. “Repetition” is a manifesto whose central instruction may be to distrust manifestos. Naturally, it wins unanimously.

Its opponent, “Clear Off”, is a far less successful experiment in being modern. It summons OMD, New Order and The Cure; the spectre of Gavin Friday hovers over the proceedings like an especially theatrical invoice. There is something to enjoy in the rhythm, the piano and the three-beat motion. One genuinely sharp musical detail: Steve Hanley's bass, ordinarily given to inventive movement, sits almost stubbornly on the root note while the lighter instrumentation floats overhead. Here, in other words, is the sort of repetition the earlier song was accused of practising. Unfortunately it has been dressed for a fashionable 1984 funeral.

There is Beefheart in the gait and a domestic doorbell in the synthesiser. The lyrics produce an alarming association between immigration, suspicion and Dennis Nilsen. The conversation even reaches Eliot's The Waste Land, the movement from punk through post-punk into shiny New Wave, and the suspicion that the band knew when a B-side was merely a B-side. It is a splendidly disproportionate apparatus of interpretation to bring to a song that several participants find, in the end, a bit wet. The tragedy of “Clear Off” is not that it sounds like the eighties. It is that it sounds as though it knows it does.

II. THE CALL OF CTHULHU, AS ADMINISTERED BY A SOUND ENGINEER

“Squid Lord” makes the record company's deadline sound like a Lovecraftian curse. The Seminal Live studio material is discussed as something hurried, scuzzy and not entirely finished. The scuzzier album recording has advantages over its grimy version to the cleaner Peel session. There is an amplified bass hum that most engineers would attempt to remove, which raises the important question of whether The Fall's production method was an aesthetic programme or a sustained campaign against the mixing desk. The answer, sensibly, is yes.

There is also a Stone Roses detour. A riff from their comeback single “All For One” is played and accused of bearing an uncomfortable resemblance to the squid's riff. The panel then considers the relative merits of the Stone Roses' late-period efforts, which are not treated kindly. The alleged borrowing is a claim made during the discussion rather than a settled historical finding; its importance here is comic and diagnostic. The Fall's allegedly primitive, unfinished noise can turn out to contain a perfectly exportable pop-rock idea. It merely refuses to polish it for resale.

The song's lyric leads Brendan into one of the episode's finest imaginative excursions: Smith summoning Cthulhu to administer supernatural revenge upon a collection of provincial enemies, possibly in puppet-show form. The squatting turnip-jeaned offender, the streak of piss and the unfortunate man destined for a draughty hospital bed become figures in a low-budget cosmic morality play. H. P. Lovecraft meets the north-western insult tradition, with less architectural description and considerably more personal malice. The opening riff is difficult to forgive despite the opening guitar riff, though he adores the thumping section that follows. The group is divided between those who find the rough edges invigorating and those who think the edges have been left rough because nobody could be bothered to finish the job.

“Return”, from Code: Selfish, offers a competing kind of mechanical insistence. There is much to like in its chug and its apparently ridiculous “baby” refrain; An improbable theory emerges that Smith is briefly possessed by Robert Plant, recalling a game in which his old band would shout “Percy” whenever Plant uttered the sacred syllable. Others hear the whole thing an “unedifying churn”, its programmed percussion suggestive of someone pressing several buttons before leaving for the pub. There are traces of B-52's and Devo-like qualities, and There is some charm in the old keyboard sound. This is not a debate about whether repetition is good. It is about the almost impossibly fine distinction between hypnotic economy and being bored off one's fucking arse.

The vote produces a numerical crisis. A show devoted to adjudication suddenly has to invent a points system in public. Fingers are held up, totals announced, and the squid wins by arithmetic. There is something magnificently Fall-like about this: an apparently anarchic enterprise resolving a metaphysical dispute through the administrative technology of a primary-school classroom.

III. THE VOICE AS PUBLIC NUISANCE

“Don't Call Me Darling” brings Brix Smith into the dock, though nobody can agree what she is charged with. The most intriguing feature is the song's most intriguing reversal: in the classic eighties arrangement Brix could provide sweetness against Mark's abrasion; here Mark almost croons while Brix supplies the aggression. Despite the considerable virtues of Brix's contribution to The Fall, this particular performance too grating. There is enjoyment to be found in the garage-rock stomp. The vocals can be defended, although the voices and attacks the backing track, hearing something between Madness, Jools Holland and an ill-advised karaoke accompaniment. The problem is not that the band is insufficiently competent. It is that it sounds competent in an insufficiently interesting way.

The comparison with “Guest Informant” is especially useful. Brix's vocal extremity can be an instrument of tension rather than a novelty attachment; the difference is whether the surrounding music gives that extremity a meaningful surface to attack. The description of staccato indie chords as the last refuge of the scoundrel gets at the issue. The Fall could survive almost any amount of bad taste. What they struggled to survive was ordinary bad taste.

Then “4½ Inch” arrives, and the evening briefly becomes an industrial accident in the most complimentary sense. The track's disputed personnel and fragmentary construction prompt talk of samples, Dave Bush, Julia Nagle and remnants of earlier band performances. Al is delighted by its abrupt changes, funky breaks and booming percussion. Ezra hears something almost industrial, with the house apparently collapsing in sympathy with the words. Phil brings in the Beatles' “Revolution 9”, the Chemical Brothers, Jon Spencer Blues Explosion, The Jesus Lizard and the perennial Can paradox of tightness versus looseness. These are not merely names thrown into a fashionable critical salad. They describe a recognisable collision: loop-based discipline, blues-rock violence, cut-up voices and a mix that behaves like a room full of objects falling down the stairs.

The track invites a magnificent description of the track as a genre massacre: musical styles battered together, a synthesiser wandering through the wreckage, a frightened little keyboard lurking in the corner, Smith's layered voice multiplying like enraged wasps at closing time. The passage is both comic and musically attentive, which is a harder trick than it sounds. The title's ecstatic midges return here as the perfect emblem of the group: microscopic things possessed of an almost supernatural quantity of agitation. The panel is in rare agreement. “4½ Inch” wins, not because it is polished, but because it makes its damage productive.

IV. A RABBIT WALKS INTO A TRUE-CRIME PODCAST

The final pairing is the strangest: “Noyes”, from Re-Mit, against “What About Us?”, from Fall Heads Roll. “Noyes” is discussed as a specimen of Smith's late-period spoken harassment, the frontman needling his musicians while the accompaniment potters on. A theory of performance lurks inside the joke. Smith's antagonism, he suggests, can be understood as an attempt to manufacture energy, a shamanic technique practised by a man whose chosen ritual equipment consisted largely of insults and a microphone. It is a persuasive account of how the apparently throwaway might perform a genuine function. There is much to like in the roast; the absent commentator finds it neither funny nor musically worthwhile. The question becomes whether The Fall's mythology can make a weak backing track interesting, or whether the mythology is precisely what the backing track exists to accommodate.

“What About Us?” seems, at first, the more conventional heavyweight: a sharply played, well-produced late-period Fall song. Phil is impressed by the band's tightness but suspicious of Smith's unusually structured lyric delivery. This can be teasingly translated into a demand for greater professionalism from Mark E. Smith, a proposition roughly equivalent to asking a fox to improve its poultry-management procedures. Yet the lyric is gloriously unreasonable. The discussion follows its rabbit imagery, the East German immigrant perspective and the Harold Shipman reference into territory where literal reading becomes both irresistible and faintly ridiculous. A pet rabbit is implicated; the immigration metaphor is considered; Shipman's failure to offer drugs becomes a matter for musical grievance. The whole thing resembles a police report assembled by Lewis Carroll after several pints.

The late-period question is larger than either song. Brendan suggests that The Fall increasingly became a very competent band, and wonders whether this was precisely what Smith wanted and precisely what some listeners did not. There is also regret for the Reformation Post TLC line-up, whose live reputation was formidable but whose studio legacy remained compromised by circumstances. The conversation keeps circling back to a central tension: technical competence can preserve the music's force, but it can also sand away the unpleasant grain that made the group necessary. Smith wanted musicians capable of playing the material; his listeners sometimes wanted the sensation that the material was only just surviving its musicians.

The closing vote is beautifully confused. Steve favours “Noyes”; Al and Ezra prefer “What About Us?”; Phil opts for “Noyes”; Brendan says he too favours “Noyes”, before the final recap names “What About Us?” as the winner. It is tempting to call this an administrative error. A more generous reading is that the podcast has finally achieved the condition of its subject: the result is recorded, the result is contradicted, and both results remain in circulation.

What, then, is The Fall? Steve's opening answer — a group rather than a band, a fascination that grew into an enormous body of writing — turns out to be more revealing than any attempt at a grand definition. Across these four contests, The Fall are the difference between a root-note bassline that deadens a song and one that makes it levitate; between a badly finished record and a thrillingly unfinished one; between Brix screaming as decoration and Brix screaming as structure; between a rabbit joke and an entire theory of the late twentieth century. Their music does not abolish repetition. It repeatedly stages the possibility that repetition might, on this occasion, do something else.

And if that is not a manifesto, it is at least a perfectly serviceable reason to spend another eighty minutes arguing about it.
