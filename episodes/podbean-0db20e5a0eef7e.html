The Future Is an Old Man in a Pub

By Fiery Jack

On The Fall, modernity, repetition and the strange business of being permanently out of time.

There is something wonderfully perverse about The Fall's relationship with modernity. They were a group that seemed to regard the future with equal measures of suspicion, appetite and contempt. Mark E. Smith could sound like a man complaining about the price of bitter in 1974 while presiding over music that anticipated the next decade's experiments. The Fall embraced electronics without becoming electronic, absorbed dance music without becoming a dance act, and employed successive generations of musicians without ever quite joining their generation. They were modern almost by accident, and old-fashioned almost as a matter of principle.

This is partly because Smith's idea of progress had little to do with improvement. The normal story of a rock group involves development: the rough early recordings give way to technical accomplishment, new equipment opens up new possibilities, and the artist eventually achieves some recognisable maturity. The Fall made nonsense of this. Their early records could sound startlingly sophisticated in their arrangements, while their later records could be astonishingly primitive. Sometimes they appeared to move backwards and forwards simultaneously. They were not pursuing a sound so much as pursuing the conditions under which a sound might become interesting.

“An Older Lover” is a useful place to begin. The title itself suggests an uncomfortable collision between experience and desire, and the music has little interest in smoothing over that discomfort. Its repetition is not simply the repetition of a conventional rock riff. It establishes an environment in which Smith can circle his subject, worrying at it, changing its meaning through insistence. This is one of the peculiar achievements of early Fall music: it makes the apparently limited resources of a guitar group feel inexhaustible. Rather than progressing through a composition, the listener becomes increasingly trapped inside it.

“Putta Block,” by contrast, suggests another way of making musical obstinacy productive. The Fall's capacity to turn a small, ungainly idea into a fully functioning musical proposition was not a failure of imagination. It was an alternative to the assumption that music must continuously offer new information. A repeated phrase can become funnier, nastier or more mysterious simply by refusing to go away. The point is not that repetition automatically produces profundity. It is that The Fall understood repetition as a form of pressure.

The Modern World Has Terrible Taste

By the time of “Bremen Nacht,” The Fall had entered one of their most remarkable periods of technological promiscuity. The late-eighties group could accommodate programmed rhythms, keyboards, dance-floor momentum and abrasive guitar without treating any of these as incompatible. The music's relationship with contemporary club culture was neither straightforward imitation nor ironic dismissal. It was more interesting than either. The Fall seemed to recognise that a dance beat could provide exactly the kind of relentless framework Smith had always wanted, while remaining determined to interfere with its pleasures.

“Bremen Nacht” is a particularly fine example because its propulsion is both seductive and vaguely oppressive. The rhythmic machinery keeps advancing, but the record refuses the emotional release that dance music traditionally promises. It is possible to move to it, certainly, but difficult to imagine surrendering to it completely. Something remains askew. The voice does not ride the music so much as occupy it, like an unwelcome passenger who has decided to provide a running commentary on the journey.

That tension is fundamental to The Fall's treatment of modern sounds. They were never especially interested in demonstrating that they understood a genre. They wanted to find out what happened when the genre was made to accommodate them. The distinction matters. A band attempting to sound contemporary usually tries to conceal the distance between itself and the music it has borrowed. The Fall made that distance audible.

“Zandra” brings the argument into another era. By the time it appeared, the group had accumulated decades of musical history, and Smith's voice carried that history whether he intended it to or not. The late Fall could be extraordinarily direct, sometimes even brutally simple, but simplicity now arrived with a peculiar weight. A short riff or blunt vocal gesture was no longer merely a new musical decision. It echoed countless earlier versions of the same decision, made by different musicians under different circumstances.

This creates an intriguing problem for anyone attempting to judge the band's later work. Should a song be rewarded for discovering something new, or for finding another use for something already discovered? The Fall's longevity makes the distinction almost impossible to maintain. A musical idea that might have seemed crude in 1981 could become strangely moving when revisited decades later. Familiarity was not necessarily evidence of exhaustion. Sometimes it was the subject of the music itself.

The Machinery Keeps Running

The pairing of “Way Round” and “Inevitable” points towards a different kind of modernity: the modernity of things that simply continue. The Fall's songs are full of systems, routines, habits, journeys and recurring irritations. Their music frequently seems less concerned with reaching a destination than with examining the peculiar machinery that keeps people moving.

There is something almost bureaucratic about certain Fall rhythms. They proceed with the indifference of a conveyor belt or an office procedure. Smith's words interrupt, contradict and complicate the process, but the machinery rarely stops to accommodate him. This is one reason the band's repetitive structures are so different from the ecstatic repetition associated with much psychedelic or dance music. The Fall's repetition can be hypnotic, but it is also administrative. Things happen because they keep happening.

The idea becomes more complicated when the music itself begins to sound as though it might fall apart. A Fall performance can contain several competing senses of time: the drummer's pulse, a guitar figure that seems to be pushing ahead, a keyboard that arrives at inconvenient intervals, and Smith delivering words according to a schedule known only to himself. What ought to be a unified arrangement becomes a collection of stubborn individual activities.

Yet the result is rarely chaos in the conventional sense. The remarkable thing is how often these incompatible activities produce a perfectly coherent record. The Fall's musicians were not merely following Smith's eccentricities. They were constructing musical environments capable of surviving them. Their achievement was to make instability repeatable.

That is a far more interesting definition of a band than the familiar notion of four or five musicians expressing a shared vision. The Fall were often at their best when no such agreement appeared to exist. The music worked because its participants did not entirely surrender their differences.

The later recordings make this especially apparent. What might initially sound like a lack of refinement can become, after several listens, a highly distinctive balance of competing elements. An awkward keyboard sound, an excessively persistent bass line or a vocal that seems to have arrived from another room can turn out to be the detail holding the whole thing together. Remove the apparent mistake and the record becomes less interesting.

Hot Cakes and Cold Futures

“Hot Cake” and “Bo Demmick” bring us back to the question of what it means for The Fall to grow old. By this stage, the group had long since ceased to be young, and Smith had become an increasingly extraordinary presence: physically diminished, vocally unpredictable, yet still capable of imposing an unmistakable identity upon almost any arrangement.

There is a temptation to hear the late Fall principally as evidence of decline. Certainly, the records can be uneven, and it would be absurd to pretend that every rough edge conceals a masterpiece. But the language of decline also assumes that the group had once achieved an ideal state from which every subsequent recording represented a departure. The Fall had spent their entire career making that idea difficult to sustain.

“Hot Cake” belongs to a period when the band could still generate a startling amount of energy from apparently uncomplicated materials. Its appeal does not depend on demonstrating some previously unsuspected sophistication. Rather, it asks whether directness itself might remain a form of invention. How much can be done with a groove, an abrasive sound and a voice that refuses to behave? The answer, as ever, depends on the particular combination.

“Bo Demmick” raises the same question from another angle. The late Fall's roughness can feel almost confrontational, as though the record is daring the listener to apply the usual standards of musical accomplishment. This is not an excuse for everything. Some experiments fail; some repetitions merely repeat. But there is a distinction between music that has run out of ideas and music that is actively suspicious of what an idea is supposed to look like.

What makes The Fall's enormous catalogue so rewarding is that it resists the reassuring narrative of artistic progress. Their best records are scattered across incompatible periods and lineups. A song from the beginning can sound more futuristic than one recorded thirty years later; a late recording can reveal possibilities that an earlier, more celebrated lineup never explored. The band's history resembles less a journey towards perfection than a series of arguments about what perfection might mean.

And perhaps that is why The Fall never became quite as historical as other groups of their generation. Their records are unmistakably products of their time, yet they continually evade the tidy categories by which musical history organises itself. Punk, post-punk, rockabilly, electronic music, garage rock, dance music: all are present, but none adequately describes the result.

The Fall did not transcend their influences. They made those influences quarrel.

Modernity, in this sense, is not a matter of keeping up. It is the ability to remain troublesome in changing circumstances. The future keeps arriving, bringing new machines, new musicians, new fashions and new definitions of what music ought to be. The Fall keep responding with the same awkward question: Yes, but what happens if we do it wrong?

And occasionally, gloriously, doing it wrong turns out to be the most modern thing imaginable.
