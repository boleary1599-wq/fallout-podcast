A Quick Trip to the Ice House

On the difference between doing very little and doing virtually nothing.

By Gregor Bloedel

One of the great casualties of the Happy Mondays mythology is the Happy Mondays. There is no shortage of material concerning the pharmaceuticals, the financial disasters and Bez, whose contribution to popular music remains sufficiently unusual that nobody has satisfactorily invented a job title for it. But listen to Mark Day's guitar, Paul Ryder's bass and Gary Whelan's drums without concentrating exclusively on Shaun Ryder. Whatever else these people were getting up to, they understood how to construct music. The absence of conventional guitar distortion is particularly striking. A group supposedly distinguished by its appetite for excess exercised considerable restraint in the recording studio.

The comparison with Can needn't depend upon direct influence. Approaching rhythm through funk, disco and soul might bring a group into much the same territory as approaching it through Stockhausen. The independence of the instruments, the rolling backbeat and Ryder's occasional resemblance to Damo Suzuki suggest alternative routes towards a similar understanding of what a rock group might accomplish. The guitar needn't be the boss. Neither need the vocalist. The Fall understood this, too, although their arrangements sometimes suggest that nobody had been informed who was supposed to be in charge.

Smith's reported objections to excessive tom hits make for an excellent joke when listening to ‘A Figure Walks’. The entire supernatural apparatus of the song depends upon somebody repeatedly hitting a tom. Remove that percussion and Smith's predicament becomes a rather less alarming account of pedestrian activity. Its six minutes pass surprisingly quickly because the drumming establishes the physical sensation of being followed. Contrast this with the cheerful little reggae stomp of ‘Ride Away’, which seems to last twice as long as it actually does. Repetition is sometimes accused of preventing things from happening in music. On ‘A Figure Walks’, repetition is the thing that's happening.

The curious thing about ‘Ride Away’ is that its most irritating feature gradually becomes its most engaging one. The little keyboard figure sounds like somebody demonstrating an instrument in a shop while the band happens to be playing nearby. Eventually, one comes to anticipate it. There is something of Faust's ‘The Sad Skinhead’ in this awkward approximation of reggae, although the prospect of Lee Scratch Perry providing incidental music for Camberwick Green is equally enticing. Its problem isn't the wrongness of the constituent parts but the limited activity between them. The keyboard has personality; the rhythm has a job.

This is where the Happy Mondays comparison becomes particularly useful. Their grooves could absorb fragments of ‘Day Tripper’, nursery rhymes, David Essex and Ryder's Bukowski-like investigations into the less salubrious aspects of human existence without losing their identity. Stephen Malkmus has a similar capacity for introducing borrowed melodic scraps into songs that appear to have no vacancy for them. The pleasure isn't simply that we recognise something. It's that the musicians have found somewhere unexpected for it to live. An old pop melody rubs shoulders with an account of somebody having a thoroughly unpleasant afternoon.

Smith occasionally dispensed with the bother of borrowing individual musical components and acquired the entire building. Craig Leon identified the backing of ‘Mollusc in Tyrol’ as originating in his ballet soundtrack Nommos. Smith appears to have recorded himself inhabiting Leon's composition before returning to inhabit a recording of himself inhabiting it. The percussion phases, the synthesisers spiral and the voice seems to exist simultaneously inside and outside the machinery. Somewhere in all this noise there is an unfortunate Austrian mollusc. It must be one of the few occasions upon which industrial music has concerned itself with the geographical progress of a gastropod.

Whether the construction warrants its duration is another matter. The claustrophobia is impressive, particularly through headphones, but the various elements seem reluctant to interfere with one another in ways that fundamentally alter the music. Dub production suggests that sounds might acquire new characteristics through their disappearance, reappearance or manipulation. Here they frequently acquire additional unpleasantness. Yet the spiralling electronics and increasingly deranged recitation are sufficiently compelling to resist outright dismissal. There is a respectable case for hating every second of it, and an equally respectable case for investigating exactly what is making those seconds so objectionable.

The difference with ‘Hurricane Edward’ isn't that the latter is less irritating. It's that its irritations travel. The little loops, whistle-like fragments and warped guitar textures keep changing the proportions of the recording, until the emergence of a more live-sounding performance alters its character altogether. There is a recognisable musical intelligence at work, albeit one not especially interested in demonstrating itself through the conventional operation of a guitar. The textures recall aspects of U.S. Maple's playing; the rhythmic construction suggests Mutsumi's ‘Let's Get Sick’. These seemingly improbable comparisons are more useful than another application of the term experimental.

There is an equally persuasive comparison between Smith's delivery and King Krule. Both voices seem capable of occupying an arrangement without agreeing to its terms. Think of Clint Eastwood and Toshiro Mifune in Yojimbo, and the extent to which a peculiar manner of carrying oneself can become the basis of an entire aesthetic. It isn't necessary to establish a precise chain of musical influence to hear these affinities. What matters is recognising the transformation of an apparent limitation into a means of expression. The guitar on ‘Hurricane Edward’ does something comparable. It has no particular obligation to reassure anybody that its operator knows the chords.

The figure on the beach in the television adaptation of M. R. James's Whistle and I'll Come to You is terrifying because something about its movement is wrong. There is no need for the musical equivalent of a rubber monster being thrown at the screen. The Birthday Party's ‘Release the Bats’ offers magnificent entertainment through the opposite approach, Nick Cave and the musicians positively revelling in the opportunity to frighten the neighbours. The restraint of ‘A Figure Walks’ creates a different order of disturbance. Its monster has no reason to hurry. Smith and the listener are already trapped inside the rhythm.

James also offers a more convincing literary parallel than H. P. Lovecraft, whose allegedly indescribable creatures tended to receive extensive descriptions. Smith supplies enough physical detail to make his pursuing presence disagreeable, but his great contribution to supernatural fiction is the possibility that he might turn around and belt it. Cosmic horror encounters the practical considerations of somebody being followed through the streets of an English town. The yellow fingernails and the threat of an encounter with something beyond human comprehension are sufficiently disturbing, but there comes a point when a man has had enough.

The details of ‘The Steak Place’ suggest a different kind of sinister environment. The carpeting, aluminium handles and peculiar candelabra might easily belong to an establishment in The Sopranos. Smith's restaurant invites speculation about the activities taking place behind its outward respectability. Is it a front for something considerably nastier than the preparation of meat, or merely somewhere that hasn't been redecorated since 1974? The Gene Vincent-like strumming and finger clicks are entirely appropriate to the surroundings. The trouble is that the musicians have apparently mistaken the provision of suitable background music for the provision of an interesting Fall song.

It is possible to defend that blandness as a deliberate aesthetic decision. But compare the guitar on ‘Room to Live’, which wanders in and out of country and western music while the rhythm section prevents the whole thing from capsizing. It is much less conventionally accomplished than the guitar on ‘The Steak Place’, yet a great deal more happens within its limited vocabulary. There is something of Country Teasers here, though not necessarily the familiar riff-based Fall influence discernible in their work. The pleasure is in hearing country music subjected to circumstances its practitioners might regard as distinctly unhelpful.

The reported circumstances of the Room to Live sessions, with musicians left uncertain about who would be playing together, offer an explanation for some of its unsteadiness. Whether the pitch fluctuations originate in the performance or the treatment of the tape remains another question. The distinction between its raggedness and that of Dragnet is nevertheless audible. On the earlier album, the recording equipment sometimes sounds as though it's struggling to accommodate the band; on Room to Live, the band sounds as though it's struggling to accommodate itself. The accidental joy that emerges in the closing moments is all the more rewarding for not having been signposted.

One shouldn't become so preoccupied with these pleasures that the straightforward appeal of ‘Hey! Student’ becomes inadmissible. There is a perfectly legitimate enjoyment to be had from its rattling garage-rock propulsion. It gets people dancing. ‘Mollusc in Tyrol’ is more likely to get people moving in the direction of the nearest available exit. But there is something disappointing about the combination of generic boogie, punkish aggression and dated production on ‘Hey! Student’. Smith's reported hostility towards the intellectual intervention in rock represented by Soft Machine is amusing in this context. The assumption that Hatfield and the North and The Fall cannot peacefully inhabit the same record collection seems considerably less adventurous than either group's music.

Good production, on the other hand, isn't necessarily the enemy of trouble. The identity of Happy Mondays proved sufficiently robust to survive substantial changes in their recorded sound. Martin Hannett's production on Bummed provides an immediately recognisable environment, but it doesn't explain why a particular guitar part works against a particular bass line. Nor can the later commercial presentation of the group automatically reproduce their earlier swagger. The quality that makes a band distinctive resides partly in arrangements, partly in performances and partly in a collection of habits that cannot be bought from a producer.

This is what makes ‘The Early Days of Channel Führer’ such a curious disappointment. Ben Pritchard's picked guitar is lovely. Smith invests the performance with a degree of tenderness, and the reverb gives it an atmosphere that a more aggressively recorded version would probably destroy. Yet one can very easily drift off. There is something of Will Oldham in the arrangement, or the closing minutes of a British romantic film in which a woman has departed for Edinburgh and a man considers his predicament at a bus station. It isn't bad music. The uncomfortable possibility is that its tastefulness has become more interesting than its identity.

‘Bill Is Dead’ is sufficient evidence that The Fall could achieve genuine pathos without becoming anonymous. Conversely, the daft keyboard on ‘Ride Away’ supplies an identity that its conventionally functional rhythm cannot. There is more to investigate in the failures of ‘Mollusc in Tyrol’ than there is in many perfectly competent pieces of popular music. Smith could almost certainly make the racing form sound interesting. This doesn't mean the racing form deserves a songwriting credit, but it does suggest that judging his accompaniments exclusively by their attractiveness is a fairly useless pursuit.

The Fall's habit of producing failures more intriguing than their successes makes the distinction between good and bad musicianship particularly unreliable. It also makes listening to their records a much more rewarding activity than compiling a list of the occasions upon which they got everything right. The Happy Mondays at their best, the stumbling country music of ‘Room to Live’ and the dismantled guitar playing of ‘Hurricane Edward’ are all reminders that unconventional music needn't be made by people incapable of doing anything conventional. What matters is what happens between the instruments, even when they appear to be doing almost nothing. And sometimes, as on ‘A Figure Walks’, the most frightening thing a musician can do is refuse to do anything different.
