NOBODY OWNS THE BO DIDDLEY BEAT

The Fall, musical theft, the pleasures of repetition and the peculiar art of making somebody else's music sound like a threat.

By Professor B. Diddlesworth, Department of Comparative Rhythmic Misappropriation

There is something wonderfully futile about attempting to establish ownership of a rhythm. You can copyright a composition, register a trademark, purchase a recording and employ a solicitor to pursue somebody who has reproduced four seconds of your bass line. But a beat is a more troublesome proposition. It travels between musicians, changes instruments, crosses oceans and eventually becomes so familiar that nobody remembers where they first heard it. Bo Diddley understood this better than most. His famous rhythm, with its lopsided accents and insistent forward motion, became one of rock and roll's fundamental building blocks. It was also an exceptionally convenient piece of musical equipment for anybody who wanted to start a band without having to invent very much. The Fall were particularly enthusiastic customers, although they approached the business with the proprietary indignation of a man discovering that somebody else had been using his wheelbarrow.

The distinction between borrowing and stealing becomes almost meaningless in music built upon repetition. The Bo Diddley beat itself belongs to a much older rhythmic history, commonly associated with the Afro-Cuban clave and related patterns. Its familiar three-two arrangement produces an appealing contradiction: a rhythm that feels both perfectly settled and perpetually on the verge of stumbling. Diddley's genius was not inventing rhythmic asymmetry but making it an immediately recognisable signature of amplified American rock and roll. What happened afterwards was inevitable. The Rolling Stones, the Yardbirds, the New York Dolls and innumerable garage bands helped turn a distinctive musical gesture into a common language. By the time Mark E. Smith got hold of it, the rhythm had accumulated enough previous owners to resemble a stolen bicycle being passed around the tenants of a particularly disreputable block of flats.

“Oh! Brother” is an unusually instructive example because it demonstrates how little The Fall needed to change to make familiar material sound fundamentally peculiar. Released as a single in 1984, it presents a remarkably concise piece of pop construction. Stephen Hanley's bass figure occupies the centre of the recording, its high-register insistence giving the impression that several instruments are simultaneously attempting to play the same three notes. The guitars and keyboards crowd around it rather than establishing an orderly accompaniment. Brix Smith's backing vocals provide a deceptively sweet melodic counterweight, while Mark E. Smith moves between conspiratorial whispering, mockery and an extraordinary high-pitched delivery that sounds like somebody impersonating an unpleasant relative at a family gathering. It is a pop record whose various components appear to have been assembled by people with slightly incompatible ideas about what pop music involves.

The record's apparent arrival from nowhere was itself something of a deception. Smith told Melody Maker in 1984 that the group had been playing an earlier version of “Oh! Brother” in 1978, before its eventual release, when it was more obviously indebted to Bo Diddley. The supposedly sudden transformation of The Fall into a group capable of making accessible singles was therefore less straightforward than it appeared. Brix's arrival undoubtedly changed their melodic vocabulary and stage presence, but the material was not simply an American pop sensibility imposed upon an otherwise impenetrable Manchester racket. The possibility of pop had been there all along, lurking beneath the guitar noise like an exceptionally well-preserved biscuit under a sofa. What changed was the band's willingness to present that possibility without immediately setting fire to it.

Even here, however, the apparent sweetness contains something disagreeable. The opening vocal draws upon a German text associated with Bogislav von Selchow, a writer with a deeply troubling political history. This is not a detail that can be dismissed as another charming instance of Smith's eccentric collecting habits. His attraction to provocative and disreputable source material was real, and its appearance in a bright, almost cheerful pop single creates an uncomfortable collision. The recording does not offer an explanation of why the material has been included. Nor does its theatrical delivery automatically absolve the choice. Smith's work repeatedly depends upon the distance between a performer and the character he appears to inhabit, but that distance can be difficult to measure. The pleasure of “Oh! Brother” is partly the sensation of listening to a record whose surface is inviting while its interior remains distinctly untrustworthy.

That quality becomes more revealing when the song is placed beside “Hey! Marc Riley”, an outtake from the This Nation's Saving Grace period. Both recordings rely upon recognisable rhythmic ingredients, but they use them for markedly different purposes. Where “Oh! Brother” compresses its ideas into a polished, infectious single, “Hey! Marc Riley” sounds like a grievance that has discovered a serviceable groove and decided to stay for the afternoon. Its subject is Smith's former bandmate, who had departed The Fall and pursued his own musical career. The history of their antagonism supplies a ready-made narrative, although the recording is more interesting as music than as another exhibit in the extensive prosecution case of Mark E. Smith versus everybody he had ever employed.

The groove begins relatively lightly, with a sense that the musicians are deliberately withholding their full weight. The cowbell and backing vocals lend it an almost cheerful informality, while the repeated vocal gestures give the impression of a private joke being conducted in public. Eventually the arrangement acquires greater force, demonstrating how a minimal rhythmic figure can change character without substantially changing its notes. It is an old garage-band trick: maintain the pattern, alter the pressure. Yet there is something particularly Fall-like about the way the song seems unwilling to develop into a conventional climax. The groove is enjoyable, the insults are petty, and the whole enterprise feels as though it might continue until somebody agrees to apologise. Nobody does.

The personal antagonism also reveals a wider principle of Smith's songwriting. He was rarely satisfied merely to represent an enemy. He wanted to turn that enemy into musical material. Former colleagues, journalists, promoters and imagined adversaries became names to be repeated, distorted, mispronounced or shouted over a rhythm. The result is closer to playground ritual than conventional autobiography. An insult gains power through repetition rather than through its original cleverness. Football crowds have understood this for generations, frequently producing an atmosphere of enormous collective conviction from a tune that would struggle to qualify as a nursery rhyme. The Fall's relationship with the Bo Diddley beat belongs to the same tradition. Once a pattern becomes sufficiently familiar, almost anything can be hung upon it, including a grudge that should probably have been settled in a pub.

The curious thing is that The Fall could achieve the same effect without any recognisable musical borrowing at all. “Terry Waite Sez”, from Bend Sinister, takes the name of a prominent public figure and places it inside a recording whose relationship to ordinary explanation is practically nonexistent. Terry Waite was already a familiar figure in British news coverage before his kidnapping in Beirut in January 1987. The song's appearance in 1986 is therefore an especially unfortunate coincidence, one that retrospectively gives its peculiar lyrical fragments an atmosphere of accidental prophecy. There is a temptation to treat Smith as a supernatural intelligence, a man capable of detecting future events through the medium of cheap lager and unreliable recording equipment. The more interesting explanation is that he possessed an unusually sharp instinct for the latent absurdity of contemporary public language.

Musically, “Terry Waite Sez” is far more approachable than its subject might suggest. Its directness, buoyant rhythmic propulsion and almost celebratory vocal energy make it a distant relative of “Oh! Brother”. But the words refuse to behave like conventional pop lyrics. Public figures become fragments of language, disconnected from the circumstances that originally made them famous. The song offers the kind of information that might emerge from a badly tuned radio in a waiting room, where a serious news bulletin has become entangled with advertisements and somebody else's conversation. Smith understood that the media were already producing an endless supply of accidental surrealism. His contribution was to remove the explanatory framework and leave the strange bits standing.

The alternative approach is represented by “CD Win Fall 2088”, whose title alone suggests a product manufactured by a company that has accidentally translated its own instructions into English. The recording's collage-like construction and conspicuous chanting produce a very different relationship between repetition and meaning. Rather than establishing a groove and allowing the words to disturb it, the music seems to collect fragments of previous Fall material and reorganise them into something closer to an experimental performance piece. Its relationship with dance and theatrical presentation is important. Repetition becomes a means of controlling bodies and space, not simply keeping a song moving. Yet the result is less persuasive when its separate elements announce themselves too insistently. The Fall were often at their most effective when their experiments sounded accidental, even when they were carefully arranged. Here the experiment can occasionally be heard explaining itself.

This tension between spontaneity and construction becomes particularly interesting in the group's later recordings. “F-'Oldin' Money”, their version of Tommy Blake's rockabilly number, demonstrates how completely Smith could inhabit another musician's material without making any obvious effort to disguise the source. The original belongs to the unruly world of late-fifties American rock and roll, where songs frequently appear to have been invented in the interval between somebody plugging in an amplifier and somebody else deciding the recording session was over. The Fall's version retains that essential economy but gives it a harder, more abrasive physical presence. The guitar and rhythm section operate with almost indecent confidence, while Smith's vocal is both inside the music and somehow contemptuous of it. The record does not sound like a tribute. It sounds like a repossession.

The recording also raises an awkward question about the value of authenticity. Rockabilly revivalism frequently involves painstaking attention to period details: the correct guitar, the appropriate echo, the right trousers and a haircut that requires a small engineering team. Smith was interested in none of this as an end in itself. He wanted the raw musical mechanism, not the museum display surrounding it. The Fall's best covers operate through precisely this kind of selective vandalism. They identify a useful structure, remove anything considered unnecessary and introduce enough instability to make the result seem newly dangerous. A purist might object that the original's atmosphere has been compromised. But the very act of treating a song as untouchable is arguably more offensive to the spirit of early rock and roll than anything The Fall could do to it.

“Distilled Mug Art” provides a still more radical demonstration of what happens when a musician decides that conventional development is an unnecessary luxury. The track is built from an acoustic, almost folk-like instrumental texture, a persistent drone and vocal passages that seem to drift between incantation and muttered conversation. Sounds shift across the stereo field, producing the impression of a small ensemble performing in different corners of a room whose dimensions cannot quite be established. There is an irregularity to the arrangement that becomes part of its attraction. At one point the music appears to hesitate or partially collapse before recovering its momentum. In a conventional recording this might have been treated as an error requiring correction. Here it gives the performance a peculiar human weight.

The song's title is equally characteristic. It suggests a category of cultural production so obscure that even its practitioners would struggle to define it, yet also evokes the thoroughly ordinary business of having an image printed onto a mug. Smith's ability to invest mundane objects with disproportionate significance was one of his most distinctive literary gifts. He could turn a domestic appliance, a provincial official or a badly designed commercial product into the centre of an entire imaginary civilisation. “Distilled Mug Art” extends that method into musical form. Its phrases accumulate significance through recurrence rather than explanation, while the instrumental drone makes individual words seem simultaneously important and meaningless. There is something of the folk ritual about it, but also something of a conversation overheard through the wall of a particularly eccentric bed-and-breakfast.

This is where the familiar assumption that rock music requires chord changes begins to look rather provincial. Much traditional music around the world depends upon sustained tonal centres, repeated rhythmic cells and gradual changes in texture. The interest lies in what happens within the pattern rather than in the distance travelled away from it. The Fall's relationship with these principles was hardly scholarly. Smith was not conducting an ethnomusicological investigation with a notebook and a respectful expression. Nevertheless, his music repeatedly arrived at similar discoveries through an entirely practical process: find something that works, keep doing it and see what happens when somebody interferes. “Distilled Mug Art” is among the clearest demonstrations of how productive that method could become. It does not need to go anywhere because its entire world is already contained within the groove.

The same principle produces unexpectedly elaborate results in “(We Wish You) A Protein Christmas”. Christmas music is an industry devoted to repetition, from familiar melodies and annual radio playlists to the ritual obligation of hearing the same sentimental declarations while attempting to purchase batteries. The Fall approached the season with a mixture of theatrical grandeur and deep suspicion. Their 2003 single, which reworks material associated with “Protein Protection” from The Real New Fall LP, surrounds Smith's fragmented seasonal observations with choral backing vocals, distorted guitars and an arrangement that repeatedly changes direction. It is both recognisably festive and almost aggressively unsuitable for conventional festivities. The choir suggests communal celebration, but the words belong to a world of family discomfort, religious imagery and vaguely threatening social obligations.

The extraordinary thing is how convincing the performance becomes. Its shifts between rock-band attack and quasi-operatic spectacle could easily have produced an embarrassing novelty record. Instead, the contrast gives the music a sense of scale that The Fall rarely pursued so openly. The theatricality is genuine, but so is the unease beneath it. There is something of the old British Christmas ghost story in the arrangement, with its mixture of domestic familiarity and supernatural disturbance. One can imagine it accompanying a BBC adaptation of an M. R. James story, provided the production had exhausted its budget and been forced to employ a choir from a neighbouring pub. Where ordinary Christmas records promise the comforting return of familiar pleasures, this one seems to suggest that the past has returned because somebody neglected to lock the door.

“Is This New”, from Imperial Wax Solvent, approaches repetition from almost the opposite direction. Its stop-start guitar riff is tightly organised, the rhythm section forceful and the interruptions placed with considerable precision. Smith's vocal moves across the arrangement rather than obediently following its accents, sometimes appearing to operate at half the speed of the musicians behind him. The effect recalls the tension between spoken language and rhythmic accompaniment in certain forms of blues, although the band's abrasive attack also brings to mind the Jon Spencer Blues Explosion. Elena Poulou's keyboard sound adds a wonderfully incongruous electronic colour, something between an inexpensive science-fiction effect and the theme music to a forgotten seventies police programme.

Yet the recording's efficiency presents its own difficulty. The late Fall could be exceptionally tight, and there are moments when that competence threatens to remove the instability that once made their music so unpredictable. “Is This New” is a fine demonstration of the group's physical power, but its carefully controlled production leaves relatively little room for the kind of accidental discovery that animates “Distilled Mug Art”. This is not simply a matter of preferring primitive recordings to sophisticated ones. “Oh! Brother” is meticulously assembled, and its arrangement is all the stranger for that care. The essential distinction is between music that uses precision to create uncertainty and music that uses precision to eliminate it. The Fall were capable of both, sometimes within the same recording.

Which brings us back to Bo Diddley and the apparently unanswerable question of who owns a beat. Ownership turns out to be the least interesting thing about it. What matters is what musicians can make the rhythm do after they have acquired it. The Fall's repertoire contains borrowed songs, recycled riffs, private jokes, political fragments, folk-like drones, theatrical Christmas productions and grooves that appear to have survived several unsuccessful attempts to bring them to a conclusion. The common feature is not originality in the conventional sense, but a refusal to accept that familiar material has exhausted its possibilities. Smith could make an old rhythm sound like a personal insult, a rockabilly song resemble a police interrogation or a Christmas carol suggest the collapse of Western civilisation. Bo Diddley might reasonably have asked for his beat back. By then, however, The Fall had already lent it to somebody else.
