THE HORROR OF ORDINARY THINGS

H. P. Lovecraft, municipal dread, insects, lost laptops and the peculiar cosmology of The Fall

By Professor D. Temperance, Correspondent for Municipal Hauntings

I. THE THING ABOVE THE BED

H. P. Lovecraft imagined a universe in which humanity was neither important nor particularly welcome. His monsters were not conventional villains. They were vast, ancient, incomprehensible entities whose existence rendered human civilisation an embarrassing administrative oversight. Mark E. Smith approached the same conclusion from the opposite direction. Where Lovecraft required forbidden manuscripts, impossible geometries and beings from beyond the stars, Smith generally needed a hotel, an insect, a television programme or somebody behaving suspiciously in a pub. The difference is one of scale rather than philosophy. Both specialised in the sensation that ordinary reality was concealing something profoundly unpleasant. Both distrusted reassuring explanations, populated their work with unreliable witnesses and suggested that some terrible process was already underway. Lovecraft's characters discover that the universe operates according to principles they cannot understand. Smith's characters discover much the same thing about Greater Manchester. Yet Lovecraft's cosmic horror is founded upon human insignificance; Smith's often emerges from humanity's extraordinary capacity for pettiness and institutional malice. The universe might be indifferent, but the man behind the counter certainly isn't. This distinction runs through eight apparently unrelated recordings. Their subjects range from insects to laptops, their sounds from delicate atmospheric textures to garage-rock intimidation. Together they offer a cosmology in which the ordinary object is never quite innocent.

II. THE HAUNTED HOTEL AND THE MEDIOCRE UNIVERSE

Hotel Bloedel belongs to the strange territory of Perverted by Language, where The Fall's abrasive earlier vocabulary accommodates new textures and uneasy recording possibilities. Its restrained arrangement is revealing. Instead of attacking through relentless rhythmic force, it allows unease to accumulate. The production can sound murky, even unsatisfactory, but that lack of clarity becomes difficult to separate from the song's atmosphere. Brix Smith's contribution introduces another dimension: a melodic and textural sensibility that sometimes seems to be establishing a different band within the existing one. The result is not entirely comfortable. Comfort would be a peculiar ambition for a threatening hotel. There is something of the British ghost story here. M. R. James understood that disturbing supernatural experiences happen in recognisable surroundings: a room, a manuscript, a stretch of countryside, an object best left alone. The horror arises because the familiar setting remains intact. Middle Mass, by contrast, is physical. Guitar, bass and keyboard establish a compelling rhythmic structure while retaining their abrasive independence. The music seems to argue with itself. The contrast demonstrates the flexibility of the early Fall aesthetic: one recording approaches unease through atmosphere, the other generates it through friction. Neither accepts that a rock song's components should flatter one another. Even Middle Mass suggests an uncomfortable state of existence: neither exceptional nor negligible, merely trapped in the undistinguished centre of things. Mediocrity becomes a metaphysical condition. Lovecraft imagined humanity dwarfed by incomprehensible forces. The Fall offer the equally unsettling possibility that the universe is simply badly organised.

III. BRITISH PEOPLE IN HOT WEATHER

There is no more reliable method of exposing the fragility of British civilisation than increasing the temperature by approximately six degrees. The national response involves public drinking, inappropriate clothing, sudden outbreaks of optimism and the collapse of behavioural standards. British People in Hot Weather understands this with anthropological precision. Its bright, almost absurdly accessible arrangement creates an unusual setting for Smith's observations. Synthesiser colours and a lively rhythmic structure suggest a group briefly entertaining the possibility of becoming a conventional pop act. Naturally, the lyrics concern people behaving like complete idiots. The combination is magnificent. The synthesiser brass sounds triumphantly inappropriate, as though a provincial leisure centre has commissioned a theme tune for the apocalypse. Smith was frequently called a misanthrope, but the label misses the imaginative pleasure he took in human absurdity. His songs do not simply express hatred of ordinary people. They transform ordinary behaviour into grotesque spectacle. The result sometimes resembles British comic strips more than protest music. Imagine a Leo Baxendale character discovering the recreational possibilities of a heatwave. Within three panels the swimming pool would be destroyed, somebody's trousers would be on fire and the mayor would be trapped inside a giant inflatable banana. The song operates according to similar escalating indignity. Its music is energetic and inviting; the world it describes is ridiculous. The listener, rather inconveniently, belongs to that world. Its cheerfulness is not an escape from Smith's nightmare. It is the nightmare's holiday soundtrack.

IV. LADYBIRDS, APHIDS AND THE NATURAL ORDER

Insects occupy an unusually significant position in The Fall's imaginative universe. They are small, persistent, difficult to control and capable of producing considerable psychological disturbance. They also possess an organisational sophistication that puts most human institutions to shame. Ladybird (Green Grass) introduces a vulnerable emotional register. Its melodic qualities and approachable arrangement create a space in which Smith's words acquire a curious tenderness, though the natural imagery is not reassuring. The insect becomes a figure through which innocence, vulnerability and destruction can be explored without conventional sentimentality. William Blake offers a useful comparison. His poetry transforms small natural phenomena into evidence of immense spiritual or political forces. A flower or insect may contain an entire system of meaning. Smith's methods are less devotional, but he shares Blake's instinct that the insignificant can become a site of extraordinary pressure. The Aphid approaches insect life from a more aggressively peculiar direction. Its repetitive vocal phrases and rough rhythmic construction resemble an obscure ritual conducted by people who misunderstood the instructions. The insect becomes an organising principle for a miniature world of strange commands. One recording discovers emotional complexity in a delicate subject; the other treats insect life as an excuse for rhythmic intimidation. Both extract an imaginative environment from material another songwriter might dismiss as trivial. Lovecraft's creatures dissolve distinctions between human, animal, plant and something unclassifiable. Smith's world is similarly porous. People become insects; insects become political symbols; objects acquire personalities. The natural order seems rearranged by an unsupervised committee. Darwin would have requested further evidence. Smith would probably have told him to get stuffed.

V. PINE LEAVES AND THE BEAUTY OF THE UNEXPECTED

Pine Leaves challenges the assumption that The Fall's music must always sound abrasive. Its textures are unexpectedly delicate. The arrangement moves through contrasting ideas, including unusual instrumental colours and horn-like sounds that might initially seem incompatible with the surrounding material. Yet those contradictions generate an affecting atmosphere. The song possesses a peculiar spaciousness, allowing details to register without demanding that every element contribute to a conventional rock climax. This is an essential part of The Fall's achievement. Their music was not merely a prolonged exercise in aggression. It could be atmospheric, beautiful, even strangely pastoral. But its beauty rarely arrived through the expected procedures. Rather than smoothing away contradictions, the group allowed them to remain audible. The result resembles a landscape painting in which the artist has included several objects that should not exist. At first the scene appears tranquil. Then the viewer notices something moving behind the trees. The recurring imagery of conflict and destruction adds another dimension. Nature is not necessarily a refuge from human violence; it may be the place where that violence leaves its most enduring traces. There is an echo of the English pastoral tradition, but without its reassuring assumptions. Nature is not innocent. It simply has a longer memory. Pine Leaves does not resolve its musical contradictions. It inhabits them, and discovers a beauty that conventional polish would probably have destroyed.

VI. OPEN THE BOX, LOSE THE LAPTOP

By the time The Fall reached Open the Boxoctosis and Laptop Dog, their music had developed a distinctive relationship with repetition. A phrase could be repeated until it ceased functioning as ordinary language. An instrumental figure could continue long after a conventional songwriter would have introduced a chorus, bridge or merciful conclusion. The effect could be hypnotic, infuriating or magnificent, sometimes within the same recording. Open the Boxoctosis draws on garage rock, using insistent rhythms and repeated vocal gestures to establish increasingly unreasonable insistence. The demand to open the box begins to resemble an instruction whose purpose has been forgotten. There is a Beckettian quality to this procedure. In Waiting for Godot, repetition becomes evidence of existence trapped within patterns that no longer offer progress. In The Fall, repetition can resemble a malfunctioning institutional announcement. The instruction continues. Nobody knows why. The box remains closed. Laptop Dog relocates this absurdity to technology. A lost computer becomes an existential catastrophe, as though an entire identity has been left on a train. Its abrasive arrangement and forceful drumming threaten to overwhelm the other elements. This creates an interesting contradiction: the words attack technological dependency while the music behaves like an intrusive machine. The rhythm keeps hammering, demanding attention and refusing the listener relief. The song resembles being trapped in a system that insists it exists for your convenience. Every notification is urgent. Every password must be changed. Every update will improve your experience. Somewhere in the background Smith explains that you deserve everything coming to you.

VII. THE COSMIC HORROR OF A LOST AFTERNOON

The connection between Lovecraft and The Fall extends beyond shared imagery. Both understood the artistic possibilities of uncertainty. Lovecraft's finest stories rarely reveal their monsters completely. Their power depends upon the suggestion that the visible world represents a tiny fraction of a larger, threatening reality. Smith's lyrics refuse explanation in comparable ways. Names appear without introductions. Events arrive without adequate context. Accusations are made against people whose identities remain uncertain. The listener receives fragments and must construct a world around them. But where Lovecraft's universe is dominated by ancient cosmic forces, The Fall's is populated by ordinary things that acquire extraordinary menace. A hotel becomes a site of dread. A heatwave exposes civilisation's fragility. An insect acquires metaphysical significance. A laptop becomes an object of pathological devotion. A box must be opened, although nobody can remember what is inside. The arrangements reinforce these transformations. Repetition destabilises familiar structures; rough production obscures instrumental boundaries; unexpected melodic passages appear amid abrasive textures. Not every experiment succeeds. Not every repetition becomes hypnotic. Not every unusual sound deserves preservation. Yet the willingness to allow awkwardness and uncertainty into the finished recording is fundamental to The Fall's greatness. Smith understood that reality was already sufficiently strange. The artist's task was not necessarily to invent an alternative universe, but to listen to the existing one until its reassuring explanations collapsed. Lovecraft gazed into the cosmic abyss and discovered humanity meant nothing. Smith looked around a British town centre on a hot Saturday afternoon and reached a broadly similar conclusion. The difference is that Smith probably got a better song out of it.
