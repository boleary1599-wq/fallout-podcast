The Conventional Is Now Experimental

The Monks, creative vandalism and the danger of The Fall becoming a tribute to themselves.

By Neville Counterpane

The tyranny of the beat

There is something almost offensively modern about Black Monk Time. Recorded in 1965 and released the following year, the Monks' solitary studio album sounds less like an early manifestation of garage rock than an attempt to dismantle rock music before its conventions had been properly established. The Beatles were expanding the vocabulary of popular music; the Monks were investigating how much of that vocabulary could be thrown away. The banjo, an instrument burdened with associations of communal entertainment, becomes a particularly vicious percussion device. The organ provides rhythmic punctuation rather than harmonic reassurance. Even the voices are subjected to the tyranny of the beat, syllables repeated until they cease to function as language.

The Monks' importance to The Fall extends considerably beyond the latter's enthusiasm for covering their songs. Listen to the accelerating desperation of ‘Higgle-Dy Piggle-Dy’ and then consider how frequently The Fall generated excitement through the apparent inability of their music to escape itself. The conventional rock arrangement promises development through chord changes, instrumental breaks and melodic resolution. The Monks discovered that development could occur within repetition itself. Their extraordinary visual commitment — the tonsures, black clothes and nooses — established an uncomfortable relationship between musical identity and institutional discipline. Here were former American servicemen performing an aggressively regimented form of music while questioning the society that had regimented them.

The Peel session recording of ‘New Puritan’ demonstrates how thoroughly The Fall absorbed this lesson without simply reproducing the Monks' sound. The comparison with ESG is instructive. Steve Hanley's bass establishes a physical centre around which the guitars perform a series of angular manoeuvres. Paul Hanley's drumming combines insistence with responsiveness, maintaining the rhythmic architecture while accommodating the little deviations that continually threaten to destabilise it. There is an almost architectural pleasure in hearing the guitar figures unspool, separate and return to recognisable positions. The musicians sound as though they have established a set of rules without entirely determining what will happen within them.

Twenty-seven years later, ‘Reformation!’ would deploy superficially similar resources with rather different results. Its relentless bass, blunt drumming and dirty guitar figures establish a recognisable Fall environment, but the sense of discovery is considerably diminished. The difference is not simply one of musicianship or the relative vitality of two incarnations of the group. On ‘New Puritan’, repetition creates opportunities for musical events; on ‘Reformation!’, the repetition is frequently the event itself. The Monks had discovered a means of making rock music develop without conventional development. The Fall's subsequent history would occasionally demonstrate how easily that discovery could be reduced to an arrangement technique.

The revolutionary potential of sweetness

The central proposition of ‘New Puritan’ concerns the tendency of artistic innovation to become another form of orthodoxy. Smith's denunciations of cultural complacency and record-collecting obsession are delivered with the conviction of somebody who has discovered a new religion and is already disappointed by its congregation. Yet the logic of the song suggests that its own musical language must eventually become suspect. Once the angular guitar figures, oppressive bass and hectoring vocals have acquired the status of an established aesthetic, what remains available to an artist genuinely interested in disturbing expectations?

One answer is ‘Edinburgh Man’. Its gently chiming guitars, sympathetic backing vocals and wistful melodic movement seem to belong to a different musical tradition entirely. There is something of Galaxie 500 in the arrangement, particularly in the way a modest amount of electric abrasion prevents the sweetness from becoming oppressive. At a time when Manchester was being enthusiastically repackaged as the centre of the musical universe, Smith's imaginative relocation elsewhere feels entirely appropriate. What makes the recording remarkable is its refusal to undermine his affection. There is no great eruption of noise, no sudden grotesque revelation and no apparent need to humiliate the musicians for having produced something beautiful.

For an audience that had developed a considerable appetite for confrontation, this was arguably a more radical decision than introducing another badly behaved synthesiser. The distinction between accessibility and conventionality becomes particularly interesting when ‘Edinburgh Man’ is considered alongside ‘Underground Medicine’. The latter's manic drumming, crowded guitar figures and exuberantly intrusive keyboards belong to a group attempting to fit several musical ideas into a space conventionally reserved for one. Its peculiar melodic sensibility is already present, but it is competing with the group's considerable enthusiasm for making a racket. By 1991, The Fall had acquired sufficient confidence to let a melody exist without continually demonstrating their opposition to it.

Which makes the treatment of Brix Smith's ‘Shiny Things’, transformed into ‘Bonkers in Phoenix’ four years later, particularly fascinating. The finished recording subjects its melodic material to an extraordinary barrage of electronic interference, pitch manipulation and spoken interruptions. Brix's accelerated voice appears to be broadcasting from inside a malfunctioning children's television programme. The melody survives the assault, and its survival becomes the organising principle of the recording. The noise acquires expressive value because something recognisably delicate is struggling beneath it. Where ‘Edinburgh Man’ trusts the expressive potential of sweetness, ‘Bonkers in Phoenix’ turns sweetness into the principal casualty of an imaginary sonic catastrophe.

Neither approach is intrinsically more experimental than the other. What matters is whether the musicians have discovered something about the material that was not immediately apparent when it arrived.

The instrument that refuses to belong

The Monks' banjo is an important ancestor of Marcia Schofield's synthetic brass on ‘In These Times’. Both sounds possess cultural associations that make their presence in these particular musical environments faintly ridiculous. The brass preset has none of the physical authority of an actual horn section, but its ostentatious artificiality becomes an expressive resource. Steve Hanley's wonderfully mobile bass provides the foundation for a brisk, almost rockabilly arrangement, while the keyboard and the vocal interplay between Smith and Brix continually interfere with its straightforward momentum. The recording is unusually busy without becoming congested, a distinction that the younger group had not entirely mastered on ‘Underground Medicine’.

Smith's peculiar aquatic feline, his Jesuits and his portable cassette players occupy a landscape in which technological novelty and cultural homogenisation have become inseparable. His reported irritation at encountering familiar Anglo-American popular music during a visit to Italy supplies a particularly revealing context. Travel promises an escape from one's immediate cultural surroundings, only to reveal that those surroundings have arrived first. The synthetic brass belongs perfectly to this world of manufactured experiences. The cheerful arrangement makes the landscape considerably more hospitable than the words suggest, just as the gentle melody of ‘Bonkers in Phoenix’ makes its surrounding electronic unpleasantness seem more violent.

The wheezing keyboard that intrudes into ‘New Puritan’ performs a comparable function. Its contribution is relatively brief, but it alters the perceived character of the surrounding instrumentation. It possesses the indignity of an asthmatic church organ attempting to participate in an exorcism. The effectiveness of these sounds has little to do with the sophistication of the equipment or the virtuosity of its operator. Their importance lies in their apparent refusal to acknowledge the musical assumptions established by the other instruments. The Fall's keyboards were often most interesting when they sounded as though they had been brought to the wrong rehearsal.

This helps explain a curious limitation of ‘Brillo de Facto’. The final incarnation of The Fall had developed a thinner, slinkier guitar sound, with something of the Jon Spencer Blues Explosion in its distorted relationship with rhythm and blues. The clipped guitar figure also recalls the peculiar rhythmic logic of Captain Beefheart's ‘I'm Gonna Booglarize You Baby’. The dub-like breakdown and changes in instrumental density suggest a group still capable of surprising itself. Yet Elena Poulou's absence is conspicuous. The arrangement occasionally seems to be waiting for an additional voice capable of challenging its existing relationships, rather than another musician capable of reinforcing them.

It is not necessarily a question of needing keyboards. The Monks could have replaced their banjo with another guitar and retained approximately the same instrumental competence while losing a substantial portion of their musical identity. What ‘Brillo de Facto’ sometimes lacks is precisely that sense of an instrument refusing to belong.

The unequal distribution of creative cruelty

The transformation of ‘Shiny Things’ raises a question that admirers of The Fall are occasionally reluctant to confront: when does artistic intervention become an abuse of artistic authority? Brix's dissatisfaction with the result cannot simply be dismissed as an unfortunate failure to appreciate the genius of the finished product. Nor does that dissatisfaction automatically invalidate the recording. A work can be artistically successful while emerging from an unpleasant creative process. The studio practices of Faust and Lee Scratch Perry offer useful precedents for the manipulation of recorded material, but they do not settle the question of what happens when the material being manipulated belongs to another creative personality with substantially different intentions.

The relative gentleness with which the acoustic guitar and singing associated with Ed Blaney are treated on ‘Rude (All the Time)’ makes the issue considerably more interesting. Smith's interjections offer occasional moments of verbal interest, but the arrangement possesses little of the tension that makes ‘Bonkers in Phoenix’ so compelling. Its informality occasionally resembles the aftermath of a pub performance in which somebody has neglected to turn off the recording equipment. The curious thing is not that Smith vandalised one composition. It is that he apparently considered the other sufficiently interesting to leave largely intact.

There is no consistent aesthetic principle that can adequately explain these decisions. ‘Edinburgh Man’ demonstrates the considerable benefits of allowing a relatively straightforward composition to retain its emotional character. ‘Bonkers in Phoenix’ demonstrates the possibilities of doing almost precisely the opposite. ‘Rude (All the Time)’ suggests that leaving something alone can also amount to an abdication of creative responsibility. The mistake is to regard Smith's interventions as inherently beneficial simply because they were Smith's interventions. His artistic judgement was capable of transforming mediocre material, ruining promising material or discovering possibilities that its original author could scarcely have imagined.

The relationship between creative conflict and personal conflict remains impossible to disentangle completely. Nevertheless, it is worth remembering that the most productive tensions within The Fall were rarely generated by Smith alone. They depended upon the existence of musicians whose instincts, however reluctantly, he was obliged to accommodate. The magnificent instability of ‘New Puritan’ requires a group capable of generating musical events that its singer cannot entirely dictate. A dictator without meaningful opposition is unlikely to produce particularly interesting political theatre, let alone particularly interesting rock music.

When rebellion becomes a house style

The video for ‘Reformation!’ presents the newly recruited American musicians as though they have been dispatched from another planet to assist an increasingly agitated man in a hotel room. It is an unusually appropriate visual representation of what The Fall had become: a science-fiction concept involving the periodic replacement of human components. Following the departure of much of the preceding line-up during the 2006 American tour, the very existence of the reconstructed group constituted a statement of defiance. But the survival of the institution and the survival of its artistic purpose were not necessarily the same achievement.

Smith's numerical fragments, non sequiturs and barked declarations on ‘Reformation!’ offer the pleasure of recognition to an audience unusually well acquainted with the constituent parts of his performances. The recording is almost a form of fan service, reassuring listeners that the essential characteristics of The Fall remain operational. Compare this with the extraordinary lyrical world of ‘In These Times’, where apparently unrelated observations accumulate into a recognisable cultural anxiety, or the Roman Totale mythology hovering around ‘Underground Medicine’. In those earlier recordings, Smith's peculiar vocabulary seems to be developing in response to an expanding imaginative world. On ‘Reformation!’, it occasionally seems to be responding to the established requirements of a Mark E. Smith performance.

This is why the final album's ‘Brillo de Facto’ is more encouraging than its relatively modest musical achievements might suggest. Smith's fragments of celestial imagery, cake shops and infantile vulnerability suggest an emotional world far removed from the functional aggression of ‘Reformation!’. The musicians, meanwhile, are exploring rhythmic possibilities that do not depend entirely upon the established Fall template. The recording is imperfect, but its imperfections are those of a group attempting to discover something, rather than those of a group attempting to demonstrate that it still exists. The distinction returns us to the Monks, whose apparent inability to produce conventional pop music made their efforts to do so particularly rewarding.

The proposition at the centre of ‘New Puritan’ was never likely to provide a comfortable foundation for a forty-year career. An artist committed to opposing convention must eventually confront the conventions established by his own work. Smith's response was inconsistent, occasionally cruel and sometimes magnificently counterintuitive. But somewhere between the gentle guitars of ‘Edinburgh Man’, the cheap brass of ‘In These Times’ and the electronic desecration of ‘Bonkers in Phoenix’ lies the reason The Fall remained interesting long after their original musical vocabulary had become familiar. Their greatest records were not necessarily those that sounded most like The Fall. They were those on which the musicians seemed least certain what sounding like The Fall was supposed to mean.
