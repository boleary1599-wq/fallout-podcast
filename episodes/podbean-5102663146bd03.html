THE RIGHT KIND OF WRONG

Incompetence, imitation, infernal fruit and The Fall’s lifelong war against musical competence

By Neville Pseud, Visiting Lecturer in Applied Shambles

I. THE BEAUTY OF GETTING IT WRONG

There are two kinds of bad music. The first is made by musicians who cannot play. The second is made by musicians who can play but have absolutely nothing worth communicating. Mark E. Smith generally preferred the former, although he was perfectly capable of employing representatives of both categories simultaneously. The distinction is fundamental to understanding The Fall. Their finest recordings frequently sound as though they are in imminent danger of collapsing, yet somehow acquire a force and individuality that more accomplished groups spend entire careers attempting to manufacture. The trick is that incompetence alone is insufficient. There must be an idea, a personality, an antagonism or at least somebody in the room with the determination to make the whole enterprise profoundly uncomfortable. Otherwise, the result is merely a collection of people making a noise. Consider the distance between No Xmas for John Quays and Anecdotes + Antidotes in B#. Both operate in the vicinity of musical crudity. Both reject conventional sophistication. Yet one possesses the alarming vitality of an electrical accident, while the other threatens to become an extended demonstration of why rehearsal rooms should have time limits. It concerns the difference between music that sounds as though it has escaped from its creators and music that sounds as though its creators have wandered off and left it running. The Fall were capable of making both.

II. NO CHRISTMAS FOR THE UNDESERVING

No Xmas for John Quays is one of the great early examples of The Fall's ability to transform an apparently primitive arrangement into something completely possessed. Yet the recording possesses an urgency that more technically proficient punk groups frequently failed to achieve. Smith's vocal performance is the essential ingredient. He sounds less like a singer than a man attempting to communicate urgent information while being forcibly removed from a building. His voice cracks, accelerates and threatens to detach itself from the music altogether. The performance is grotesque, funny and strangely exhilarating. The song's Christmas setting provides an especially productive contrast. Popular music traditionally presents Christmas as an occasion for communal warmth, sentimentality and the compulsory purchase of records by Slade. Smith offers an alternative seasonal experience involving exclusion, irritation and the uncomfortable spectacle of somebody who has clearly failed to receive the appropriate festive instructions. It is Christmas as imagined by a particularly vindictive social worker. The reference to Frankie Lymon introduces another layer of pop history. The teenage rock-and-roll idol belongs to a tradition of youthful exuberance that Smith approaches from the direction of exhaustion, resentment and premature decay. In My Area operates with a different kind of awkwardness. Its guitar figures can sound almost deliberately limited, with the arrangement occasionally suggesting musicians attempting to play the same song from slightly different sets of instructions. But this instability creates its own pleasures. The guitar work becomes a form of argument, while the repetitive structure establishes a peculiar territorial insistence. Smith's geography is never merely descriptive. An area is somewhere to defend, investigate, distrust or escape. The music's roughness gives that territory its character. Neither recording requires virtuosity. Both require conviction.

III. THE WAR AGAINST INTELLIGENCE

The War Against Intelligence possesses one of the most promising titles in The Fall's catalogue. It suggests an entire political philosophy, a social catastrophe and possibly the minutes of a particularly unpleasant school governors' meeting. Unfortunately, a magnificent title cannot guarantee a magnificent recording. The song emerges from the group's early-1990s engagement with contemporary production methods, electronic textures and a comparatively polished rhythmic vocabulary. This was not inherently a problem. The Fall had always absorbed outside influences, frequently transforming them into something barely recognisable. The difficulty arises when the borrowed musical language remains insufficiently transformed. A conventional chord sequence, a relatively accessible chorus and a few unusual vocal gestures do not automatically constitute an interesting experiment. There are moments of genuine intrigue, particularly in the relationship between Smith's voice and the strange sounds operating around it. But the recording sometimes seems caught between two ambitions. It wants to be a recognisable pop song and a disruptive Fall performance. Can a band devoted to musical awkwardness become too competent for its own good? Not necessarily. Competence is not the enemy. Predictability is. The problem with the least successful Fall recordings is not that the musicians have learned to play. It is that the music occasionally forgets to do anything unexpected with that ability. And then, quite suddenly, the Devil arrives carrying a fruit bowl.

IV. DR FAUSTUS AND THE INFERNAL GREENGROCER

Dktr. Faustus is a magnificent example of The Fall transforming apparent nonsense into an extraordinarily vivid imaginative environment. The song draws upon the Faust legend, with its familiar themes of forbidden knowledge, temptation and supernatural bargains. But Smith's treatment of the material is gloriously eccentric. Instead of presenting the Devil through conventional Gothic spectacle, the recording becomes fascinated by exotic fruit, peculiar aristocratic arrangements and the suspicious availability of agricultural produce during winter. The supernatural manifests itself through an unusually well-stocked greengrocer. There is something wonderfully British about this. A German scholar may sell his soul in pursuit of unlimited knowledge, but an English observer is more likely to become concerned about where the Count obtained his bananas. The arrangement matches this imaginative disorder. Its rhythms shift, its instrumental textures collide and its keyboard figures create a peculiar atmosphere somewhere between a medieval entertainment and a malfunctioning fairground attraction. Brix Smith's backing vocals provide one of the recording's most distinctive elements. Her bright, apparently incongruous contributions cut across Smith's ominous delivery, producing a contrast that is simultaneously comic and unsettling. The repeated references to fruit ought to destroy the song's atmosphere. Instead, they become central to it. This is the crucial difference between productive absurdity and mere randomness. The Fall were particularly accomplished at this form of construction. A strange historical reference, an inappropriate instrumental sound and an apparently meaningless vocal interruption could combine to produce something far more distinctive than any conventional arrangement. Smith's literary interests were rarely straightforward exercises in adaptation. He did not simply retell established stories. He interfered with them. Goethe supplied the metaphysical architecture. The Fall installed a banana stall in the entrance hall.

V. THE TERRIBLE FREEDOM OF THE JAM

The distinction between deliberate disorder and accidental tedium becomes particularly important with Anecdotes + Antidotes in B#. The recording emerges from a later period when The Fall's arrangements could sometimes appear assembled from fragments of improvisation, electronic experimentation and whatever happened to be available in the studio. There is nothing inherently wrong with this approach. Can, Faust and numerous experimental groups demonstrated that improvisation could generate forms of collective intelligence unavailable to conventional composition. But successful improvisation requires an unusual degree of attention. The musicians must listen, respond and recognise when something genuinely interesting has happened. Otherwise, improvisation becomes an excuse for not making decisions. Anecdotes + Antidotes in B# risks precisely this problem. Its instrumental gestures suggest the possibility of an interesting development without consistently delivering one. The wah-wah guitar, in particular, raises a difficult philosophical question. Why? The wah-wah pedal is a wonderful invention in appropriate circumstances. Jimi Hendrix could transform it into an extension of his expressive vocabulary. The Stooges could make it sound dangerous and obscene. But the mere presence of a wah-wah pedal does not constitute an artistic idea. Sometimes it simply indicates that somebody has discovered a switch. It is tempting to interpret every awkward passage as deliberate subversion, every repetitive section as hypnotic minimalism and every unfinished arrangement as a courageous rejection of commercial expectations. This is nonsense. Sometimes a bad recording is simply a bad recording. The group's greatness becomes more interesting, not less, when that possibility is acknowledged.

VI. CITY DWELLER AND THE DANCE-FLOOR PROBLEM

City Dweller approaches the problem of contemporary musical fashion from another direction. Its electronic textures and rhythmic structure place it within The Fall's complicated relationship with early-1990s dance culture. For a group so closely associated with abrasive guitar music, their willingness to engage with electronic forms was entirely consistent with their history. Smith had never been especially interested in maintaining the boundaries that critics constructed around his work. City Dweller possesses a certain rhythmic appeal, but its production can also feel oddly restrained. The music gestures towards dance-floor energy without entirely committing to the physical force that such an approach might demand. Yet the lyrics offer something more substantial. The city becomes another of Smith's hostile environments, populated by dubious individuals and institutions whose activities provoke suspicion. Dance music traditionally promises collective pleasure, release and temporary escape. Smith's urban imagination is considerably less optimistic. This tension gives the recording a distinctive identity even when its musical execution remains imperfect. The Fall's experiments with electronic music were most successful when they treated new technology as raw material rather than a set of fashionable instructions. Smith did not need to become a dance musician. He needed to discover how to make dance music behave badly.

VII. GREEN-EYED MEN AND BORROWED CLOTHES

Green Eyed Loco-Man belongs to a later incarnation of The Fall in which the group's sound could become surprisingly expansive. The guitars are fuller, the arrangement more confident and the overall presentation closer to conventional alternative rock. There is a swagger to the recording that earlier versions of the group might have regarded with considerable suspicion. But this introduces another problem. When The Fall sound like a competent rock band, what distinguishes them from all the other competent rock bands? His phrasing, pronunciation and refusal to behave like a conventional singer can destabilise even the most straightforward arrangement. Yet the surrounding music sometimes approaches the kind of anthemic rock vocabulary that The Fall traditionally existed to undermine. Scenario presents almost the opposite difficulty. Its loose construction and unusual vocal qualities recall the group's longstanding appetite for musical eccentricity. But eccentricity does not automatically produce originality. The recording invites comparisons with earlier experimental rock, including Captain Beefheart and the psychedelic American underground. Those influences are fascinating, but their presence raises a distinction between transformation and imitation. Beefheart's music could sound chaotic while depending upon extraordinarily precise arrangements. The apparent disorder was often meticulously organised. The Fall's best recordings achieve a related effect through different methods: repetition, friction, unexpected decisions and Smith's capacity to impose a personality upon almost any musical situation. But when those elements fail to combine, the result can sound less like a radical experiment than a rehearsal that somebody accidentally released. And a difficult recording is not automatically a profound one.

VIII. THE INCOMPETENCE PRINCIPLE

Few groups have been so consistently celebrated for refusing conventional musical standards, yet few have demonstrated so clearly that artistic freedom requires discipline of its own. That discipline need not resemble technical virtuosity. It may involve the ability to recognise an effective repetition, an unexpected instrumental combination or a vocal performance whose apparent absurdity conceals a remarkable imaginative precision. No Xmas for John Quays possesses that precision through sheer urgency. Dktr. Faustus achieves it through a bizarre but coherent imaginative world. In My Area transforms limited musical resources into a distinctive sense of place. Elsewhere, the same methods produce less convincing results. A repetitive figure becomes tedious. An improvised passage loses direction. A borrowed musical style remains insufficiently transformed. A group willing to take genuine risks must occasionally produce something genuinely dreadful. Smith's greatest achievement was to establish a musical universe in which the unexpected remained possible. It was a universe where a rudimentary keyboard could become more expressive than a virtuoso guitarist, where a list of fruit could acquire supernatural significance and where the distinction between genius and rubbish might depend upon a single unreasonable vocal intervention. The Fall were not great because they couldn't play. They were great because, at their best, they made conventional ideas of playing seem completely beside the point. And when they were terrible, they were frequently terrible in ways that no other group would have thought to attempt. Which is, admittedly, not much consolation if you've just spent six minutes listening to somebody experiment with a wah-wah pedal.
