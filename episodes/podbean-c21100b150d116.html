The Company Supplied Caked Up Speed

Mark E. Smith, Lee Perry and the strange business of deciding what constitutes a finished record.

By Cyril Drainpipe

The trouble with discovering Lee “Scratch” Perry through his Black Ark recordings is that everything subsequently has to pass through the Black Ark. The relatively clean production of his later work can initially seem like a betrayal. Where is that extraordinary psychedelic murk? Where are the drums that sound as though somebody has discovered an additional physical state between liquid and solid? Why can I suddenly hear everything?

The Fall present much the same problem. An attachment to the black-and-white abrasiveness of Dragnet can become an impediment to appreciating the group that made Your Future Our Clutter. A sufficiently thorough exploration of either artist eventually requires the listener to dismantle the very criteria that attracted them in the first place. The dirt was never the whole story.

Perry and Smith were avant-garde without particularly needing to announce themselves as such. Perry could organise a sophisticated piece of psychedelic music around an insult concerning a rat; Smith could extract an examination of personal identity from an episode of Neighbours. Neither needed the permission of a respectable artistic institution to regard these activities as worthwhile. Their affinity extends beyond reggae's influence on The Fall to the organisation of sound itself: the allocation of space, the management of attention and the decision about when something should stop.

The unfinished business of finishing

Pete Greenway's performance on ‘Nine Out of Ten’ presents an awkward problem for anyone attempting to establish the musical principles upon which The Fall operate. The guitar is repetitive, excessively reverberant and seemingly incapable of developing beyond its initial proposition. It is also magnificent. The recording preserves the peculiar sensation of a musician reaching the end of his contribution and then being obliged to inhabit it for longer. The extra duration changes the listener's relationship with the guitar without requiring the guitarist to discover a new chord.

There is a related moment at the end of ‘Muzorewi's Daughter’, where the extraordinarily insistent guitar figure begins to disintegrate. The recording briefly acquires another personality. One almost wishes that this deterioration had occurred earlier, allowing the detuned, collapsing sound to become a substantial component of the arrangement rather than a terminal event. It is a strange criticism to level at a piece of music: that it should have fallen apart sooner.

The distinction between these recordings, separated by nearly four decades, is revealing. The young Fall allow the dissolution of their musical structure to signify an ending. The elderly Smith treats the apparent ending as an opportunity to discover another structure altogether. Both approaches depend upon recognising musical potential in events that a competent producer might ordinarily regard as mistakes.

‘Light/Fireworks’ inhabits a territory where the distinction between an incidental sound and a compositional element has become almost meaningless. Its electronic fragments, peculiar vocalisations and apparent documentary material resist organisation around the successful delivery of a conventional song. The recording itself is the event. It belongs alongside the moments in Perry's work where the listener becomes fascinated by a sound whose original musical function is no longer obvious.

Dub's fundamental insight is that the recorded performance can become raw material for another act of composition. Removing an instrument, allowing an echo to occupy the resulting space or interrupting the continuity of a rhythm can be as consequential as writing a new melody. Listen to the physical presence of the drums on ‘Zion's Blood’: the production makes the rhythmic event seem larger than the individual actions required to produce it. Smith was not Perry's equivalent at a mixing desk. His musical intelligence frequently manifested itself through instructions to other people. But both recognised possibilities of which the performers themselves might be unaware.

Sometimes the guitarist needs to carry on. Sometimes the guitarist needs to stop. Sometimes the fireworks are more interesting than the guitarist.

The advantages of leaving a hole

What Perry could achieve by removing an instrument, The Fall could sometimes achieve simply by resisting the temptation to introduce another one. The slide guitar at the beginning of ‘Petty (Thief) Lout’ suggests a spacious, almost jazzy conception of country music; the exposed guitar and ominous tom patterns of ‘Muzorewi's Daughter’ establish an equally intriguing relationship with quite different materials. Both recordings become less interesting when their arrangements acquire a more muscular rock identity. The additional weight obscures the very relationships that made the music compelling.

There is a useful distinction here between filling a space and exploiting it. On ‘Weather Report 2’, the alternating chords establish a gentle, almost post-rock atmosphere in which Elena Poulou's keyboard interventions can acquire a character of their own. One can imagine Tortoise introducing vibraphone without fundamentally disturbing the arrangement. When distorted bass and bleak electronic percussion emerge, however, the new sounds constitute an intrusion rather than an enlargement of what was already there. The Fall accommodate within one recording something of the stylistic distance between the Velvet Underground's White Light/White Heat and their third album.

Perry's ‘Zion's Blood’ offers another way of hearing that distinction. The physical depth of the recording depends on relationships between sounds that cannot adequately be explained by assessing individual instrumental performances. Even the apparently simple pop construction of ‘People Funny Boy’ makes room for an unexpected sound to alter the character of the record. An arrangement can be remarkably economical while accommodating a great deal of information.

This helps explain why subtraction is no guarantee of artistic success. R. Dean Taylor's ‘Gotta See Jane’ uses strings and guitar to participate in the emotional development of its pop melodrama; The Fall's version removes much of that complexity without establishing a comparably interesting relationship between the remaining materials. The fuller country-rock arrangement of ‘Petty (Thief) Lout’ and the comparatively reduced arrangement of ‘Gotta See Jane’ arrive at a similar problem from opposite directions. Neither quite capitalises on the possibilities suggested by its constituent sounds.

Reusing an arrangement presents a related difficulty. The musical material shared by ‘Don't Take the Pizza’ and ‘Hey! Student’ invites comparison with Perry's repeated transformations of rhythmic material: what changes when the same underlying proposition acquires a different vocal identity? Smith's percussive interjections and the sprawling guitar breakdown in ‘Pizza’ suggest possibilities beyond the central joke, which struggles to sustain the performance. The point of leaving a hole in an arrangement is that something interesting might occupy it. A joke, an echo, a keyboard or a collapsing guitar figure can all perform that function; none has an automatic entitlement to the space.

Five episodes of Murder, She Wrote

David Cronenberg's Videodrome begins its exploration of the relationship between technology, violence and desire with the discovery of an illicit television transmission. Its power derives partly from the impression that the viewer has gained access to something that was not intended for public consumption. Dragnet often produces precisely that sensation.

The stark guitar figure of ‘Muzorewi's Daughter’, its almost ceremonial drumming and Smith's increasingly unhinged squeals create the impression of a private ritual accidentally intercepted by a radio receiver. Yet the political and racial imagery complicates the pleasures of that transgression. The song's apparent hostility towards Bishop Abel Muzorewa and its cannibalistic imagery cannot simply be excused on the grounds that disturbing people was part of The Fall's artistic remit. Finding a recording compelling need not entail endorsing the imaginative world it establishes.

Television would continue to provide Smith with material, although the illicit transmission of Videodrome is some distance from the afternoon scheduling of Neighbours. Susan Kennedy's return to an adolescent psychological state following an accident is exactly the sort of narrative that a respectable literary culture might dismiss while celebrating an equivalent premise in a novel by an established European experimentalist.

The propulsive electronics of ‘Susan vs Youthclub’ are an inspired accompaniment to this peculiar disturbance of personal identity. Its squelching synthesiser and abrasive guitar occupy an uncertain territory between dance music and rock. The instability of the musical idiom becomes part of the instability of the premise.

Something equally strange happens when Murder, She Wrote enters the melancholy environment of ‘Weather Report 2’. Smith's observations concerning mistakes, respect, sex and the passage of time coexist with the mundane experience of repeatedly watching Angela Lansbury solve murders. A more self-consciously literary songwriter might have removed the television reference to preserve the emotional seriousness of the composition. Smith understands that human beings do not suspend their consumption of rubbish television while confronting mortality.

Perry's ‘White Belly Rat’ and ‘Bathroom Skank’ suggest a comparable refusal to segregate the ridiculous from the musically sophisticated. The pleasure of the insult, the bodily joke and the peculiar rhythmic construction can coexist. What makes ‘Light/Fireworks’ affecting is its suggestion that an ordinary evening might contain sufficient material for a work of art. The world is already making noises; the artist needs only to establish a sufficiently interesting relationship with them.

The missing tenth

There is a peculiar contradiction in the reception of ‘Nine Out of Ten’. Greenway's guitar performance can be experienced as either an extraordinary feat of physical and emotional endurance or an interminable exercise in strumming. The recording provides little assistance in resolving the disagreement. It simply continues.

That insistence connects it with the Xerox-like repetition of ‘Muzorewi's Daughter’, but the emotional consequences are radically different. The earlier recording uses repetition to establish menace. The later one creates an impression of exposure. Smith's vocal is left with nowhere to hide. His account of an orphaned childhood should not be mistaken for straightforward autobiography. Its power resides in the construction of a life repeatedly subjected to external judgement. From infancy onwards, somebody appears to be assessing the narrator's performance.

Nine out of ten is an especially funny judgement because it implies the existence of an identifiable deficiency without requiring anyone to explain what that deficiency might be. Music criticism has institutionalised this peculiar form of withholding. The masterpiece receives nine because the critic cannot quite bring themselves to imagine that the business of making masterpieces has reached its conclusion.

Perhaps the missing point is the excessive guitar reverb. Perhaps it is the unnecessary country-rock arrangement. Perhaps it is the pizza joke that goes on too long. Perhaps the artist should have watched something other than Murder, She Wrote.

Perry's later recordings and The Fall's less celebrated albums expose the inadequacy of such fixed systems of assessment. A listener who insists that every Perry recording should reproduce the Black Ark atmosphere, or that every Fall album should possess the characteristics of Dragnet, is effectively penalising both artists for continuing to work. The very process of discovering what makes ‘Weather Report 2’ beautiful requires a willingness to abandon some of the expectations established by the music of 1979. Likewise, the cleaner surfaces of later Perry recordings can reveal the humour and rhythmic intelligence that an exclusive fascination with the Black Ark's production might obscure.

And so the guitar resumes on ‘Nine Out of Ten’. Smith has apparently said what he needs to say, but the recording refuses to grant him the conventional dignity of a final statement. There is no orchestral resolution, no concluding philosophical observation, no carefully staged departure. There is just Pete Greenway, still playing.

The company supplied nine out of ten. It neglected to supply instructions for the remaining point.
