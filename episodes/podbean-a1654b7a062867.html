Episode 4 · It Is the Whole Truth!

By Janet Johnny

A critical essay on the fourth F.A.L.L.O.U.T. showdown · 6 June 2021

THE UNFINISHED SONG

The Fall were a band for whom a song was less a finished composition than a temporary settlement between incompatible forces. A riff might be held in place long after another group would have introduced a chorus; a vocal could behave like an interruption rather than a melody; a perfectly serviceable pop idea might be roughed up until it appeared to have been found under a floorboard. What makes the music unmistakable is not a single sound. It is the sense that several different versions of the same record are trying to happen at once.

That is why the question of a first favourite, raised early in this episode, matters more than it initially seems. A first favourite is rarely the most representative track. It is the one that teaches a listener how to hear the band. The hammering economy of “Cab It Up!” can provide an entrance; the prolonged, almost hostile momentum of “Deer Park” can provide another. Neither is a complete map. The Fall's catalogue punishes the idea that there is one authorised doorway, because each new lineup and recording method changes the terms of entry.

Consider “L.A.” and “Slates, Slags, Etc.” The former has a taut, oddly sleek propulsion. Its repetition is almost danceable, but not quite welcoming: the pulse advances while the vocal remains coolly detached. “Slates, Slags, Etc.” is more abrasive and crowded, a record that seems to make its own friction audible. These are not merely different moods. They are different answers to the question of what a Fall arrangement should do. One pares the mechanism down until its motion is the event; the other lets the mechanism scrape and shudder. A listener who calls one more authentically Fall-like than the other has usually mistaken a personal point of entry for a rule.

WHAT THE RECORDING DOES TO THE SONG

The difference between a composition and a recording becomes especially sharp when a song survives in several performances. The Fall's many radio sessions, live tapes and revised arrangements are not just supplementary material for collectors. They expose how little of the music can be separated from the conditions in which it was made. A bass line that sounds buried on one recording can become the organising principle of another; a keyboard part can move from background colour to an intrusive second voice. The same title does not guarantee the same experience.

“M5” is an unusually useful case. In the episode, the discussion turns to the Peel-session performance and what it reveals beside the album version. That observation opens a larger issue than whether the session is “better.” Production is not a transparent window onto a song. It determines which collisions we can hear. If an effect is less enveloping, the attack of an instrument becomes clearer; if the voices are differently balanced, the entire apparent hierarchy of the band changes. A version can sound more ferocious precisely because it is less imposing. The music has not been purified: it has been rearranged by the recording itself.

Set “M5” against “He Pep!” and the notion of a stable Fall style becomes harder still to defend. The latter belongs to a more openly synthetic, unsettled vocabulary, where hooks and awkward textures jostle rather than resolve. The contrast is not between a real band and an artificial one. The Fall had always been interested in making the familiar feel badly assembled. The technology changes, but the antagonism remains. What sounds cheap or eccentric in one decade can turn out to be the most durable feature of the record in another.

Even the apparently straightforward “Ed’s Babe” against “Yes O Yes” asks us to hear past the false opposition between accessibility and difficulty. A recognisable tune is not an escape from The Fall's strangeness. Sometimes it is the delivery system for it. The band could make a hook feel like an accident, or make repetition so obstinate that a fragment of melody arrived as a surprise. Their pop instinct was real; so was their determination to interfere with it.

THE RIGHT TO SOUND WRONG

One of the least useful ways to divide The Fall's work is into properly realised masterpieces and embarrassing leftovers. It assumes the music was always trying to achieve the same kind of finish. But an important part of its achievement is the right to sound wrong: too repetitive, too abrupt, too thin, too thick, too synthetic, too casually recorded. These apparent faults are not automatically virtues. Some tracks really do exhaust their ideas. The point is that conventional polish is a particularly poor test of whether a Fall record has succeeded.

“Systematic Abuse” and “First One Today” make an excellent final pairing for precisely that reason. They do not offer the grand assurance of a signature anthem. Their attraction lies in their stubborn, sometimes ungainly construction, the sense of music being pushed into existence with whatever happens to be available. It is easy to confuse such surfaces with a lack of ambition. Yet there is an ambition in refusing the approved gestures of seriousness: in treating a flimsy sound as something worth insisting upon, or allowing a crude motif to remain crude because refinement would remove its force.

This is also why the knockout format is productive even when its verdicts are not. Pairing two songs forces an argument about the criteria we usually leave unstated. Are we rewarding invention, impact, atmosphere, durability, the particular record we heard first, or the performance that finally made a neglected song click? A bracket cannot settle those questions. It can, however, reveal that they are different questions. The Fall are an exceptionally awkward band to rank because their best music keeps changing the meaning of “best.”

The whole truth, then, is not a definitive list. It is that the song, the arrangement, the recorded object and the listener's memory are never quite identical. The Fall's greatness lives in the gap between them: a catalogue that sounds as though it is continually disagreeing with itself, and is more alive for doing so.
