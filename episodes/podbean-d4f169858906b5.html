The Strange Machinery of The Fall

By Hip Priestley

Tommy Cooper, Cold War paranoia, occult transmissions and the unholy business of making a beautiful record: eight songs from a band that never quite trusted the idea of music.

Before anybody plays a record, the conversation turns to the noises Mark E. Smith made with his mouth. Not the lyrics, or even the singing, but the extraordinary collection of squawks, guttural eruptions, strangled falsettos and apparently involuntary bodily emissions that accompanied his performances for forty years. Phil favours the Tommy Cooper noises, those deep, ridiculous expulsions that seem to originate somewhere below the diaphragm. Others nominate the lupine banshee, the rusted bathroom toilet and the peculiar relationship between Smith's slouch, his cigarette and his ability to project sounds that no vocal coach would willingly acknowledge.

It is an excellent place to begin an investigation of The Fall, because Smith's voice was never merely a means of delivering words. It was an instrument whose limitations became its defining possibilities. He could transform an ordinary syllable into an accusation, a meaningless phrase into a memorable hook, or a perfectly respectable piece of music into something resembling a man being attacked by his own digestive system. The noises were not interruptions of the performance. They were part of its musical architecture.

Episode Eleven's four pairings explore what happens when that unruly instrument encounters different kinds of musical machinery: primitive punk, Cold War electronics, unexpectedly tender pop and the extended repetitions of the late Fall. What emerges is a catalogue of productive contradictions. The group could be simultaneously sophisticated and amateurish, emotionally affecting and deliberately ridiculous, mechanically repetitive and gloriously unpredictable. Its greatest achievement may have been the discovery that none of these qualities needed to be reconciled.

1. Cary Grant's Wedding: The Cultural Revolution Will Have a Buffet

“Cary Grant's Wedding”, preserved on the gloriously disreputable live album Totale's Turns, begins with a vocal eruption that ought to be included in any serious history of rock singing. Smith's opening scream is confrontational, absurd and remarkably expressive, announcing a performance in which the usual distinction between musical competence and theatrical aggression has become almost irrelevant.

The arrangement is stranger than its rough surface initially suggests. A heavy, ominous guitar figure gives way to something approaching a jaunty punk romp, creating a collision between Black Sabbath's primitive menace and the more economical pleasures of new wave. The contrast is crucial: The Fall are capable of making the music sound simultaneously threatening and ridiculous, with Smith's vocal performance providing the unstable connection between the two.

The song's celebrity references operate in a similarly contradictory fashion. Cary Grant represents the polished mythology of Hollywood, an apparently effortless sophistication maintained through an enormous apparatus of publicity, performance and carefully managed appearances. Smith's decision to place this figure within a rough, confrontational punk song transforms the wedding into a grotesque cultural ceremony. The references to Joe Strummer and Jake Burns complicate matters further, bringing the supposedly authentic world of punk into contact with the celebrity machinery it claimed to reject.

The accusation is not simply that punk musicians have become famous. It is that the scene's claims to authenticity are themselves theatrical constructions, subject to the same rituals of status, recognition and exclusion as the establishment they oppose. A wedding invitation becomes a wonderfully petty symbol of cultural legitimacy: who has been included, who has been excluded, and who has decided that attendance matters in the first place?

Mystic Martin's suggestion that the song belongs to a tradition of surreal, confrontational attacks on the punk establishment is persuasive, although the recording's humour is more generous than straightforward denunciation. Smith seems delighted by the absurdity of the material. The music's alternating heaviness and frivolity makes the entire spectacle resemble a ceremony that has been invaded by people who cannot decide whether to disrupt it or join the celebrations.

“Choc-Stock”, from Dragnet, attacks the same problem from a different direction. Its skipping rhythm, oddly cheerful guitar figure and conspicuously cheap recording quality produce a piece of music that seems determined to undermine its own claims to seriousness. The drums sound dreadful in a particularly distinctive way, while the backing vocals introduce a kind of amateur pop enthusiasm that sits awkwardly alongside the album's murkier material.

Yet the song's apparent triviality is part of its argument. Its references to popular consumption and musical taste suggest a world in which the supposed distinction between sophisticated and vulgar culture has become another instrument of social exclusion. The Fall's primitive pop song becomes a refusal to accept that the pleasures of ordinary entertainment require approval from people with better record collections.

The later Saint Etienne version demonstrates how naturally the material can function as pop, but the original possesses a more interesting awkwardness. Its pleasures are constantly compromised by the recording's ungainly texture and Smith's refusal to deliver anything resembling conventional charm. Where “Cary Grant's Wedding” attacks the pretensions of cultural authenticity through theatrical confrontation, “Choc-Stock” does so by making a deliberately silly record whose silliness is impossible to separate from its intelligence.

Neither song allows the listener the comfortable position of laughing at somebody else's bad taste. The joke extends to the people who believe themselves sophisticated enough to understand it.

2. U.S. 80's–90's: The Cold War as a Dancefloor Problem

By 1986, The Fall had developed a musical language capable of accommodating considerably more than the scratchy guitars and primitive rhythms of their earliest recordings. Brix Smith's arrival had introduced new melodic possibilities, while the rhythm section had become increasingly adept at constructing arrangements that were both mechanically insistent and surprisingly flexible. “U.S. 80's–90's”, from Bend Sinister, demonstrates how these developments could produce music that was immediately accessible without surrendering the group's essential strangeness.

The recording is built around a beautifully economical bass figure and a driving rhythm whose regularity allows the guitars and electronic effects to introduce disturbances without compromising the underlying momentum. The contrast between its comparatively straightforward rock passages and the peculiar noises operating in the background creates a sense of musical space that is unusual for The Fall. There is considerable activity within the arrangement, but much of it occurs at the margins, where small electronic interventions seem to be transmitting information from somewhere outside the song.

The title suggests a particular historical atmosphere. The late Cold War was a period in which American popular culture, technological optimism and the possibility of nuclear annihilation coexisted with remarkably little apparent difficulty. Television, advertising and pop music offered increasingly sophisticated visions of the future, while the political machinery of the present remained organised around the possibility of destroying it. The Fall's music was particularly well equipped to accommodate such contradictions, not least because Smith rarely distinguished between political paranoia and the ordinary absurdities of modern life.

“Theme from Error-Orrori”, originally appearing on the 1990 compilation Home, transforms this atmosphere into something considerably more sinister. Its lurching, uneven rhythm and oppressive instrumental textures produce an impression of machinery functioning according to instructions that have been lost. Where “U.S. 80's–90's” retains the pleasures of a recognisable rock arrangement, “Error-Orrori” seems to inhabit a world in which those pleasures have been dismantled and reassembled incorrectly.

The difference is partly rhythmic. The former song moves with confidence, its repetitive foundation creating a sense of forward motion even when the instrumental details become peculiar. “Error-Orrori” is more unstable, its shuffling momentum suggesting a machine that has developed an unpredictable fault. The music does not simply establish an atmosphere of menace; it makes the listener experience uncertainty as a physical property of the arrangement.

The Episode Eleven discussion develops an illuminating comparison between the two recordings and the competing ideologies of the Cold War. The suggestion is deliberately playful, but it identifies something important about their musical personalities. “U.S. 80's–90's” possesses the restless confidence of a culture organised around consumption and technological progress, while “Error-Orrori” offers a darker, more collective and less reassuring rhythmic environment. Neither interpretation needs to be accepted literally for the comparison to be useful.

The occult references that emerge during the conversation provide another possible approach. The Fall's music frequently suggests the existence of hidden systems, concealed authorities and messages transmitted through unreliable channels. Smith's fascination with the supernatural was never entirely separable from his interest in ordinary institutions, because both offered explanations for the uncomfortable suspicion that human behaviour might be governed by forces nobody properly understood.

What distinguishes “Error-Orrori” is that the music itself seems to participate in this uncertainty. Its unsettling character does not depend upon a clearly articulated supernatural narrative. The arrangement behaves as though something has gone wrong with the machinery of popular entertainment, leaving the listener to investigate the consequences.

3. Birthday Song: Sentiment in the Black Lodge

The Fall's reputation for aggression and musical ugliness has always obscured their remarkable capacity for beauty. Throughout the catalogue, moments of unexpected tenderness emerge from arrangements that otherwise seem determined to discourage emotional identification. These are seldom conventional love songs, and their emotional power often depends upon the suspicion that Smith is uncomfortable with the feelings he has momentarily allowed himself to express.

“Devolute”, from The Unutterable, approaches the problem from the opposite direction. Its electronic textures and fragmented construction belong to one of the group's most adventurous late-period records, an album on which Smith's increasingly unpredictable vocal performances are surrounded by arrangements that draw upon dance music, abrasive rock and experimental electronics without settling comfortably into any of them.

The recording has a peculiar, dislocated atmosphere. Its synthetic elements suggest the spaciousness of electronic music, but the vocal performance resists the smoothness such arrangements might ordinarily encourage. Smith's words appear as fragments within the musical environment rather than as the organising principle of a conventional song. The effect can be fascinating, although it also exposes the limitations of the group's more exploratory methods. An arrangement can become so thoroughly absorbed in its own instability that the listener begins to wonder whether anything substantial is being communicated.

There is a temptation to regard such uncertainty as an artistic achievement in itself. The Fall's reputation for perversity makes this particularly attractive: if a recording is confusing, abrasive or apparently unfinished, perhaps the confusion represents a deliberate rejection of conventional musical expectations. Sometimes it does. At other times, a promising experiment remains an experiment, and the absence of a satisfying resolution is less interesting than its defenders might wish.

“Birthday Song”, from The Marshall Suite, demonstrates what happens when the group's peculiar methods encounter an unexpectedly direct emotional proposition. Its gentle electronic atmosphere and wistful melodic character are almost startling within the context of an album assembled during one of the most turbulent periods of Smith's career. Rather than attempting to impose his personality upon the arrangement through aggression, he allows the music to establish a space in which vulnerability becomes possible.

The result is beautiful in a manner that The Fall rarely permitted themselves. The song's emotional character is not entirely reassuring; Smith's delivery retains its familiar detachment, and the language suggests relationships that remain complicated even when affection is acknowledged. But the recording possesses a tenderness that cannot easily be dismissed as irony.

The comparison with the dreamlike atmospheres of Twin Peaks is particularly apt. Angelo Badalamenti's music frequently makes beauty feel unsettling by placing apparently familiar emotional gestures within an environment whose underlying reality cannot be trusted. “Birthday Song” achieves something comparable through less elaborate means. Its melodic gentleness becomes more affecting because the listener knows how easily The Fall could have destroyed it.

The discussion also raises the intriguing possibility of hearing the song through the electronic textures associated with groups such as One Dove and Deep Forest. Such comparisons might initially seem improbable, but they help identify how far The Fall's musical vocabulary had travelled from the primitive rockabilly and post-punk arrangements of the early records. By the late nineties, the group could produce something approaching atmospheric electronic pop without necessarily abandoning its identity.

What makes “Birthday Song” remarkable is that its emotional force does not depend upon Smith suddenly becoming a conventional singer. His limitations remain audible, but the arrangement allows them to acquire a different significance. The voice that elsewhere communicates suspicion, irritation and contempt becomes capable of expressing affection precisely because it cannot quite deliver the polished gestures associated with sentimental pop.

There are not many Fall recordings that could reasonably be described as beautiful without considerable qualification. “Birthday Song” is one of them, and its beauty is inseparable from the disorderly catalogue in which it appears.

4. Auto Chip: How Long Can a Bad Idea Remain Interesting?

The late Fall presents a peculiar challenge to criticism because the group's defining methods had become sufficiently familiar to risk turning into conventions of their own. Primitive repetition, abrasive guitar textures, unpredictable vocal performances and apparently unfinished arrangements were no longer radical discoveries. They were the recognised characteristics of a band whose reputation had been established through decades of refusing to behave like other bands.

“Auto Chip 2014–2016”, from Sub-Lingual Tablet, exposes the problem with unusual clarity. Its extended structure depends upon a repetitive instrumental foundation whose principal attraction lies in the accumulation of small variations. The guitar work has a dry, almost desert-rock quality, while the rhythm section establishes a persistent environment through which Smith's increasingly fragmented vocal performance moves with little apparent concern for conventional development.

The recording demands patience, but patience alone does not guarantee a reward. Its repetitive figures can become hypnotic, particularly when the instrumental textures begin to shift and the arrangement acquires a greater sense of momentum. Yet there are passages where the central idea seems insufficiently substantial to justify the time devoted to it. The familiar critical defence of repetition — that persistence transforms perception — is only persuasive when something interesting actually happens to perception.

This is where the Episode Eleven disagreement becomes especially productive. Some hear the recording as a compelling example of late-period Fall, with its strange vocal noises, abrasive textures and eventual instrumental development. Others find that the extended structure exposes the weakness of the underlying riff. The comparison with Neu! is instructive, although the differences are equally significant. Neu!'s motorik rhythms often derive their force from precision and the gradual transformation of a stable musical environment. The Fall's repetitions are considerably less disciplined, their effects dependent upon the unstable relationship between instrumental persistence and Smith's refusal to behave predictably.

The song becomes more interesting towards its later stages, when the accumulated repetitions begin to produce a stronger sense of musical movement. This raises an uncomfortable question: does the eventual payoff justify the preceding material, or would the recording be more effective if somebody had exercised a little editorial discipline?

“Over! Over!”, from Reformation Post TLC, approaches repetition through a more recognisable garage-rock vocabulary. Its relationship with the American psychedelic group The United States of America is particularly revealing, demonstrating Smith's continuing fascination with obscure or neglected corners of rock history. The Fall's habit of appropriating earlier musical ideas was never simply nostalgic. They frequently treated the past as a collection of components that could be removed from their original contexts and made to perform unfamiliar functions.

Yet “Over! Over!” also exposes the dangers of this approach. Its comparatively straightforward riff and repetitive structure can seem underpowered beside the more inventive recordings of the group's earlier periods. The guitar sound possesses a certain garage-rock familiarity, but familiarity is not necessarily a virtue for a band whose greatest achievements depended upon making familiar musical gestures behave unexpectedly.

The album's circumstances complicate matters further. Reformation Post TLC followed another spectacular personnel upheaval, and its title acknowledged the process of reconstructing a group whose membership had once again been dramatically altered. Smith's recurring references to betrayal, ageing and the supposed deficiencies of other musicians make it possible to hear parts of the record as an extended commentary upon the collapse and reconstruction of The Fall itself.

Such an interpretation does not automatically improve the music. An autobiographical grievance can provide interesting lyrical material without supplying an adequate musical arrangement. Indeed, one of the less attractive characteristics of the late Fall is the tendency for Smith's personal mythology to become a substitute for the imaginative transformation of experience that distinguished his greatest writing.

Nevertheless, the comparison between “Auto Chip” and “Over! Over!” reveals something important about the group's endurance. The Fall continued to investigate the possibilities of primitive repetition long after those possibilities had supposedly been exhausted. Sometimes the results were magnificent; sometimes they were tedious. The distinction was rarely predictable, and Smith's refusal to acknowledge it was both a source of artistic freedom and a considerable liability.

The machinery of The Fall was never designed to operate efficiently. Its components were replaced, damaged, repurposed and occasionally discarded without warning. The singer's voice deteriorated into an increasingly extraordinary collection of sounds, while the musicians attempted to construct arrangements capable of accommodating whatever emerged from it. What survived was not a stable musical identity but a method of generating unpredictable results from apparently inadequate materials.

That method produced an enormous quantity of difficult, ridiculous and occasionally terrible music. It also produced moments of astonishing beauty, humour and invention, often when the available ingredients suggested that nothing worthwhile could possibly emerge.

Perhaps the most revealing thing about Episode Eleven is that a discussion beginning with Tommy Cooper noises eventually arrives at questions of musical form, cultural authority, political paranoia and the relationship between artistic persistence and failure. These are not separate subjects imposed upon The Fall by overenthusiastic listeners. They are part of the extraordinary range of associations that the group's music continues to generate.

Smith's voice could sound like a malfunctioning machine, an angry publican, a supernatural messenger or a man attempting to sing a love song without admitting that he meant it. Sometimes it sounded like all four simultaneously. The Fall's achievement was to construct a musical world in which such contradictions were not merely tolerated but became the principal source of invention.

The machine was never supposed to work properly. Its most remarkable quality was that, against every reasonable expectation, it occasionally produced something beautiful.
