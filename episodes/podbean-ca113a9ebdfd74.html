A Beautiful M.E.S. or Just a Mess?

On the tyranny of Mark E. Smith, the genius of his musicians, and the dangerous business of listening to a record forty times

By Dr Algernon P. Scrunt

There is a particular kind of Fall fan who believes that everything Mark E. Smith recorded must contain something worth discovering. A dreadful B-side is merely misunderstood. An underdeveloped arrangement is an assault on conventional songwriting. A record that sounds as though somebody accidentally recorded over half of it is a radical interrogation of the recording process.

The problem is that this makes The Fall impossible to criticise. The worse the music, the more sophisticated the explanation required to defend it.

Yet the opposite position is equally inadequate. The Fall made some of the most extraordinary music in British rock precisely because they disregarded conventional distinctions between finished and unfinished, competent and incompetent, deliberate and accidental. Their records frequently resemble several musicians attempting different songs while an irritable landlord conducts an inspection of the premises.

Sometimes the results are magnificent.

The difficulty is determining when.

This question runs through the thirteenth episode of the F.A.L.L.O.U.T. podcast, whose guest, Visible Stu, approaches The Fall from an unusually healthy distance. He considers the group culturally indispensable while admitting that perhaps only one in ten of their songs genuinely excites him.

It is a distinction that dedicated fans might regard as heresy, but it establishes the evening's essential tension.

Importance is not the same thing as enjoyment.

Consider Flat of Angles, from 1980's Dragnet. Its constituent parts appear barely compatible: a sliding guitar figure, dub-inflected bass, restless drumming and wheezing keyboard interventions. Smith narrates a grubby crime story involving murder, paranoia and a retreat into a flat.

The song ought to collapse under its peculiarities. Instead, instability becomes its organising principle.

Stu hears something resembling the rhythmic elasticity of Burial: a beat that seems to slip out of alignment before snapping back into place. It's an unexpected comparison, but an illuminating one. Early Fall music is usually discussed through punk, garage rock and Krautrock. Its rhythmic strangeness can sometimes be understood more clearly through the electronic music that followed it.

Meanwhile, Phil takes issue with the description of the guitar as an irritating two-note slide riff. Having actually attempted to play it, he discovers an arpeggiated chord sliding into a scratched second shape.

A supposedly primitive recording turns out to contain more musical information than its rough surface suggests.

The same cannot quite be said of Psycho Mafia, although that is hardly an insult. Its overdriven drums, snarling guitars and furious forward momentum make it an exceptionally effective piece of punk rock. The discussion uncovers a more disturbing possible context for its gang mythology, involving psychiatric patients, exclusion and the streets around Prestwich.

Smith's peculiar gift was to populate ordinary rock music with people who appeared to have escaped from another literary universe.

But Psycho Mafia remains recognisably a punk song. Flat of Angles seems to be inventing its own rules as it proceeds.

The Fall are often most interesting not when they abandon musical conventions, but when they force familiar elements into unfamiliar relationships.

Unfortunately, the same freedom can produce considerably less rewarding results.

Zagreb arrives with an impressive conceptual pedigree: intimations of approaching conflict in Yugoslavia, sinister imagery and a supposedly ambitious three-part construction. Its lyrics promise political and psychological catastrophe. The music struggles to deliver the corresponding drama.

Ezra hears an attempted motorik rhythm deprived of combustion, accompanied by drums that suggest an unwelcome encounter with Phil Collins. Elsewhere, the conversation summons stadium rock, glam stomp, Stevie Wonder and U2: a remarkable collection of allegations against a single recording.

Stu, however, enjoys precisely what the others dislike. He likes hearing The Fall sound expansive rather than claustrophobic.

His disagreement matters. A polished drum sound is not inherently objectionable. Neither is a synthesiser, an understated vocal or a stadium-sized production.

The more persuasive criticism is that Zagreb seems underdeveloped. Intriguing musical ideas appear, disappear and fail to accumulate meaning. Its movements sound less like an ambitious composition than a collection of possibilities nobody bothered to pursue.

This is not productive disorder. It is indecision.

So-Called Dangerous presents a different problem. Its bassline, rhythm guitar and dance-rock production evoke the Happy Mondays so insistently that the question becomes whether Smith is imitating Manchester's fashionable musical vocabulary or mocking it.

Brendan proposes the more entertaining interpretation: Smith has assembled recognisable fragments of the contemporary scene into an elaborate demonstration of its limitations. The apparent imitation is actually an accusation.

It's a splendid theory. Unfortunately, it doesn't necessarily improve the record.

A mediocre groove does not become brilliant merely because its mediocrity might be intentional. The possibility that Smith is taking the piss cannot rescue everything he recorded.

Which brings us to Spinetrak.

Here the group sounds gloriously overstuffed. Guitars crash and scrape, a siren cuts through the arrangement, and Brix Smith supplies the melodic energy and vocal counterweight that help transform the recording into a deranged alternative-rock anthem.

Smith, characteristically, continues delivering his verse even after the chorus has begun. Rather than resolving the conflict, the record allows both performances to proceed.

Stu calls it a beautiful mess, then asks the evening's most uncomfortable question: how much of this apparent brilliance was carefully constructed, and how much was the inevitable consequence of a band recording an enormous quantity of material?

It's a question Fall devotees should ask more often.

Part of the answer is Brix. Her contribution is not decorative material applied to Smith's vision. Her musical instincts supply the structure against which his interruptions become exciting. Without that structure, much of the apparent spontaneity would simply become noise.

The Fall's genius, when it appears, is collective.

Then comes Levitate, and the entire argument threatens to disappear down a rabbit hole.

Most of the panel finds the recording unremarkable. Its minimal drums and repetitive guitar inspire comparisons with Oasis, while Stu hears precisely the sort of forgettable filler that makes The Fall's enormous catalogue so intimidating.

Brendan, however, has discovered that an exceptionally committed Fall enthusiast considers it his favourite song.

This changes everything.

Repeated listening begins. An elaborate narrative emerges involving debt, the Lake District, a possible meditation retreat and an invitation to transcendental enlightenment. What previously sounded like an inconsequential recording gradually becomes a miniature masterpiece of Smithian storytelling.

The defence eventually requires approximately forty listens.

There is something admirable about this persistence, and something magnificently absurd. Repetition can reveal musical relationships that initially escape us. It can also create an emotional investment in material that has done remarkably little to earn one.

The question is whether repeated listening uncovers the song or invents it.

At some point, a Fall fan must consider the possibility that Mark E. Smith has simply made a boring record.

The final pairing makes the question of authorship unavoidable.

Taurig, from Imperial Wax Solvent, is a brutalist electronic construction whose distorted synthesiser pulse and mysterious vocal fragments suggest an alternative Fall operating somewhere between electro-punk and science-fiction horror. Its electronic textures, shaped by Eleni Poulou and producer Andi Toma, are central to its appeal.

Stu asks the obvious question: how much of this brilliance belongs to Smith, and how much to the musicians surrounding him?

The answer should not be controversial. The musicians created the sounds. Smith helped establish the circumstances in which those sounds could become a Fall record.

That is an important form of authorship. It is not the only one.

Bury, meanwhile, demonstrates what happens when a remarkably tight late-period lineup encounters a singer whose voice sounds, as Ezra memorably suggests, like a sack of cats drowning in a tar pit. Its glam-rock stomp, abrasive keyboards and angular guitars produce a thrilling collision of precision and ugliness.

Even here, Smith cannot resist interference. The album version begins beneath a deliberately filthy layer of compression before bursting into clarity. Some hear an inspired production trick. Others resent having to endure nearly two minutes of sonic obstruction before the song properly arrives.

Both positions are defensible.

And that is the point. The Fall were neither an inexhaustible fountain of genius nor a shambolic enterprise occasionally redeemed by luck. They were a succession of resourceful musicians working with a leader whose instincts could be inspired, obstructive, arbitrary and catastrophic.

Smith's greatest achievement was not making every Fall record brilliant. It was sustaining an environment in which brilliance remained possible long after most groups had exhausted their ideas.

But possibility is not achievement. Freedom is not an excuse. And an unfinished song does not become a masterpiece because its author refused to finish it.

Sometimes the beautiful mess is beautiful.

Sometimes it is simply a mess.

The interesting thing about The Fall is that you can spend forty listens trying to decide which.
