THE GHOST IN THE MACHINE HAS A SHAKIN’ STEVENS RECORD

On occult arithmetic, Northern Soul, industrial repetition and the strange pleasure of hearing The Fall fail to behave themselves.

By Dr. M. Hexen, Institute of Hostile Aesthetics

The number twenty-three has had a surprisingly distinguished career in the field of people seeing things that are not there. William S. Burroughs collected coincidences involving it; Robert Anton Wilson helped popularise the resulting “23 enigma”; and an entire subculture developed the reassuring belief that the universe was sending coded messages, rather than simply demonstrating that people are very good at counting the things they have already decided to count. It is an ideal doorway into The Fall, whose recordings can make an ordinary bass note sound like a message from a hostile civilisation.

Consider the curious proximity of “Ludd Gang” and “I Feel Voxish”, two artefacts from the *Perverted by Language* period. They share a musical vocabulary of high-register bass, hard rhythmic edges and Craig Scanlon’s angular, sometimes chiming guitar. Yet they do not sound interchangeable. “Ludd Gang” seems to construct a machine out of melodic fragments, its insistent bass figure both catchy and faintly persecutory. “I Feel Voxish” distributes its hooks unevenly: a guitar gesture, a drum interruption, a vocal outburst, a sudden change of weight. It resembles a piece of equipment whose individual parts have developed conflicting political convictions.

That is the essential Fall trick. Repetition is not merely repetition. It is a mechanism for making small differences disproportionately significant. The German *motorik* tradition offers an obvious comparison, but the Fall's machine is less well maintained than Neu!’s. Its wheels wobble. Someone has replaced the instruction manual with an insult. The Joy Division comparison is useful too, particularly the melodic prominence of bass guitar, but where Peter Hook’s high-register lines often create melancholy grandeur, Steve Hanley’s can suggest a persistent nuisance that has acquired architectural status.

The literary machinery is equally eccentric. The Luddite reference invokes the nineteenth-century machine breakers, although Ned Ludd himself is a figure of contested historicity, a convenient name around which collective resistance could gather. Smith’s language makes this sort of instability feel entirely natural: an invented or exaggerated figure becomes real by being repeatedly invoked. It is folklore produced by amplification.

The same song-world can accommodate a far less respectable visitor: H. P. Lovecraft. The language surrounding “I Feel Voxish” includes an echo of “The Picture in the House”, Lovecraft’s 1920 tale of an antiquated dwelling, an elderly host and a terrible appetite. The relevant horror is not a gigantic tentacled deity. It is the discovery that an apparently ordinary domestic space contains an appetite incompatible with civilised life. Smith’s bathroom knife-sharpening, mould and bodily disgust occupy a similar imaginative register. The everyday room becomes a place where the social contract has quietly expired.

There is a productive distinction here between Smith and the Gothic tradition. Lovecraft’s prose frequently insists upon the unspeakable; Smith generally appears delighted to speak, preferably over somebody else. The horror is not beyond language. It is language behaving like a badly supervised animal.

Then comes the problem of the cover version. R. Dean Taylor’s “There’s a Ghost in My House” entered British pop consciousness through Northern Soul culture, where forgotten American records acquired extraordinary new lives in clubs and dance halls. The original's combination of heartbreak and supernatural imagery is almost absurdly efficient. Its ghost is a memory of love that refuses to depart, the familiar pop metaphor made physical by a propulsive beat. The Fall's version is strikingly faithful to that structure. For a band famous for dragging other people's material into strange new jurisdictions, the relative lack of interference is almost suspicious.

Yet faithfulness can itself be a form of perversity. Smith’s singing sits awkwardly inside the bright soul architecture. Rather than a polished confession of romantic torment, it becomes the testimony of a man irritated that the haunting has not been dealt with by the relevant department. The song’s famous video, with its domestic locations and peculiar visual comedy, adds an entire second argument. Its effect depends less on narrative explanation than on the collision of pop performance, household banality and a distinctly unglamorous sense of place.

The comparison with “Sinister Waltz” is revealing, even though the surviving transcript becomes severely corrupted around the point where that recording is introduced. A waltz ordinarily promises grace, social order and bodies moving in an agreed pattern. Add “sinister” and the promise becomes a threat. This is the central Fall opposition: dance music as collective pleasure, dance music as coercive ritual. A beat can liberate you, but it can also demand obedience.

That tension connects the seemingly disparate later pieces in the programme. “Cyber Insekt” belongs to a period when electronics, rock instrumentation and Smith's increasingly peculiar vocal presence collide in unstable combinations. Its title alone compresses two of his enduring preoccupations: the inhuman organisation of the technological world and the unpleasant persistence of small living things. The insect is an excellent Fall emblem. It is industrious, repetitive, difficult to reason with and apparently untroubled by aesthetic judgement.

The electronic Fall never quite resembles a group adopting fashionable technology for the sake of modernity. Instead, machines are introduced into a pre-existing atmosphere of irritation. The result can be closer to an overheated office than a futuristic nightclub. J. G. Ballard understood this perfectly: the future does not always arrive as a shining new world. Sometimes it arrives as the same old neurosis with improved equipment.

The title “Chilinist” suggests another Fall speciality, the half-recognisable word that seems to belong to a system of classification nobody has explained. Smith’s neologisms are not simply nonsense. They create the impression of a wider vocabulary whose rules are just beyond reach. Lewis Carroll used a similar device in “Jabberwocky”, where unfamiliar words become legible through grammatical position and atmosphere. Smith often reverses the process: recognisable words become less intelligible because of the company they keep.

A related disturbance operates in the seasonal pair “Can Can Summer” and “The Past #2”. The can-can suggests exuberance, theatrical display and the body refusing decorum. The past suggests the opposite: memory, recurrence and the impossibility of escaping what has already happened. The Fall’s discography is full of this collision between forward motion and backward haunting. A new line-up, new studio, new beat or new decade never entirely removes the old materials. They reappear as echoes, mutations, bad memories and occasionally glorious inventions.

The French philosopher Henri Bergson distinguished mechanical clock time from lived duration. The Fall's best repetitions seem to occupy both. They proceed according to a rigid pulse while changing the listener’s experience of how long a minute lasts. A repeated phrase may become exhilarating, intolerable or unexpectedly funny without undergoing any obvious alteration. The record has not changed; the person listening to it has.

This is why the familiar accusation that The Fall made the same record repeatedly is both wrong and strangely perceptive. They certainly returned to the same procedures. So did Samuel Beckett, whose characters circle familiar predicaments while extracting new varieties of failure from them. The question is whether repetition exhausts meaning or manufactures it. In The Fall’s case the answer is usually yes.

The catalogue is consequently not a tidy progression from primitive beginnings to sophisticated maturity, nor a straightforward tale of decline. Its innovations and embarrassments are scattered across the decades. A jagged B-side can contain more invention than an album's supposed centrepiece; a cover can be exhilarating because of its fidelity and disappointing for exactly the same reason. The songs resist being arranged into a sensible moral hierarchy.

Which brings us back to the number twenty-three. The temptation to identify a hidden system is irresistible, especially when the evidence consists of a band that has spent decades producing contradictory clues. Burroughs would recognise the appeal. Wilson would probably add another coincidence. Smith would be more likely to tell both of them to get out of the way of the bass amplifier.

The real mystery is not whether a ghost inhabits the house, a secret message lurks in the number, or a mechanical rhythm conceals the political history of Europe. It is why these apparently incompatible ingredients can form a recognisable artistic identity. The answer may be that The Fall were less a musical style than a method of contamination. Whatever entered their orbit emerged sounding as though it had acquired a grievance.

The ghost is in the house. The insect is in the circuitry. The knife is being sharpened in the bathroom. The bass note is still going. And somewhere, in the midst of it all, somebody has put on a Shakin’ Stevens record and made the situation immeasurably worse.
