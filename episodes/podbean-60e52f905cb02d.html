THE GHOST IN THE GROOVE

From Prestwich to Kyoto: The Fall, O-Manche and the afterlife of repetition.

By R. P. Kicker, Correspondent for Unexplained Rhythms

There is a wonderful moment in “Psykick Dancehall” when Mark E. Smith imagines his own vibrations continuing after death, passing through the years while people dance to them. It is an unusually generous vision of posterity from a man who could regard the continued existence of his own band members as an impertinence. The dead remain among us, not as spirits in white sheets but as disturbances in the air. A record becomes a kind of haunting. The musicians may disappear, the buildings may be demolished, the people who bought the records may grow old and develop opinions about conservatories, but somewhere a needle drops and the whole business starts again.

The song appeared on Dragnet in 1979, a record whose production still sounds as though it might have been carried out inside a condemned building. Its two guitars move independently, one chopping away at the rhythm while the other produces those peculiar bent, dissonant figures that seem to exist somewhere between a musical phrase and a technical fault. The drums are remarkably straightforward. Their job is to keep the song moving while everything else threatens to come loose. Smith delivers his account of psychic disturbances with the authority of somebody who has received confidential information from a spiritualist and is already considering whether to sack them. There is nothing especially elaborate about the arrangement, but the interaction between its components is astonishingly sophisticated. It is post-punk not as a genre but as a method of making instruments behave improperly.

The story behind the song is suitably unreliable. One account places its origins in a supposedly haunted dance school in Prestwich; another involves a disco above a spiritualist church, a much more convincing location for supernatural activity in late-seventies Lancashire. The competing stories are almost too appropriate. Smith was fascinated by the occult but rarely interested in providing the reassuring explanations that usually accompany it. His references to the medium Helen Duncan, whose wartime prosecution became a celebrated episode in British spiritualist history, place the song within a peculiarly British tradition of amateur paranormal investigation, provincial eccentricity and institutional suspicion. It is the world of séances above shops, not the spectacular supernatural machinery of Hollywood. The music has the same quality. Nothing announces itself as frightening. The guitars simply refuse to behave as expected.

Yet the most remarkable thing about “Psykick Dancehall” is its confidence that music can outlive the people who make it. This is not merely an attractive lyrical conceit. It describes precisely what happens to The Fall's records as they travel beyond their original circumstances. In late-seventies Kyoto, British punk was available principally through the more established names: the Sex Pistols, the Clash, Buzzcocks, perhaps Wire. A comparatively obscure group recording for Step Forward was another proposition entirely. Dragnet might have existed in Manchester in 1979, but encountering it in Japan could involve a delay of years. The physical record had to make the journey before its vibrations could do anything at all.

That geographical distance offers a useful correction to the idea that The Fall were simply a Manchester band responding to Manchester events. Their records travelled through entirely different musical cultures, acquiring meanings that could not have been anticipated in Prestwich. For musicians working in Japanese underground rock, the distinction between punk, garage, psychedelia, progressive rock and noise was not necessarily arranged according to the same British tribal boundaries. Zeni Geva's ferocious precision and Acid Mothers Temple's sprawling psychedelic excess might appear to occupy opposite ends of a musical spectrum, but both understand the extraordinary possibilities of sustained repetition. A riff can be a framework for collective discipline or a means of dismantling it. The Fall, frequently doing both at once, belong somewhere in the middle.

There is an important distinction, however, between hearing an affinity and claiming an influence. Repetition is not an exclusively Fall invention, and a Japanese guitarist capable of producing twenty minutes of overwhelming psychedelic guitar does not necessarily owe anything to Mark E. Smith. The relevant tradition is much wider, taking in the motorik persistence of Neu!, the collective improvisations of Can, American garage rock and the obstinate minimalism of numerous musicians who discovered that playing the same thing repeatedly could become more interesting than changing it. The Fall's particular contribution was to introduce a permanent element of human disagreement. Their repetitions were rarely perfectly symmetrical. Somebody was always leaning against the beat, introducing an unnecessary chord or making a noise that threatened to upset the arrangement.

This explains why “Pilsner Trail”, an outtake associated with the Perverted by Language period, is both recognisably characteristic and oddly unsatisfying. Its two-chord guitar figure, skipping rhythm and delayed vocal possess all the necessary ingredients of the early-eighties group. There are hints of the angular minimalism associated with ESG and the stubborn garage-rock propulsion of Room to Live. Yet the song never quite achieves the strange internal movement that makes the best Fall repetitions so compelling. An unusually extended guitar solo provides variation without substantially changing the argument. The track goes on, certainly, but continuation is not the same thing as development. “Psykick Dancehall” creates the impression that something mysterious is happening inside its repetitive structure. “Pilsner Trail” sometimes sounds like the musicians are waiting for something to happen outside it.

That distinction becomes more complicated when the music moves into the early nineties. The arrival of Madchester and the growing influence of dance production presented The Fall with an apparently straightforward proposition: here was a musical culture built around repetition, mechanical rhythms and the pleasures of extended grooves. Much of what the group had been doing for years suddenly appeared fashionable. Yet their attempts to accommodate these developments could be surprisingly uncomfortable. The electronic arrangements of Shift-Work and Code: Selfish sometimes replaced the instability of the old rhythm section with something smoother, more conventionally inviting. What had once sounded like a group arguing its way through a piece of music could resemble a group arriving at a perfectly reasonable agreement.

In Japan, Madchester acquired the wonderfully economical nickname O-Manche. The term manages to make an entire British cultural phenomenon sound like either a specialist noodle dish or a minor medical complaint. More importantly, it demonstrates how thoroughly the music had become detached from its original geography. Happy Mondays, the Stone Roses and the broader Manchester dance-rock phenomenon were no longer simply local products of clubs, record shops and regional rivalries. They were a recognisable international style. “The Mixer”, from Shift-Work, fits comfortably into that category, although its pleasures are rather more peculiar than the label suggests.

The song is built around a surprisingly delicate arrangement of electronic percussion, castanet-like clattering, handclaps, a smooth bass line and violin. Its keyboards have the slightly inexpensive charm of equipment that might have been purchased from a catalogue, while Smith adopts a reflective delivery that would not sound entirely impossible coming from Tim Booth of James. The resemblance is not wholly flattering, but neither is it automatically disqualifying. There is an appealing softness to the performance, and the violin gives the arrangement a fragile melodic quality that might easily have been lost beneath more aggressive guitars. The handclaps, meanwhile, seem to have been ordered in industrial quantities. One imagines an engineer being instructed to add a few more, then a few more again, until the studio resembles the audience of a particularly enthusiastic daytime television programme.

Robert Gordon's association with the recording is particularly interesting. His work with Forgemasters placed him close to the beginnings of Sheffield's bleep techno movement, a style that reduced dance music to sharply defined electronic signals, cavernous bass and rigorous rhythmic structures. “The Mixer” is hardly a bleep-techno record, but its arrangement benefits from the same attention to the placement of small electronic sounds. The individual components are modest; their relationship is the attraction. There is even something touching about Smith's apparent affection for the recording apparatus itself. A man whose lyrics routinely transform ordinary objects into suspicious or hostile presences has discovered a mixing desk with feelings.

“Immortality”, from Code: Selfish, offers a less convincing version of the same electronic accommodation. Its bass figure is almost aggressively elementary, the sort of thing a beginner might produce while waiting for somebody to explain where the other notes are. Yet buried within the arrangement is a genuinely effective vocal hook, a moment when Smith's melodic instincts briefly rescue the proceedings from their own predictability. Around it, however, the track accumulates cheerful piano figures and familiar dance-rock gestures that can make the whole enterprise resemble an unsuccessful attempt to manufacture another Charlatans single. The problem is not that the music has become too commercial. It is that the arrangement appears insufficiently interested in its own best idea.

The Fall's relationship with novelty records provides another route into this problem. Smith was an unusually enthusiastic collector of musical rubbish, although rubbish in this context often meant material that conventional taste had failed to recognise as interesting. Garage singles, comic recordings, strange spoken-word performances and forgotten commercial curiosities supplied him with an alternative history of popular music. “I'm a Mummy”, originally recorded by Bob McFadden and Dor in 1959, belongs firmly to that tradition. Its horror-comedy premise, mock-Egyptian atmosphere and ridiculous vocal exchanges are exactly the sort of material that might have appeared on a compilation of obscure American novelty records.

The Fall's version, recorded for Levitate, strips much of the original's theatrical apparatus away and substitutes a wiry garage-rock arrangement driven by an exceptionally lively drum performance. Smith's variations on the mummy's declarations are entertaining, although the backing vocal occasionally becomes an irritant rather than an effective comic counterpoint. What makes the recording interesting is the tension between the original's carefully constructed joke and The Fall's apparent indifference to presenting it properly. A novelty song usually requires its performers to understand the joke and deliver it with precision. Smith seems more interested in inhabiting the character, a misunderstood monster who has returned to discover that everybody finds him alarming. There are worse descriptions of his public reputation.

But the group's greatest comic performances rarely depend upon a conventional joke. They arise when Smith discovers something intrinsically absurd about ordinary language and refuses to explain why it matters. “Das Katerer”, from Levitate, is a magnificent example. Its electronic arrangement is sparse, industrial and oddly funky, with percussion and sampled sounds assembled into a rhythm that recalls the mechanical propulsion of “Free Range”. There is little conventional guitar activity to distract from the central performance. Smith assumes the role of a caterer, offering food, reassurance and a selection of statements whose relationship to the hospitality industry becomes increasingly difficult to determine. The repeated references to chicken and chips are delivered with such conviction that the distinction between avant-garde performance and a man reading a takeaway menu becomes practically meaningless.

It is tempting to describe this as surrealism, but that risks making the performance sound more calculated than it is. Smith's method has more in common with the peculiar comedy of Frank Sidebottom or the most disorientating passages of The Fast Show, where an apparently ordinary character becomes fascinating through repetition, misplaced emphasis and the refusal to acknowledge anything unusual. “Das Katerer” works because the music takes the absurdity completely seriously. Its industrial beat continues without commenting on the vocal, while the samples create a strange, slightly oppressive environment around what ought to be a reassuring offer of dinner. The more carefully one examines the arrangement, the more ridiculous the words become. The more closely one attends to the words, the stranger the music sounds.

There is also a connection to the group's earlier electronic experiments. “Free Range” had demonstrated that mechanical rhythms could accommodate Smith's fragmented delivery without sacrificing momentum. “Das Katerer” takes a related approach but removes the apparent necessity of producing a conventional song. The voice becomes another sample, repeating and mutating phrases while the instrumental backing maintains its own logic. This is why the track's comedy survives repeated listening. The joke is not simply that Smith is singing about catering. It is that the recording creates a complete musical world in which catering has acquired the urgency of a political emergency.

The late-period Fall present a different kind of problem. By the time of Ersatz GB and Re-Mit, the group had developed an exceptionally disciplined instrumental identity. Pete Greenway's guitar, David Spurr's bass and Keiron Melling's drums could produce a formidable, tightly organised racket, while Elena Poulou's keyboards supplied melodic interruptions and electronic colour. “Sir William Wray” demonstrates the strengths of this arrangement. Its guitar riff is direct, the rhythm section locks together almost immediately, and the keyboard moves through the track with an agility that prevents the music from becoming entirely monochromatic. Smith's vocal, meanwhile, has deteriorated into a fascinating collection of gargles, barks and partially intelligible declarations. He sounds at times like Rowley Birkin QC attempting to deliver a lecture on amphibious aristocracy.

Yet there is something troubling about a Fall performance in which everybody plays their part so efficiently. The instrumental arrangement is impressively tight, but its very competence can reduce the sense of possibility. Greenway's guitar occasionally falls back upon familiar figures, and the track threatens to become an example of the band reproducing its established strengths rather than discovering anything unexpected. “Cosmos 7”, by contrast, is far less comfortable. Its frantic drums and rounded bass possess a physical intensity that recalls the Jesus Lizard, particularly the extraordinary relationship between David Wm. Sims and Mac McNeilly. The guitar is not always equally inventive, sometimes duplicating the bass rather than creating a productive disagreement with it, but Smith's vocal performance is extraordinary.

By this point his words are frequently almost impossible to decipher. Phrases concerning mythical European lifestyles and reptilian identities emerge from the noise, although attempting to establish a definitive lyric sheet would probably be an excellent way to waste an entire weekend. What matters is the sound of the voice itself. Smith has become a kind of human electronic disturbance, producing rhythms, textures and violent interruptions that exist alongside the instruments rather than simply describing something over them. The song's imperfections become part of its appeal. Its drumming is thrilling, its guitar occasionally unimaginative, its vocal frequently incomprehensible, and the entire recording possesses an urgency that the more efficiently assembled “Sir William Wray” sometimes lacks.

This brings us back to the question raised by “Psykick Dancehall”. What survives when a musician disappears? The answer cannot simply be the records, because records are not inert historical documents. They change according to who hears them, where they are heard and what other music surrounds them. A Fall record encountered in Kyoto a decade after its release is not quite the same cultural object as one purchased in Manchester in 1979. Nor is a piece of late-period electronic Fall necessarily understood in the same way by somebody raised on British indie music and somebody whose musical experience includes Zeni Geva, Acid Mothers Temple and the Japanese psychedelic underground.

The extraordinary thing about The Fall is that these different interpretations can coexist without resolving into a single account of what the group was supposed to be. Their best music is sufficiently specific to remain recognisable and sufficiently unstable to resist being pinned down. “Psykick Dancehall” still sounds mysterious, “The Mixer” can be both embarrassingly polite and unexpectedly beautiful, and “Das Katerer” remains an astonishing piece of electronic comedy whose central proposition appears to involve the provision of chicken and chips. The vibrations have indeed lived on. They have travelled halfway around the world, acquired new names, survived the musicians who produced them and continued to disturb people who were nowhere near Prestwich when the trouble began.
