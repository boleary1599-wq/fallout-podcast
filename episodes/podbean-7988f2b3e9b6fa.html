THE FALL: THE SOUND OF A BAND BEING PROFESSIONALLY MISUNDERSTOOD (AKA THE OTHER DAVE BUSH TAPES)

From Seminal Live to Code: Selfish, The Fall entered the 1990s with better equipment, bigger ambitions and the growing suspicion that somebody had tidied up the wrong room.

By Dr. Cyril V. Remainder, Visiting Professor of Applied Discontent

I. The Last Days of the Old Regime

There is something particularly depressing about a band fulfilling its contractual obligations. It suggests the completion of a tax return, the surrender of a company car, or a final appearance at a regional shopping centre before the administrators arrive. In 1989, The Fall released Seminal Live, an album whose title promised historical importance while its contents suggested that somebody had been rummaging through a filing cabinet marked MISCELLANEOUS.

Half studio recordings, half live performances, it was the group's final album for Beggars Banquet. It is difficult to imagine a more appropriate farewell. Mark E. Smith had spent the previous decade treating the music industry as an elaborate confidence trick perpetrated by men in expensive trousers. Now his own band appeared to be submitting the necessary paperwork.

The studio material has an exhausted, provisional quality. “Dead Beat Descendant” rattles along with considerable determination, while “H.O.W.” possesses the sort of unpleasant vitality that makes you suspect Smith has discovered a new method of transmitting diseases through consonants. Elsewhere, inspiration seems to be arriving by second-class post.

The live material tells a different story. “Pay Your Rates” and “2 x 4” demonstrate a group capable of extraordinary collective violence, even when the studio recordings suggest creative depletion. Steve Hanley's bass remains a magnificent instrument of coercion, Craig Scanlon's guitar cuts across the rhythm rather than decorating it, and Simon Wolstencroft supplies the sort of drumming that might accompany a particularly determined industrial dispute.

The contrast exposes one of The Fall's enduring paradoxes. Their records could be chaotic, incomplete or wilfully unpleasant, yet the band itself was frequently an exceptionally disciplined musical organism. What sounded like disorder was often the product of years of accumulated understanding.

Brix Smith and Marcia Schofield were also approaching the end of their respective tenures. Their departure would remove much of the group's melodic and keyboard colour. Yet the most remarkable thing about The Fall was their capacity to survive precisely the circumstances that would have destroyed an ordinary band.

The old regime was collapsing. Unfortunately for everyone concerned, the new one was about to discover a recording budget.

II. Extricate: The Luxury of Being Difficult

Extricate arrived in 1990 on Fontana, bringing with it the unmistakable atmosphere of a group that had been allowed into a considerably nicer studio.

Produced with Craig Leon, the album possesses a clarity almost indecent by Fall standards. Instruments occupy recognisable positions. The bass has depth. Percussion sounds deliberate. One can occasionally identify what Mark E. Smith is saying, an alarming development that should perhaps have prompted an investigation.

Yet the album is anything but straightforward.

“Telephone Thing”, involving Coldcut, introduces a loping, electronically inflected groove that places Smith inside a contemporary dance-music environment without making him sound remotely contemporary. He approaches the rhythm like an irritable caller who has been transferred between departments for forty-seven minutes. His repeated demands and suspicious observations turn the telephone into an instrument of bureaucratic persecution.

It is an ideal Fall subject. Technology promises communication but produces surveillance, confusion and hostility. Marshall McLuhan might have recognised the medium becoming the message; Smith would have wanted to know who was paying the bill.

“Bill Is Dead” presents an even stranger transformation. Its almost stately melody allows Smith to approach conventional singing, producing a performance that is simultaneously tender, exhausted and vaguely menacing. The voice suggests a man remembering happiness from behind the counter of a closed betting shop.

The song's emotional force comes partly from its refusal to behave like a normal ballad. Smith does not become vulnerable in the approved manner. There is no grand confession, no cleansing climax, no reassuring evidence of personal growth. Instead, the music creates a space in which ordinary melancholy can exist without being explained.

Elsewhere, “I'm Frank” introduces a wonderfully awkward relationship between guitar attack and pop accessibility. “Black Monk Theme Part I” and “Black Monk Theme Part II” draw on the American garage-rock group the Monks, whose combination of repetition, provocation and rhythmic severity made them natural ancestors of The Fall.

Martin Bramah's return contributes to the album's renewed guitar character. His presence reconnects the group with its beginnings without producing a nostalgic recreation of 1979.

This is the crucial distinction. Extricate does not represent The Fall becoming respectable. It represents their discovery that respectable recording techniques could be used to manufacture thoroughly disreputable music.

The production gives the songs room to breathe, but what emerges from that space is frequently odder than anything buried beneath the earlier distortion.

The result resembles a Ken Loach film photographed by Stanley Kubrick: immaculate compositions containing people who remain profoundly suspicious of the entire enterprise.

III. The Great Manchester Misunderstanding

The geographical circumstances were unfortunate.

By 1990, Manchester had become an international cultural commodity. The Stone Roses, Happy Mondays and the Hacienda provided a marketable combination of guitars, dance rhythms, recreational pharmaceuticals and clothing that looked as though it had been selected during a power cut.

The Fall, meanwhile, had been producing repetitive, rhythmically obsessive music in Manchester for more than a decade. They had explored funk, electronics, dub, rockabilly and strange forms of disco long before anybody thought to describe the results as a movement.

Nevertheless, the arrival of dance influences in their early-1990s recordings created the irresistible possibility of categorising them alongside the city's fashionable younger groups.

This was rather like discovering that Samuel Beckett occasionally visited a pub and announcing that he was therefore part of the contemporary darts scene.

The distinction is musical as much as ideological.

Where Madchester frequently celebrated collective release, The Fall tended to transform repetition into confinement. The bassline was not necessarily an invitation to dance; it might equally represent an administrative procedure from which there was no escape.

“Telephone Thing” works because its groove never resolves Smith's paranoia. The rhythm remains attractive, even seductive, while the vocal actively undermines any possibility of uncomplicated pleasure.

By Code: Selfish, however, the electronic component had become much more pronounced. Dave Bush's keyboards and programming helped construct a sound in which sequenced patterns and synthetic textures occupied territory previously dominated by guitar abrasion.

“Free Range” is the outstanding example. Its relentless electronic propulsion creates a physical excitement that is unmistakably connected to contemporary dance music, yet Smith's apocalyptic language refuses the optimistic assumptions of rave culture.

The track sounds less like an invitation to a warehouse party than a warning that the warehouse has already been requisitioned by an international conspiracy.

Here The Fall achieve something remarkable: they absorb the musical vocabulary of their surroundings without adopting its emotional priorities.

The beat may belong to 1992. The worldview belongs to a man who suspects the year itself is operating under false pretences.

IV. Shift-Work and the Terrible Prospect of Competence

If Extricate suggested new possibilities, Shift-Work in 1991 demonstrated the dangers of having too many.

The album is often described as restrained, melodic or mature. These are dangerous adjectives when applied to The Fall. Maturity, in rock criticism, frequently means that a band has stopped doing the things that made it interesting and started purchasing tasteful furniture.

There is certainly an unusual smoothness to Shift-Work. The guitars are less abrasive, the arrangements more controlled, and Smith's vocal performances occasionally approach something resembling conventional tunefulness.

“Edinburgh Man” possesses a melancholy directness that would have seemed almost unimaginable during the group's earlier confrontational years. “Rose” and “The War Against Intelligence” explore different degrees of melodic accessibility, while “Idiot Joy Showland” demonstrates that Smith's appetite for attacking the entertainment industry remains reassuringly intact.

Yet the album's problem is not simply that it is too polished.

The Fall had made polished music before. The Frenz Experiment contained moments of considerable accessibility, while The Wonderful and Frightening World of The Fall had already demonstrated that production sophistication could coexist with grotesque invention.

The difficulty lies in the relationship between the performances and their presentation.

Wolstencroft's drumming, so essential to the group's physical identity, can sound oddly constrained. The snare occasionally possesses the brittle, flattened character of a drum machine, while the arrangements lack the sense of competing musical personalities that animated earlier records.

The result resembles an office in which every employee has attended the same compulsory training seminar.

Nobody is doing anything particularly wrong. That is precisely the problem.

There is also a curious relationship between Shift-Work and Smith's increasingly melodic vocal approach. His willingness to sing rather than declaim can be fascinating, but the effect depends upon the music resisting him. When the arrangements become equally accommodating, the productive friction diminishes.

The Fall were never simply a vehicle for Smith's personality. Their greatest recordings depended upon the collision between his language and the musicians' independent rhythmic intelligence.

Remove too much of that resistance and the songs risk becoming illustrations of the singer rather than arguments with him.

Shift-Work is not a disaster. It is something more unsettling: an album in which The Fall occasionally sound comfortable.

V. Code: Selfish and the Mechanical Nervous Breakdown

By 1992, Code: Selfish pushed the electronic transformation further.

The album's production is frequently criticised for its clinical character, yet that coldness is not necessarily incompatible with The Fall's aesthetic. Repetition, mechanisation and emotional estrangement had been central to the group's music since the beginning.

The question is whether the machines intensify the music or merely occupy it.

“Free Range” answers decisively in favour of the former. Its programmed momentum, driving bass and abrasive vocal delivery produce one of the group's most convincing marriages of technology and hostility.

“Everything Hurtz” is similarly effective, building an atmosphere of persistent discomfort from mechanical rhythms and clipped musical gestures. The title itself might serve as a concise diagnosis of the British economy during the early 1990s.

Elsewhere, the results are less convincing.

The electronic textures sometimes overwhelm the distinctive interplay between Hanley's bass, Scanlon's guitar and Wolstencroft's drums. Instead of a collection of musicians generating tension through repetition, one hears a carefully assembled arrangement attempting to simulate the same effect.

This is not a complaint about technology. Kraftwerk, Cabaret Voltaire and the more rhythmically severe recordings of Can had already demonstrated that machines could generate enormous psychological pressure.

The distinction lies in how the elements interact.

The Fall's earlier repetition was rarely static. Tiny alterations in emphasis, timing and instrumental attack produced a sensation of perpetual instability. The songs seemed capable of continuing indefinitely, but never quite in the same form.

On parts of Code: Selfish, repetition becomes more predictable. The machinery is impressively assembled, yet the listener can sometimes anticipate its next movement.

“Free Range” succeeds because it transforms that predictability into menace. The rhythm advances with bureaucratic certainty while Smith supplies the impression of an increasingly unreliable witness to a global catastrophe.

The album's most revealing moments occur when this mechanised discipline collides with something unmistakably human: a stray guitar phrase, an awkward vocal intervention, or a bassline refusing to behave like a programmed accessory.

It is the sound of a band negotiating with its own replacement.

VI. The Pub, the Factory and the End of the World

The historical setting matters.

Between 1989 and 1992, Britain moved from the final years of Thatcherism into the Major government, recession and the increasingly corporate presentation of popular culture. Independent music was becoming an industry with its own professional infrastructure, promotional expectations and marketable regional identities.

The Fall's movement towards cleaner production and electronic arrangements can be heard against this background without reducing the records to economic allegory.

Smith's imagination remained stubbornly attached to an older Britain: pubs without televisions, provincial resentments, workplace grievances, obscure historical references and the peculiar dignity of people who had no intention of becoming interesting to anybody else.

His ideal public house was not a lifestyle destination. It was somewhere to sit with a drink and avoid being entertained.

There is something almost religious about this preference. The traditional pub becomes a secular monastery, offering solitude, repetition and the possibility of contemplating humanity's shortcomings without somebody playing a promotional video above the fruit machine.

This helps explain the peculiar tension in The Fall's early-1990s music.

The records increasingly employ the language of modernity: sophisticated production, electronics, sequencers, dance rhythms and commercially viable arrangements. Yet the voice at their centre remains profoundly hostile to the social world these developments supposedly represent.

Smith is not rejecting technology. He is refusing its promise of improvement.

Walter Benjamin's angel of history, blown backwards into the future while surveying accumulating wreckage, might have recognised something of himself in these recordings. The machinery accelerates, the surfaces become cleaner, and the catastrophe remains stubbornly unresolved.

What makes the period compelling is not a simple narrative of decline. Extricate contains some of the group's most imaginative music, while Code: Selfish produces moments of extraordinary electronic aggression. Even Shift-Work, for all its limitations, reveals possibilities that earlier versions of the band could scarcely have contemplated.

The problem was never that The Fall became modern.

It was that modernity occasionally made them sound ordinary.

And for a group whose greatest achievement was turning ordinary British existence into a bewildering system of conspiracies, threats, jokes and inexplicable personal grievances, that was the one indignity they could not afford.

The machines could stay. The professionalism was negotiable. The unpleasantness remained absolutely essential.
