Sodomised by Presumption

Garage-rock purity, counterfeit revelations and the difficult business of remaining The Fall.

By Neville Counterpane

I. Before the Genre Existed

The Sonics enjoyed one considerable advantage over the thousands of garage-rock groups that followed them: nobody had told them they were a garage-rock group.

There was no established aesthetic to reproduce, no retrospective compilation to consult, no obligation to sound as though their records had been discovered beneath a pile of agricultural machinery in Washington State. They were simply making an extraordinary racket.

The opening assault of ‘The Witch’ remains remarkable because its constituent parts are so familiar. Gerry Roslie's Little Richard-derived screams, the punctuated guitar figures, the saxophone and the emphatic percussion belong to an established musical vocabulary. The innovation lies in the conviction with which these elements are combined. The guitars sound simultaneously clean and filthy. The performances are disciplined without being polite.

The famous economy of their recording arrangements, particularly the single microphone capturing the drum kit, raises an interesting question about the relationship between technological sophistication and musical effectiveness. The Sonics understood, whether by instinct or necessity, that the sound of somebody hitting a drum kit was already a sufficiently interesting proposition.

The Fall would spend much of their existence negotiating precisely this distinction. The disappointing production of Live at the Witch Trials, the oppressive bottom end of ‘Garden’ and the strangely domesticated violence of ‘Quit iPhone’ demonstrate three very different relationships between musical performance and its recording.

The Sonics subsequently became part of a historical narrative encompassing the Stooges, the MC5, punk and garage revivalists such as the Mummies. But historical narratives have a dangerous tendency to transform discoveries into instructions.

An instinctive expression of energy becomes a genre. A genre becomes a collection of approved gestures. Eventually, a musician purchases equipment specifically to reproduce the sound of somebody who could not afford that equipment.

The Fall were at their best when they absorbed the possibilities of such music rather than its rules. At their worst, they became another group demonstrating its familiarity with the Stooges.

There is a considerable difference between inheriting an attitude and inheriting a riff.

II. The Machinery of Revelation

There is a particular kind of profundity available to somebody who refuses to finish explaining themselves. Mark E. Smith understood its possibilities better than almost anybody.

‘Garden’ and ‘Oleano’, separated by thirteen years, are extraordinary examples of narrative construction through the withholding of information. Both appear to concern events of tremendous significance. Neither provides sufficient information to establish what has happened.

In ‘Garden’, the announcement of a divine presence is compromised by the mundane architectural circumstances of its arrival. The prophet is apparently on the second floor. There is a lift shaft to consider. We have entered a cosmology whose supernatural inhabitants must negotiate the same inconveniences as employees of a municipal housing association.

The theatrical register recalls the invented mythologies of Lord Dunsany and the grand pronouncements of the King James Bible. But there is also something of the psychedelic rock singer announcing that he has discovered a fundamental truth about existence, shortly before making a complete tit of himself.

‘Garden’ can be understood as a parody of gnosis, recreating the experience of acquiring esoteric knowledge whilst denying the listener access to that knowledge. It resembles the peculiar certainty of an acid-induced revelation, in which an otherwise unremarkable phrase suddenly possesses the explanatory power of an entire philosophical system.

The joke is that the music itself seems thoroughly convinced.

The chiming guitar, the ceremonial movement of the two drummers and the enormous, smeared bass frequencies create an atmosphere of almost supernatural expectation. Smith can introduce a ridiculous detail without destroying the atmosphere because the musicians have established it so convincingly.

In ‘Oleano’, the same process takes place aboard an apparently troubled vessel. The whereabouts of the captain, the significance of the helm and the repeated declarations of investigative intent suggest a maritime catastrophe whose exact nature remains beyond our understanding.

The comparison with Slint's ‘Good Morning, Captain’ is particularly fruitful. Both recordings conjure entire imaginative environments through fragments of information. But where Slint cultivate an atmosphere of barely suppressed trauma, The Fall introduce synthesisers that would not disgrace an ABBA record.

‘Music Scene’ operates upon language from another direction. Smith's peculiar pronunciations destabilise familiar expressions, inviting interpretations that may never have occurred to him. A mispronounced word becomes a potential alternative word, carrying a different history and an entirely different set of associations.

The listener becomes complicit in the construction of meaning.

The temptation is to interpret every such occurrence as evidence of Smith's extraordinary literary intelligence. His greater achievement, perhaps, was to develop a method in which deliberate ingenuity and accidental nonsense became increasingly difficult to distinguish.

III. Repetition and Its Discontents

There is a tremendous difference between music that refuses to end and music that has simply failed to arrive anywhere.

The audible instruction to conclude proceedings towards the end of ‘Music Scene’ introduces a delicious complication into the recording. The studio, ordinarily concealed behind the finished artefact, becomes part of the performance. Authority makes an appearance and is promptly ignored.

Yet the recording contains the limitations of early Fall alongside its considerable virtues. Its busy guitar work and inventive percussion suggest a group far more musically adventurous than many of its punk contemporaries. The production, with its constricted drum sound and oddly submerged guitars, is less successful at communicating that adventurousness.

By ‘Garden’, The Fall had developed a considerably more sophisticated understanding of extended duration. The album version, with its heavier, more threatening bottom end, arguably uses repetition more effectively than the longer, slightly quicker Peel recording.

The music gives the impression of growing in intensity without substantially increasing its volume or speed. It is as though the musicians have formed a circle around the listener and are taking imperceptibly small steps forward.

‘Rememberance R’ possesses some of this quality. Its spacious percussion and languorous adaptation of familiar Stooges materials create an unexpectedly slinky environment. A second guitar gradually introduces more abrasive, atonal textures. The possibility of transformation becomes increasingly apparent.

Unfortunately, the recording's spoken contribution arrives just as those musical developments become particularly interesting.

‘Extricate’ presents another difficulty. Its repetitive vocal, squelching synthesiser and slashing guitar suggest several potentially productive directions, none of which is pursued with much conviction. The prospect of Adrian Sherwood's involvement makes the absence of imaginative spatial manipulation especially frustrating.

Repetition is sometimes treated as an inherently radical quality of The Fall's music. It is no such thing. Repetition acquires significance through the relationships between the elements being repeated, the changes occurring around them and the expectations developing within the listener.

It is perfectly possible to play the same thing for six minutes and achieve considerably less than somebody playing it for thirty seconds.

IV. The Virtues of Bad Taste

One of the most attractive qualities of The Fall was their occasional refusal to recognise the distinction between sophisticated music and rubbish.

The instrumental break in ‘Garden’ is almost absurdly beautiful. Out of the murky, ceremonial surroundings emerges a melody of childlike simplicity. The bass briefly suggests the sort of music that might accompany an ice-cream van, while the guitars retain something of their earlier abrasiveness.

The effect depends on the incompatibility of these elements.

A similar incompatibility rescues ‘Oleano’ from becoming an otherwise competent, driving rock performance. Its melodic keyboards introduce a gloriously theatrical quality. The comparison with early Sparks is particularly illuminating: the Mael brothers likewise understood that flamboyance, heavy guitar and apparently ridiculous melodic flourishes could reinforce rather than cancel one another.

There is a tendency within certain traditions of underground rock to regard melodic extravagance as evidence of compromised integrity. Smith's willingness to place cheap, demonstrative keyboard sounds alongside unpleasant guitars repeatedly exposed the limitations of that assumption.

But indiscriminate bad taste is no more interesting than indiscriminate good taste.

The country inflections of ‘And Therein’ occupy a more precarious position. The arrangement occasionally threatens to become the sort of mediocre country-rock performance The Fall might otherwise have covered for their own amusement. Its comparatively restrained verses could benefit from another instrumental voice.

Nevertheless, the peculiar rhetorical emphasis of the refrain gives the song an effectiveness that its modest arrangement does not immediately suggest.

Smith's archaic language transforms an observation about private comfort and public suffering into something approaching a moral pronouncement. Its significance resonates beyond the immediate circumstances: the experience of witnessing somebody else's misfortune from a position of security, acquiring the appropriate emotional response and being unable to establish what practical purpose that response serves.

There is an unexpected connection here with ‘Quit iPhone’, in which Smith objects to being made a spectator of other people's domestic existence.

Both recordings concern the relationship between the individual and information about the lives of strangers. Both suggest that exposure to those lives does not necessarily constitute a meaningful relationship with them.

The modest country song and the abrasive garage rocker have more in common than their respective arrangements would suggest.

V. Borrowed Music, Borrowed Anger

The Fall's relationship with American vernacular music was rarely straightforward.

The Sonics provide a useful illustration of the creative possibilities inherent in inherited musical forms. Their material was frequently conventional in construction but extraordinary in execution. They did not require unprecedented chord sequences to produce unprecedented sensations.

The Fall's interpretation of Lead Belly's ‘Bourgeois Blues’, however, raises a more complicated question about what is retained when music moves between radically different cultural environments.

The route through Tav Falco's Panther Burns is important. The Fall's version belongs to an established process of reinterpretation in which the original blues performance has already been subjected to garage-rock distortion.

Its appeal is considerable. The riff possesses a pleasing physical momentum, Smith's phrasing introduces characteristic irregularities and the recording avoids the excessive duration that compromises some of the group's other appropriations.

But Lead Belly's anger was rooted in racial discrimination and actual exclusion. Smith's grievances appear more concerned with the irritating habits of comfortable provincial existence.

The transformation is consistent with his longstanding suspicion of bourgeois behaviour. It nevertheless diminishes the gravity of the original subject. Somebody's objection to the inhabitants of Warrington is unlikely to carry quite the same historical weight.

There is a musical loss, too. The fluidity of Lead Belly's performance becomes a more rigid, bobbing garage-rock arrangement.

The muscular crudity that made The Sonics such an exhilarating proposition does not automatically constitute an improvement upon more supple musical forms.

This is not an argument against appropriation. The history of popular music is largely a history of appropriation. It is an argument against assuming that the process of making something sound more like The Fall necessarily makes it more interesting.

VI. The Perils of Becoming The Fall

The telephone occupies an unexpectedly persistent position in Smith's imaginative universe.

In ‘Garden’, it belongs to an obscure network of spiritual and psychological associations. In ‘Quit iPhone’, the instrument itself has become the obvious antagonist.

There is something potentially tedious about the spectacle of an elderly musician railing against modern technology. Van Morrison's complaints about social media demonstrate how readily such material can become an exercise in generational resentment.

Smith largely escapes that trap through the specificity of his hostility. His objection to being invited to look inside other people's houses identifies a peculiar aspect of modern communication: the assumption that information becomes valuable merely because it is accessible.

His vocal performance is considerably more adventurous than the accompanying music. There are delightful interjections from Eleni Poulou, but the underlying Stooges-derived riff could benefit from the sort of overloaded recording treatment that made The Sonics so effective half a century earlier.

The irony becomes more pronounced on ‘Rememberance R’.

Simon Archer's spoken contribution attacks the culture of musical reunions, particularly the entitlement of musicians returning after lengthy absences and expecting the credibility of their previous activities to remain intact.

His subsequent involvement with the reunited Pixies gives the passage an additional comic dimension. But the more interesting contradiction is musical. Here is a recording criticising nostalgic musicians whilst recycling one of the most recognisable components of its own group's musical inheritance.

The increasingly abrasive guitar textures suggest an escape from that contradiction. Unfortunately, the spoken condemnation of nostalgic musicians arrives and obscures the very musical activity that might have justified the recording.

The Fall never became a nostalgia act in quite the conventional sense. Smith's determination to continue recording new material ensured that audiences could never completely retreat into the security of their preferred historical period.

But releasing new recordings is not necessarily the same thing as producing new ideas.

The Sonics made extraordinary music from familiar materials because their relationship with those materials was alive. The greatest Fall recordings possess the same quality. Their familiarity is continually disrupted by something that appears not to belong: an unsuitable keyboard sound, an infantile melody, an archaic expression, a misplaced prophet or a captain who has apparently slept through a catastrophe.

This is why ‘Garden’ remains such an astonishing achievement. Its components are accessible enough to describe individually. Their collective effect remains almost impossible to explain.

The danger for The Fall was never that they might cease to sound like The Fall.

It was that sounding like The Fall might become sufficient.
