Who Owns the Fall Sound?

Ghost stories, truckers, other people's voices and the problem of sounding like yourself.

By Inspector R. D. Container, Office of Sonic Identity (fictional byline)

The Problem with a Fall Sound

There is a peculiar danger in asking what The Fall sounded like. The question assumes that a band with a recognisable identity must possess a stable set of musical properties, as though it were a regional cheese or a municipal building. Mark E. Smith spent decades demonstrating the opposite. He could put his voice over a half-assembled rhythm section, a polished pop cover, a muttering electronic experiment or an obstinate rockabilly stomp, and the results would be recognisably related while sounding as if they had been recorded in mutually hostile countries.

“Fall Sound” is a mischievous title because it invites a definition the recording itself refuses to supply. Its hard, repetitive propulsion belongs to a band reconstituting itself after another personnel catastrophe. What appears to be a statement of identity is also an announcement that the people responsible for that identity have changed. A group can sound like itself while becoming something else; indeed, for The Fall, becoming something else was often the most reliable way of sounding like itself.

The real question is not whether a track contains the correct ingredients. It is whether those ingredients produce the strange friction between repetition and disruption that gives the music its character. The guitar may be primitive or accomplished; the rhythm may be tight or staggeringly loose; the voice may not even belong to Smith. What matters is the sense that the recording has been organised around an argument nobody has quite finished having.

The eight recordings here occupy widely separated corners of the catalogue. Some are central to the band's mythology, others are embarrassing relatives, and several seem to have escaped from a different record collection altogether. Together they make the phrase “Fall sound” less a definition than an accusation.

A Haunted House Built from Repetition

“Medical Acceptance Gate” belongs to that fascinating region of early-eighties Fall music where a minimal idea is made to carry an unreasonable amount of atmosphere. A sparse rhythmic pattern, a half-lit instrumental background and Smith's insistent verbal shapes create the sensation of a room that is both empty and occupied. The effect is closer to a ghost story than to conventional songwriting. The threat is not announced; it hangs somewhere behind the furniture.

M. R. James understood the usefulness of withholding the monster. In “The Mezzotint”, the terrible discovery develops through incremental changes to an apparently ordinary object. The Fall's music frequently achieves something similar through repetition: a figure returns, then returns again, and gradually the listener begins to suspect that the figure has changed—or that the listener has. The technique also links the group to the Velvet Underground, whose drones could make time feel both suspended and inexorably advancing.

Yet the same band could use repetition for comic rather than supernatural ends. “Container Drivers” is a piece of anti-romantic working-class theatre, a song about the actual boredom and physical repetition of freight transport rather than the mythic freedom of the open road. American truck-driving records often turned the motorway into a landscape of heroic endurance. Here the containers are simply containers, the work is work, and the music keeps moving because stopping would defeat the point.

The drumming is crucial. An extended snare fill seems to overrun the expected boundary, turning a functional transition into a small practical joke. The song then regains its balance as though the musicians have stepped around an unexpected hole in the pavement. There is something of the best British television comedy in this: a routine performed with absolute seriousness until the routine itself becomes absurd.

These recordings occupy opposite ends of a spectrum of mood, but they share a method. Neither needs an elaborate harmonic argument. Both trust a rhythmic cell to gather associations over time. One becomes a haunting; the other becomes a shift at the depot. The apparent simplicity is not a lack of ideas but an insistence that an idea can be pursued beyond the point at which polite musicians would have changed the subject.

Who Is Allowed to Sing?

The mythology of The Fall usually places Smith at the centre, which is understandable and occasionally misleading. “The Book of Lies” and “Trust in Me” make the question of vocal authority impossible to avoid. They contain singing that does not behave like the familiar Smith performance, and they expose the uncomfortable assumption that a Fall song must be authenticated by a particular larynx.

“The Book of Lies” has the air of a private grievance being dragged into a public room. Its melody gestures towards cabaret without acquiring cabaret's polish, while the voices strain for a seriousness that the arrangement keeps undermining. A musical phrase approaches resolution and then appears to think better of it. The result is awkward in a way that cannot simply be filed under deliberate experimentation. There are moments when the musicians seem to be trying very hard, and the trying is painfully audible.

That does not make the record uninteresting. The clumsy emotional directness provides a revealing contrast with Smith's more usual habit of concealing a grievance inside jokes, aliases and fragments of overheard conversation. Here the sentiment comes dangerously close to the surface. It is as if an actor in a Beckett play has suddenly decided to deliver a speech from a provincial amateur production of a romantic musical.

“Trust in Me” makes the problem more literal. It was recorded with vocals by people other than Smith, a decision that can sound either generous, indifferent or provocatively careless. The track's very existence forces an argument about the relationship between composition, performance and brand identity. If the song is released under the band's name, does that make it a Fall recording? If the voice is absent, is the band's identity still present in the arrangement, the method, the decision to publish?

The distinction matters because it is too easy to treat Smith as a magical solvent capable of turning any recording into The Fall. His presence could indeed transform ordinary material, but he also relied on musicians who supplied much of the music's physical intelligence. The inverse proposition—that any music by those musicians must therefore be The Fall—is equally unsatisfactory. Identity is not a legal technicality, even when a record label insists on one.

A title such as “Trust in Me” is almost indecently appropriate. The listener is being asked to trust a name on a sleeve while the most familiar proof of that name is missing. It is the sort of contractual absurdity Smith might have appreciated, had he not also been responsible for creating it.

The Pop Song and the Escape Hatch

The Fall's covers often sound less like tributes than attempts to smuggle foreign objects through customs. “Popcorn Double Feature”, drawn from the Searchers' repertoire, is an unusually attractive example. Its melody is openly inviting, the arrangement relatively straightforward, and the whole thing carries the slightly unreal cheerfulness of a song that has wandered into the wrong nightclub.

There is no obligation to call this a betrayal. Pop music was never a foreign language to The Fall. Smith's group had always understood the force of a hook, even when it chose to bury that hook beneath noise, repetition or deliberate perversity. A good tune is not cancelled by the presence of a smiling chorus. The difficulty lies in deciding whether the band has transformed the material or simply enjoyed playing it.

“The Reckoning” presents a quieter kind of pop appeal. Its minor-key atmosphere and measured pulse reveal a melancholy that is easy to miss when the album surrounding it has acquired an unflattering reputation. A record such as Middle Class Revolt can be reduced to a verdict—tired, thin, uneven—and individual songs then become prisoners of the judgement. Hearing a single track without the album's accumulated baggage allows its bass movement, guitar texture and restrained mood to regain some dignity.

“Everybody But Myself”, from the later, more fractured world of Levitate, is a less settled proposition. Its sound can be difficult to locate within a conventional hierarchy of songwriting, arrangement and finished performance. Yet that is also what makes the comparison useful. A song can be more formally adventurous and less affecting; another can be apparently ordinary and achieve its purpose with greater precision.

The old avant-garde reflex is to reward difficulty simply for existing. The old pop reflex is to reward accessibility for making life easier. The Fall were capable of exposing both as lazy habits. A catchy cover might possess a more radical sense of timing than an experimental outtake, and a supposedly minor album track might reveal more emotional intelligence than a grand declaration of artistic freedom.

The pop song is an escape hatch from the myth of perpetual ugliness. It allows The Fall to be funny, sentimental, even charming, without becoming respectable. Whether the door leads anywhere worth visiting is a question that must be answered one recording at a time.

The Band That Kept Replacing Itself

Personnel changes are often treated as the scandalous background to The Fall, the chaotic business that happened between records. But the changes were part of the records' musical meaning. “Fall Sound” belongs to the period after the abrupt disintegration of one line-up and the hurried construction of another. The music is not merely a new group attempting to imitate an old group. It is the sound of a working method being reassembled while the machinery is already running.

The phrase “Reformation Post TLC”, attached to the album from which the track comes, is a typically unpleasant piece of Smithian administrative poetry. The initials refer to an insult directed at departing musicians. It is difficult to imagine a less diplomatic human-resources document. Yet the resulting record demonstrates that a group can be rebuilt without becoming a museum of its own previous achievements.

This is where the ghostly “Medical Acceptance Gate” and the direct, functional “Container Drivers” become relevant again. Their identities are not reducible to a stable line-up or a single production style. Both are recognisable because of how they treat repetition, vocal pressure and the possibility of collapse. The later “Fall Sound” reaches for the same grammar under entirely different conditions.

“Trust in Me” complicates that continuity by testing how much can be removed before the name loses its meaning. “The Book of Lies” tests the opposite problem: how much awkward humanity can be added before the performance stops feeling like the cold, elliptical world listeners expect. These are not marginal curiosities. They expose the fragile agreement between an artist and an audience about what the artist is supposed to provide.

The great danger of a cult band is that its admirers begin to confuse its habits with its achievements. The correct guitar tone, the correct sneer, the correct amount of sloppiness: all can be reproduced by musicians who have understood the surface and missed the mechanism. The Fall were never merely a collection of permitted sounds. They were a method for making sounds interfere with one another.

To ask whether a recording is really The Fall is therefore less interesting than asking what the recording does with the expectation that it should be. Sometimes the answer is magnificent; sometimes it is faintly embarrassing. Both outcomes belong in the history. The only truly un-Fall-like result would be a band that never risked making a fool of itself.

A fall sound, in the end, is not a trademark. It is the noise of a familiar structure being subjected to another unannounced inspection.
