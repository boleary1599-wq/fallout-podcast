The English Disease

By Neville Pseud

Municipal resentment, papal conspiracies, northern fashion and the indignities of getting old: The Fall spent forty years finding new ways to sound dissatisfied.

There is a persistent temptation to divide The Fall's career into periods of brilliance and wilderness, as though somewhere between Grotesque and Imperial Wax Solvent Mark E. Smith mislaid the essential ingredients of his own music. The chronology is familiar enough: the magnificent early records, the improbable commercial successes, the increasingly erratic nineties and the late-period spectacle of an ageing singer surrounded by musicians young enough to be his children. Somewhere along the way, the argument goes, the music became less interesting than the mythology.

But the wilderness-years argument has always been slightly peculiar when applied to a group whose defining characteristics included instability, perversity and a remarkable willingness to release things that other bands would have quietly thrown away. The Fall were never consistently brilliant, even at their most celebrated. Their best records are frequently interrupted by awkward experiments, baffling jokes and ideas that seem to have escaped from the rehearsal room before anyone could decide whether they were worth pursuing. Conversely, their supposedly diminished periods contain moments of extraordinary invention.

The more interesting question is not when The Fall lost their way, but what made their way so difficult to identify in the first place. Episode Ten's eight songs, spanning much of the group's recording history, offer a particularly useful opportunity to investigate this problem. They reveal a band whose musical identity was less a recognisable sound than a recurring argument about what music ought to be allowed to do.

1. English Scheme: The Country as a Minor Administrative Disaster

“English Scheme”, from 1980's Grotesque (After the Gramme), is one of the finest examples of Smith's ability to transform social irritation into musical architecture. The arrangement is remarkably economical: a brisk, repetitive rhythm, scratchy guitar figures and an organ sound that recalls the primitive garage rock of the mid-sixties. It possesses an almost cheerful momentum, which makes the contempt embedded in Smith's performance considerably more effective. The music seems to be enjoying itself while the singer catalogues the shortcomings of an entire civilisation.

The song belongs to a peculiarly English tradition of satire in which social observation is inseparable from class resentment. Smith's targets are not grand political abstractions but the everyday mechanisms through which people acquire status, authority and the confidence to behave like idiots. His England is a country of inherited assumptions, petty hierarchies and cultural pretensions, populated by people who have mistaken the circumstances of their lives for evidence of personal achievement. Unlike the nostalgic England of much contemporary pop, there is little sense that the nation possesses some lost authenticity worth recovering.

Musically, the brilliance lies in the tension between the repetitive arrangement and Smith's restless delivery. The rhythm section proceeds with bureaucratic efficiency while the vocal continually introduces new irritations, emphases and verbal complications. It is as though the machinery of English life is functioning exactly as intended, and that is precisely the problem. The recording's apparent simplicity conceals an unusually sophisticated relationship between form and subject: the music does not merely accompany the satire but provides its operating system.

“Bug Day”, from The Wonderful and Frightening World of The Fall, offers a revealing contrast. By 1984, the arrival of Brix Smith and the group's changing instrumental relationships had expanded the musical vocabulary considerably. The arrangements could accommodate greater melodic variety, brighter textures and a more playful sense of construction. Where “English Scheme” seems to organise its eccentricities into a single coherent argument, “Bug Day” is more fragmented, allowing musical and verbal oddities to coexist without insisting upon a tidy explanation.

The difference is not simply one of quality. “English Scheme” demonstrates what happens when The Fall's peculiar methods achieve an almost perfect alignment of music, language and subject. “Bug Day” belongs to a period when the group was increasingly interested in discovering how many different things a Fall recording could contain. One is a tightly constructed piece of social criticism; the other is an exploration of possibilities. The later record may be less immediately satisfying, but its willingness to complicate the group's established methods is part of what made the Brix-era Fall so productive.

2. Papal Conspiracies and the Trouble with Progress

By the early nineties, The Fall had become proficient at something that might once have seemed unimaginable: making contemporary, comparatively polished records. The electronic rhythms and studio sophistication of Code: Selfish reflected a group that had absorbed elements of the musical culture surrounding it without entirely surrendering its eccentricities. This created an interesting problem. What happens when a band whose identity depends upon awkwardness becomes technically accomplished at sounding modern?

“Time Enough at Last” inhabits this contradiction. Its electronic textures suggest order and control, while the arrangement retains the group's characteristic resistance to smooth development. The title recalls the famous Twilight Zone story in which a man finally achieves his dream of uninterrupted reading only to discover that circumstances have made it impossible. Whether Smith intended the allusion or simply enjoyed the phrase, it is an appropriate image for a band whose relationship with progress was always uneasy. The recording sounds like musicians exploring the possibilities of contemporary production while remaining suspicious of the very sophistication they have acquired.

The danger of this approach is that tension can become indecision. The Fall's electronic experiments were most effective when the technology introduced new kinds of rhythmic or textural instability, rather than simply placing familiar Smith performances inside fashionable arrangements. “Time Enough at Last” is interesting partly because it exposes that difficulty. The musical elements do not always appear to agree about the sort of record they are making, and the resulting friction is not invariably productive.

“Hey! Luciani”, released in 1986, offers a more convincing demonstration of how apparently incompatible materials could be made to work together. Emerging from Smith's theatrical project concerning Pope John Paul I, whose brief papacy and sudden death had inspired considerable speculation, the song transforms religious intrigue into an improbably exuberant piece of pop music. The rhythm is buoyant, the instrumental arrangement bright and insistent, and Smith delivers his peculiar historical preoccupations without the slightest suggestion that the subject demands solemn treatment.

The achievement is not merely comic. Smith understood that sinister material could become more unsettling when presented through inappropriate musical forms. A song about religious authority and possible conspiracy might conventionally employ dark harmonies, ominous atmospheres and theatrical menace. “Hey! Luciani” instead makes the whole business sound almost celebratory. Its apparent accessibility becomes part of the disturbance.

This is one reason the distinction between experimental and commercial Fall has always been misleading. The group could make adventurous use of pop structures just as readily as abrasive repetition, provided those structures were allowed to retain something peculiar. “Hey! Luciani” does not sound compromised by its catchiness. It sounds like a band discovering that catchiness itself can be made to behave strangely.

3. North West Fashion Show: The Comedian Becomes the Joke

By the middle of the nineties, Mark E. Smith had become an established character in British popular culture. The scowl, the cigarette, the Mancunian drawl and the contempt for journalists had hardened into a familiar public performance. This presented a problem for a writer whose principal method involved exposing the ridiculousness of other people's behaviour. How could someone so adept at identifying cultural pretension avoid becoming a collection of mannerisms himself?

“North West Fashion Show”, from Cerebral Caustic, approaches the problem through comedy. Its ungainly arrangement and peculiar vocal gestures create the impression of a group participating in a joke whose precise subject remains uncertain. The title alone is magnificent, combining the glamour and aspiration of the fashion industry with a regional identity that Smith repeatedly presented as resistant to such nonsense. Yet the humour is more complicated than a simple northern dismissal of metropolitan pretension. Smith himself had become a recognisable cultural commodity, a performer whose carefully cultivated indifference was now part of what audiences expected him to deliver.

The Fall's relationship with comedy is often underestimated. Their music is regularly discussed through the respectable vocabulary of post-punk criticism: alienation, repetition, experimentalism, industrial landscapes and the occult. All of these are relevant, but they can obscure the extraordinary amount of laughter in the records. Smith's vocal delivery frequently depends upon exaggeration, unexpected emphasis, deliberate awkwardness and the transformation of ordinary phrases into absurd little performances. His ability to make a ridiculous statement sound like an important revelation was among his most distinctive talents.

“Reprise: Jane – Prof Mick”, from the notoriously divisive Are You Are Missing Winner, offers a less polished example of the same instinct. The recording has the loose, exploratory quality of musicians negotiating an arrangement while the tape is running. Repeated figures establish themselves, instrumental relationships shift, and Smith's contributions seem to interfere with the group's attempts to achieve conventional coherence. At moments, the performance feels less like a completed composition than a document of the difficulties involved in making one.

There is a temptation to elevate this sort of material into a deliberate critique of musical professionalism. Sometimes that argument is persuasive; sometimes it amounts to making excuses for a recording that could have benefited from another afternoon's work. The distinction matters. Smith's willingness to release unfinished or awkward material was fundamental to The Fall's creative freedom, but it did not automatically transform every experiment into an artistic triumph.

What makes these recordings interesting is the degree to which they expose the group's internal contradictions. The musicians attempt to establish musical order while Smith complicates it; the arrangements threaten to become conventional before some new disruption intervenes. Occasionally the result is exhilarating, occasionally merely peculiar. The Fall's comedy often emerges from precisely this uncertainty, with the listener never quite sure whether the musicians are enjoying the joke or have become its victims.

4. The Last Fall: Primitive Music for an Ageing Body

By the time of Imperial Wax Solvent in 2008, Smith was approaching sixty, and The Fall had survived another spectacular period of personnel instability. The conventional narrative of rock history would have suggested that the group should either retire or settle into the dignified repetition of its greatest achievements. Smith showed little interest in either option. Instead, The Fall continued making records that were frequently abrasive, occasionally magnificent and sometimes almost aggressively indifferent to the question of whether anyone particularly wanted to hear them.

“Latch Key Kid” is a revealing late-period recording. Its title evokes a recognisable feature of working-class domestic life: the child returning to an empty house, carrying the means of entry because no adult is waiting. It suggests independence and abandonment in equal measure, precisely the sort of ordinary social arrangement that Smith could transform into something faintly sinister. Yet the song resists developing these associations into a conventional narrative, allowing its repetitive musical structure and Smith's increasingly fragmented vocal delivery to generate their own atmosphere.

The ageing voice is crucial. Smith no longer possesses the sharp, argumentative clarity of the singer who recorded “English Scheme”. His delivery has thickened, phrases emerge with unpredictable emphasis, and words can seem to disintegrate before they have been properly articulated. But this physical deterioration also introduces new rhythmic possibilities. The musicians must accommodate a voice that no longer behaves according to conventional expectations, producing a peculiar tension between instrumental discipline and vocal instability.

Against it stands “Higgle-Dy Piggle-Dy”, The Fall's contribution to the Silver Monk Time tribute project. The original was recorded by The Monks, whose extraordinary combination of primitive rock and roll, aggressive repetition and absurdist theatricality had long represented an alternative genealogy for The Fall's music. The Monks demonstrated that rock could become more powerful by refusing the usual progression towards sophistication, reducing its vocabulary to blunt rhythms, obsessive riffs and confrontational performance.

The Fall's engagement with this music is therefore more than an affectionate acknowledgement of influence. It reveals the continuity between their earliest instincts and their late-period methods. The same attraction to primitive repetition that helped shape Dragnet and Grotesque remains audible decades later, although the circumstances have changed beyond recognition. The musicians are different, the recording technology has evolved, and Smith's voice now carries the unmistakable physical evidence of age.

There is nevertheless a danger in treating this continuity as proof of artistic vitality. By the late 2000s, the group's characteristic methods were sufficiently established that primitive repetition could become another kind of formula. Covering The Monks risked confirming an existing mythology rather than discovering anything new. “Latch Key Kid” is arguably the more interesting proposition because it allows the accumulated damage and experience of the intervening decades to alter the music itself.

This is what makes the late Fall worth investigating beyond the familiar narratives of decline. The records are not simply diminished versions of earlier achievements, nor are they automatically heroic demonstrations of artistic persistence. At their best, they reveal what happens when a musical method is pursued for decades under continually changing physical and social conditions.

The wilderness years, then, are not a separate territory into which The Fall occasionally wandered when inspiration deserted them. The wilderness was the group's natural environment: a place where musical ideas were tested, abandoned, revived and sometimes pursued long after common sense suggested they should have been left alone. Their catalogue contains genuine failures, and those failures should not be disguised as masterpieces through elaborate critical generosity. But it also contains moments of extraordinary invention in periods that conventional rock history would prefer to dismiss.

The Fall never established a definitive sound because the moment they did so, they began finding reasons to distrust it. Their most valuable quality was not consistency but the willingness to become worse at something familiar in order to discover whether something unfamiliar might be possible. That method produced an enormous amount of difficult, ridiculous and occasionally terrible music. It also produced some of the most remarkable records in British popular culture.

Smith spent forty years making England sound like a place where the machinery was functioning perfectly and absolutely everything had gone wrong. Perhaps the most remarkable thing about The Fall is that the machinery kept running.
