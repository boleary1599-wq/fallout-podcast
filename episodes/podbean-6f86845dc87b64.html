The Sound of a Man's Integrity Dying

By Mere Pseud Mag. Edward

I. The Bastard Hat

Marc Riley's musical life after The Fall presents a slightly awkward proposition for anyone committed to the idea that Mark E. Smith was the sole animating intelligence of the group. There is enough evidence scattered throughout the Creepers' records to suggest that Riley took something rather valuable with him when he left: an instinct for melody, a sense of the ridiculous and an ability to recognise when a musical idea might benefit from becoming an actual tune.

'Favourite Sister', with its jangling guitars and endearingly underproduced vocals, sounds curiously like a premature dispatch from the world of the Stone Roses' early demos. There is something of the Chameleons in Riley's vocal delivery elsewhere, although the Creepers rarely sound like a group struggling to escape the musical shadow of their former employer.

Perhaps the more revealing aspect of Riley's subsequent career is his willingness to be funny. His involvement with Oink!, his association with Chris Sievey and the magnificently self-deprecating Lard's Fall Diaries suggest a personality whose comic instincts were fundamentally different from Smith's. Where Smith frequently employed humour as an instrument of intimidation, Riley seemed quite happy to make himself the object of the joke. There is something of the later Reeves and Mortimer universe in this willingness to inhabit one's own absurdity.

'Bastard Hat' addresses the dilemma almost explicitly: is being a bastard a necessary component of being an interesting person?

It is a question that hangs over a considerable portion of The Fall's catalogue. The peculiar mythology surrounding the group encourages us to regard every departure from its abrasive essence as evidence of creative dilution. Yet the emergence of Brix, the increasing prominence of keyboards and even the occasional excursion into Christmas music suggest that accessibility need not constitute surrender.

Creeperville is a pleasant enough place to spend an afternoon without necessarily feeling compelled to buy property there. Riley's subsequent music does not retrospectively establish him as the suppressed genius of The Fall. It does, however, make the contributions of individual musicians rather more interesting than the familiar mythology of Smith and his interchangeable employees would allow.

II. Bubblegum and Bile

There is something particularly satisfying about the way 'C.R.E.E.P.' refuses to resolve the apparent contradiction between its musical and lyrical personalities. It is a character assassination conducted to the accompaniment of a particularly agreeable pop single.

The keyboard figure has the uncomplicated charm of Altered Images. The production occasionally suggests Blondie, while its bouncing melodic construction has inevitably attracted comparisons with the Archies' 'Sugar, Sugar'. Whether there is any conscious appropriation here is considerably less interesting than the fact that The Fall could invite such comparisons without sounding remotely embarrassed by them.

The crucial ingredient is Brix. Her backing vocals do not merely decorate the chorus; they effectively constitute it. Smith remains occupied with his own peculiar business while the voices behind him establish the melodic centre of the record. It is a technique familiar from Motown, where the relationship between lead and supporting voices can create a hook more memorable than either could manage independently.

There are moments when Smith sounds almost nervous, as though aware that he is participating in a proper pop record and determined not to ruin it. His occasional Elvis-like inflections are particularly endearing. For once, the singer appears to be on his best behaviour.

The relationship between Smith and Brix anticipates something of the appeal of Black Francis and Kim Deal. Doolittle remains a useful point of comparison, although the Pixies rarely ventured quite so enthusiastically into bubblegum territory.

A similar melodic generosity informs 'Behind the Counter'. Its semi-wrong chords, cascading synthesiser melody and Smith's little vocal inflections make for an unusually inviting record about an unusually uninviting world. Both songs understand that bitterness can be made considerably more effective by giving it something sweet to travel in.

The principle even extends to 'Hark the Herald Angels Sing', where Smith's deadpan delivery is counterbalanced by an enthusiastic celestial chorus. The amateurish guitar, rearranged chords and conspicuously festive production are individually rather naff. Collectively, they possess a certain charm.

The important distinction is between the deliberate exploitation of naffness and the accidental production of it. The Fall could make a pop record as infectious as 'C.R.E.E.P.' or a Christmas record as gloriously inappropriate as 'Hark' without necessarily sacrificing their identity.

The Monkees would probably have understood this better than several generations of Fall purists.

III. Cardboard Boxes and Kitchen Sinks

'Dresden Dolls' possesses the familiar appeal of music caught before anybody has had an opportunity to improve it into something less interesting. The guitar is aggressive, the production has the acoustic properties of a cardboard box and the drums are frequently more inventive than the material they are accompanying.

There are echoes of the Modern Lovers, Television and Richard Hell in its rhythmic approach, alongside something of the Stranglers' nastiness and the more primitive aspects of Roxy Music. Slowing down certain garage-rock records, particularly those of the Troggs or the Seeds, brings their basic musical architecture surprisingly close to that of the song.

None of this makes 'Dresden Dolls' a neglected masterpiece. The chorus is irritating, the breakdown has the unmistakable quality of a sixth-form band's first collective composition and the lyrics offer a predictable attack on punk conformity.

But Karl Burns appears to recognise that the riff requires assistance and supplies it with almost continuous interventions. His drumming has personality because it responds to the inadequacies of the composition.

There are moments in 'The Knight, the Devil and Death' when one longs for Burns to arrive uninvited and throw the kitchen sink at proceedings. Simon Wolstencroft's metronomic approach works considerably better on 'Behind the Counter', where Hanley's bass and Bush's keyboards provide sufficient activity elsewhere.

The problem is not rhythmic simplicity. It is rhythmic simplicity in the company of insufficiently interesting music.

'Midwatch 1953' presents almost the opposite problem. Swinging drums, rockabilly guitar, apparently misplaced bass notes, synthetic strings and random electronic intrusions suggest a group being dismantled and reassembled in real time. The dropping in and fading out of musical elements occasionally recalls King Tubby's approach to the studio.

Yet there is an uncomfortable sense that the electronic interventions are being employed to rescue something fundamentally unremarkable. The monitor mixes offer a clearer view of the rather plodding indie song underneath. The bleeps and bloops, accompanied by Smith's voice, might have made for a more interesting record without it.

Compare this with 'Bonkers in Phoenix', where the production's hostility towards the underlying composition possesses something approaching a conceptual purpose. On 'Midwatch', the relationship between the musical components is considerably less convincing.

'Jetplane', by contrast, understands how accumulation can become composition. Its marching snare, pulsing bass and synthesiser melody establish a framework within which the voices and guitars can gradually develop their own peculiar relationships.

The distinction between a successful experiment and a failed one is not necessarily the quantity of strange noises involved. It is whether those noises create something that was not already there.

IV. The Administrative Gothic

The Fall's universe has always accommodated the supernatural and the administrative with remarkably little difficulty.

'The Knight, the Devil and Death', with its high strings, sheets of guitar noise and detached narration, possesses a genuinely crepuscular atmosphere. There is something of Current 93 in its implied gothic grandeur, although the rhythmic foundation owes rather more to the musical vocabulary of the early nineties. The ghost of 'Kinky Afro' is never particularly far away, while the aloof vocal delivery recalls Bongwater's 'Nick Cave Dolls'.

The reference to Albrecht Dürer's engraving opens up the rather appealing possibility of imagining the steadfast knight attempting to navigate worldly temptation and spiritual decay while the financial department quietly reduces his operating budget.

'Midwatch 1953' similarly invites interpretations that extend considerably beyond its minimal lyrical content. Its possible relationship to Britain's nuclear testing programme introduces a genuinely disturbing historical dimension. Alternatively, the title and atmosphere suggest the world of John Wyndham's The Midwich Cuckoos and the nuclear anxieties informing much British science fiction.

Smith's monotonous delivery can be heard as the voice of a decaying imperial power sleepwalking towards another atrocity. Whether the record actually earns such an interpretation is another matter. There is always a danger of discovering a considerably more interesting concept than the one the music successfully communicates.

The bureaucratic paranoia of 'Jetplane' is more immediately persuasive. Airline queues, Italian sunbeds, cash payments, obliterated ticket numbers and a rock group holding up proceedings become the components of an international conspiracy whose precise nature remains wonderfully elusive.

It resembles a report from The Day Today, delivered from one of J.G. Ballard's airport departure lounges. Smith's overlapping high and low voices, the foreign-language interventions and the increasingly insistent musical accompaniment give the impression of information being communicated with enormous urgency by people who have forgotten what the information actually means.

'Behind the Counter' inhabits a similarly antagonistic relationship with everyday systems. Cars appear to be picking on Smith, employment becomes a punishment for failed artistic ambition and the corner shop emerges as a potential battleground in an ideological retail war.

The particular genius of these records lies in their ability to make the ordinary seem conspiratorial without making it any less ordinary.

V. The Productive Misuse of Musicians

The curious thing about 'The Knight, the Devil and Death' is how little it suffers from Smith's absence. Its shortcomings are musical rather than ideological. The arrangement possesses a surprising sense of space, the narration works and the guitar noise provides moments of genuine excitement.

Craig Scanlon's contribution to its conception is a useful reminder that the group contained several creative personalities whose potential was not always fully explored.

Dave Bush presents another example. The trance-like breakdown and faintly ridiculous keyboard noises on 'Behind the Counter' are undeniably dated, but dismissing them as period decoration overlooks the extent to which they shape the composition. Even the cheesy whistles and vaguely Van Halen-like synthesiser sounds have their place.

It is easy to imagine the track working magnificently in a crowded venue, with the collective movement of the audience supplying the momentum that the production occasionally fails to capture.

The same question of musical contribution becomes particularly interesting with Peter Greenway on 'Jetplane'. His initial intervention largely duplicates what the other musicians are already doing, adding an unnecessary metallic weight to an arrangement that has established its own momentum.

When he retreats, the interplay between Smith and Eleni allows the song to bob along rather beautifully. There is something approaching a New Order chorus towards the end, a melodic turn that would not have sounded entirely misplaced on Brotherhood.

Greenway eventually returns with feedback rather than another riff, and suddenly his presence makes perfect sense.

The track's combination of rhythmic insistence, playful vocals and gradually accumulating instrumentation suggests Battles attempting 'Yellow Submarine'. Its snare-driven development also recalls the incremental drama of Godspeed You! Black Emperor, albeit compressed into a considerably less solemn musical experience.

Compare this with 'Mask Search', where a reasonably entertaining rockabilly kick-around never quite transcends its generic foundations. Dave Spurr's bouncing bass, cheeky drum dropouts and Smith's high-pitched gurgling are all welcome. But the individual contributions cannot quite rescue the collective result.

The history of The Fall is full of musicians discovering that their most valuable contribution might involve doing considerably less of what they were conventionally supposed to do.

Brix becomes the chorus. Bush becomes the hook. Burns becomes the arrangement. Greenway becomes the noise.

Smith's greatest achievement was not always knowing what he wanted from these musicians. Sometimes it was recognising the value of what happened when they stopped giving it to him.

VI. The Sound of a Man's Integrity Dying

There is something particularly revealing about the argument over whether 'Hark the Herald Angels Sing' should be considered a cover version. Does the fact that it is a traditional carol make it more acceptable? Does rearranging the chords constitute sufficient artistic intervention? Can a record be simultaneously a novelty and a legitimate Fall song?

The prospect of a Fall Christmas album occupying the same cultural territory as Pinky and Perky, the Chipmunks or a collection of Hawaiian Christmas music is enormously appealing. Each takes familiar material and imposes upon it an immediately recognisable musical personality.

The Fall's contribution would presumably involve Smith sounding miserable over something fundamentally jolly.

There are worse artistic propositions.

The difficulty is that novelty can be an extraordinarily effective disguise for musical mediocrity. 'Hark' is enjoyable, but its jangling indie arrangement is hardly comparable to the melodic invention of R.E.M.'s 'Stand', which it occasionally brings to mind. 'Xmas with Simon' offers a rather more distinctive example of what The Fall could do with festive material.

Yet the Christmas record's modest pleasures remain preferable to the elaborate but largely unsuccessful experimentation of 'Midwatch 1953'. An ambitious failure is not automatically more rewarding than an unambitious success.

Equally, the amusing lyrical premise of 'Mask Search' cannot disguise its rather ordinary boogie. Smith's delight at the reported findings of a national happiness survey, in which mushy peas and Snow Patrol apparently featured among the nation's grievances, is entirely understandable. The reduction of existential dissatisfaction to a disappointing lunch and an irritating radio playlist is magnificent.

The music is considerably less so.

What makes 'C.R.E.E.P.', 'Behind the Counter' and 'Jetplane' particularly satisfying is that their pleasures survive outside the elaborate mythology surrounding their creators. Their melodies, rhythmic ideas and arrangements possess an independent vitality. They do not need to be explained into greatness.

Perhaps artistic integrity is not something that can be preserved by refusing to enjoy Christmas records, bubblegum melodies or unfashionable synthesiser sounds.

Perhaps the more interesting sound is that of a man abandoning his principles because he has accidentally encountered something he likes.

Marc Riley understood something about that distinction. So did Brix Smith, Steve Hanley, Karl Burns, Craig Scanlon and, rather more often than he is given credit for, Dave Bush.

Sometimes The Fall made extraordinary music by being difficult.

Sometimes they made extraordinary music by forgetting to be.
