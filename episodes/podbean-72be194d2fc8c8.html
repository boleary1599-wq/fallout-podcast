THE FLICKERING LEXICON: A FIELD GUIDE TO THINGS THAT REFUSE TO EXIST

On office pornography, northern nationalism, biblical apparitions, primitive rock ’n’ roll and the futility of explaining Mark E. Smith.

By Dr. M. Hexen, Senior Correspondent for Unlicensed Metaphysics

I. THE LIBRARY OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS

Imagine an archaeologist discovering the remains of British civilisation in the year 4026. Among the surviving artefacts are a damaged photocopier, a promotional video featuring men in industrial clothing, several references to obscure Anatolian civilisations, and a filing cabinet containing the complete lyrical works of Mark E. Smith.

The Fall's extraordinary achievement was to construct an artistic universe in which everything appears significant but almost nothing can be conclusively explained. A phrase borrowed from an obscure magazine sits beside a fragment of overheard conversation. A biblical allusion is interrupted by a complaint about office equipment. A supposedly straightforward rock song suddenly reveals itself as an elaborate conspiracy involving geography, class and sexual embarrassment.

The listener becomes a detective investigating a crime that may never have occurred.

This is the peculiar territory shared by Cruiser's Creek, Just Step S'ways, Hit the North, Pittsfield Directive, Touch Sensitive, Jungle Rock, Hittite Man and Junger Cloth. Their musical surfaces differ wildly, but all eight inhabit a universe where information is unstable and certainty is generally the first casualty.

The closest literary relative may be Jorge Luis Borges, whose imaginary libraries contain every possible book, including countless volumes of meaningless combinations and a small number of texts that appear to explain the universe.

Smith's lyrical catalogue resembles one of these libraries, except that somebody has replaced the librarian with a hostile landlord and the reference section contains several copies of Exchange & Mart.

II. THE OFFICE OF UNSPEAKABLE DESIRES

There is a particularly English form of surrealism that requires no melting clocks, impossible architecture or visits from supernatural beings. It requires only an office.

The workplace imagined in Cruiser's Creek is a landscape of sexual frustration, managerial humiliation and mysterious disaster. Its imagery of steel cabinets and compromised professional relationships transforms the ordinary office into a theatre of concealed appetites.

The music behaves accordingly. The bass moves with an almost indecent confidence, the guitars deliver abrupt, distorted interruptions, and the rhythmic attack has something of the B-52's relocated to a particularly unpleasant industrial estate.

There is a cocky glam-rock strut beneath the abrasion, but it keeps being interrupted by jagged changes of direction. The result suggests a band attempting to dance while somebody repeatedly applies the emergency brake.

The song's bizarre office-world connects unexpectedly with Touch Sensitive, whose comparatively accessible garage-rock propulsion conceals another collection of unsettling social encounters.

Here, the musical apparatus is almost suspiciously efficient. The riff arrives with commercial confidence; the arrangement understands the physical pleasures of rock ’n’ roll; the string embellishments lend a degree of theatrical polish.

Yet Smith remains an unreliable narrator, apparently incapable of allowing the listener to enjoy a straightforward pop song without introducing a new source of discomfort.

Both recordings expose the absurdity beneath apparently respectable environments. One finds it among the filing cabinets; the other beneath the polished surface of popular entertainment.

David Lynch understood this relationship particularly well. The horror of Blue Velvet depends upon the discovery that suburban normality conceals something grotesque, erotic and inexplicable.

Smith's variation is considerably less cinematic. The severed ear has been replaced by a suspicious expense claim.

III. THE NORTH IS NOT A PLACE

The geographical imagination of The Fall is as unstable as its social world.

Hit the North appears, at first, to offer an uncomplicated declaration of regional identity. Its pounding electronic rhythm, insistent chorus and confrontational title suggest the possibility of a northern anthem.

But Smith's relationship with regional belonging was never straightforward. The North is simultaneously a geographical region, a cultural mythology, an economic condition and an attitude towards people who live somewhere else.

The song's driving dance-rock arrangement resembles a particularly aggressive collision between post-punk and the commercial possibilities of late-1980s electronic pop. It is both accessible and faintly belligerent.

The tension becomes clearer when placed beside Pittsfield Directive, whose less immediate musical personality suggests another kind of territorial confusion: a landscape assembled from fragments of musical memory rather than recognisable regional identity.

The North of Hit the North is no more stable than the ancient world of Hittite Man, where a civilisation from the second millennium BCE reappears amid desert imagery and electric guitar.

There is something of the Situationists here: geography transformed by psychological experience rather than conventional mapping.

But where Guy Debord imagined the dérive as an exploration of urban space, Smith's geographical excursions frequently resemble a bus journey during which somebody has supplied the wrong historical commentary.

IV. ROCK ’N’ ROLL BEFORE THE INVENTION OF HISTORY

The Fall's relationship with primitive rock music was never simply nostalgic.

Rockabilly, garage rock, glam, surf guitar and rhythm and blues appear throughout their catalogue, but rarely as affectionate reconstructions of a vanished golden age.

Instead, these styles are treated as raw materials capable of surviving considerable mistreatment.

The rockabilly propulsion of Cruiser's Creek belongs to the same broad musical ancestry as Jungle Rock, yet the contrast is revealing.

Hank Mizell's original Jungle Rock possesses the exuberant absurdity of 1950s novelty rock ’n’ roll. Its appeal depends upon rhythmic directness, playful exaggeration and a world in which animal noises can apparently constitute a perfectly respectable artistic statement.

The Fall's interpretation participates in this ridiculous tradition while also exposing its inherent strangeness.

The connection with Hittite Man is less obvious but more intriguing.

Its surf-guitar atmosphere evokes Dick Dale and the science-fiction instrumentals of Man or Astro-man?, yet the ancient imagery produces an extraordinary temporal collision.

A musical vocabulary associated with American beaches and cheap science-fiction cinema accompanies the appearance of a figure from the ancient Near East.

This is precisely the sort of historical confusion Smith could transform into an artistic method.

The Fall repeatedly treat the past as an enormous second-hand shop whose contents can be removed from their original contexts and reassembled without consulting the instructions.

The result resembles the historical imagination of the British comic 2000 AD, where ancient mythology, futuristic technology and contemporary political satire coexist without embarrassment.

One moment the listener is confronted by a primitive rockabilly stomp. The next, a prophet emerges from the desert accompanied by a guitar that sounds as though it has been borrowed from a 1960s surf compilation.

V. THE PROBLEM WITH EXPLANATIONS

The temptation to explain The Fall is almost irresistible.

Smith's lyrics contain enough literary quotations, historical references, borrowed phrases, personal grievances and deliberate misdirections to sustain a small academic industry.

Yet every successful identification introduces a new problem.

Discovering the source of an obscure phrase may explain where Smith found it, but not necessarily why he chose it, what he intended or how its meaning changed once incorporated into a song.

The office imagery of Cruiser's Creek can be investigated through biographical speculation, social history or literary comparison. But its force also depends upon the extraordinary sound of Smith delivering these fragments over a rhythm section that appears to be demolishing a building.

Likewise, the ancient references of Hittite Man invite historical investigation, but the song's imaginative power cannot be reduced to a correct account of Bronze Age Anatolia.

Roland Barthes's Death of the Author becomes an obvious reference, although Smith might have regarded the entire argument as another example of academics making an unnecessary fuss.

Barthes challenged the assumption that a text's meaning could be determined by recovering its creator's intentions.

Even when a reference can be traced, its meaning remains unstable because the performance itself continually modifies it.

The same applies to the almost aggressively direct pleasures of Touch Sensitive and Hit the North. Their apparent accessibility does not make them transparent.

A catchy chorus is not necessarily an explanation. Sometimes it is merely an especially efficient way of concealing the problem.

VI. THE GOSPEL ACCORDING TO THE FILING CABINET

Religion is never very far away in The Fall, although it rarely appears in an orthodox form.

The mysterious figure of Hittite Man seems to emerge from a landscape halfway between biblical antiquity and a low-budget science-fiction film.

His white clothing, desert associations and peculiar symbolic details suggest an apparition whose exact theological credentials remain uncertain.

The music reinforces the ambiguity. Its surf-inflected guitar lines suggest movement across an imaginary landscape, while the repetitive structure creates something approaching ritual intensity.

There are distant affinities with the hypnotic methods of Fela Kuti, whose music demonstrated how rhythmic repetition could become a vehicle for collective transformation.

Yet The Fall's version of transcendence is stubbornly provincial.

The sacred apparition may arrive, but somebody will immediately ask an inappropriate question about his clothing.

Meanwhile, the rhythmic insistence of Just Step S'ways suggests another kind of ritual: the body compelled into movement by repetition.

Its carefully constructed bass movement and rhythmic architecture demonstrate that The Fall's supposed primitivism often concealed considerable formal intelligence.

The band's repetitions were not simply the result of limited musical ability. They were methods of generating pressure.

The comparison with religious incantation is instructive. Repetition can empty language of ordinary meaning while simultaneously giving it extraordinary emotional force.

A phrase becomes powerful not because it reveals new information, but because its recurrence changes the listener's relationship to it.

The same principle operates in the insistent slogans of Hit the North and the deliberately excessive physicality of Jungle Rock.

One belongs to political mythology, another to popular entertainment, another to the strange ceremonial world of Smith's imagination.

All depend upon the transformation of repetition into belief.

VII. THE CONCORDANCE OF THE DAMNED

A concordance is an attempt to impose order upon language.

Every word is catalogued. Every occurrence can be located. Patterns emerge that might otherwise remain invisible.

Historically, such projects were associated with the study of sacred texts. The Bible could be dismantled into individual words and reconstructed as a searchable system of references.

Applying the same method to The Fall produces an extraordinary conceptual joke.

Here is a body of work filled with contradictions, deliberate obscurity, unreliable narrators and unstable meanings, subjected to the kind of forensic analysis once reserved for scripture.

The absurdity is magnificent. But the enterprise is also revealing.

Smith's writing rewards investigation precisely because it refuses to become exhausted by it.

One may discover the source of a borrowed quotation, identify an obscure historical figure or establish the correct wording of a disputed lyric.

Yet the song remains.

The bass continues its peculiar journey through Just Step S'ways. The office worker continues his unspeakable activities in Cruiser's Creek. The ancient apparition remains stranded somewhere between the Old Testament and a surf-rock compilation.

VIII. THE LAST WORD IS INCORRECT

What emerges from these eight recordings is a vision of culture as an unstable archive.

Ancient civilisations survive as lyrical fragments. Northern identity becomes an electronic chant. Primitive rock ’n’ roll acquires new and disreputable purposes. Office life turns into grotesque mythology. Apparently simple pop songs conceal elaborate structures of resentment, desire and historical confusion.

A biblical reference may be followed by a joke about an animal. A sophisticated rhythmic arrangement may support a deliberately ridiculous vocal performance. A piece of regional political theatre may also function as an excellent dance record.

Smith's alternative was to suggest that contemporary experience had already become so absurd that the appropriate artistic response was to collect its fragments and arrange them in the most provocative order possible.

And somewhere, in a room full of records, notebooks, disputed transcriptions and increasingly elaborate databases, somebody is still trying to establish precisely what was said.
