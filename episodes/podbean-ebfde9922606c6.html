THE DEVIL HAS A GEORGIAN GLAZED PORCH

The Fall's most unsettling achievement was to make English social life sound like an occult ceremony. A plank of wood, a dead president, a cheap hotel and a Wanda Jackson single explain why the ritual still works.

By Dr. Vernon P. Grotesque, Department of Unlicensed Folklore

A barn dance is a reassuring English invention until someone starts stabbing the dancers. The bunting remains in place. The village-hall treasurer continues to sell tea. Somewhere beyond the range of the camera, a hereditary conflict is being settled with farm implements. The Fall were uniquely equipped to provide the music, because Mark E. Smith understood something fundamental about the English uncanny: the horror is not that normal life has been interrupted, but that normal life has carried on.

On “2 × 4”, from The Wonderful and Frightening World of The Fall, the devil has apparently exchanged his traditional itinerant existence for a Georgian glazed porch. A length of timber becomes both weapon and rhythmic instruction. The song's compact, lurching bass figure does most of the heavy lifting; guitars snarl around it while Brix Smith's vocal presence lends the proceedings a sort of malevolent party atmosphere. It is possible to hear a cheerful tune about hitting Satan with building materials. It is also possible to hear an entire history of English property ownership compressed into a few minutes of abrasive rock music.

Nigel Kneale's Quatermass is an obvious relative, not because the record depicts a literal extraterrestrial invasion, but because Kneale understood how ancient forces might occupy modern institutions without changing their outward appearance. The church hall is still a church hall. The local dignitary is still wearing a tie. Something considerably older than either has begun making the decisions.

A SMALL PIECE OF WOOD AGAINST THE APOCALYPSE

The Fall's great rhythmic invention was to make repetition feel simultaneously primitive and unstable. Steve Hanley's bass on “2 × 4” is neither a virtuoso display nor an inert ostinato. Its tiny interruptions and accents make the whole song wobble forward. There is an audible disagreement between the rigidity of the figure and the restless activity surrounding it. That friction is more interesting than another perfectly executed garage-rock riff.

It also exposes the curious problem of production. John Leckie's comparatively legible studio sound gives the arrangement definition, yet the song's rawer television performances suggest an extra degree of danger. A clearer Fall record is not automatically a more forceful Fall record. If the machinery is made too safe, the worker can no longer lose a finger.

Then consider “And This Day”, the immense closing convulsion of Hex Enduction Hour. Where “2 × 4” turns violence into a jaunty domestic procedure, “And This Day” removes the procedure altogether. Drums collide, keyboards appear to have been wheeled in from a closed amusement arcade, and Smith's voice deteriorates into something between an accusation and a damaged public-address system. It has the dimensions of an epic and the organisational coherence of a collapsing marquee.

The Velvet Underground's “Sister Ray” is an unavoidable comparison, but Lou Reed's disreputable carnival still offers a recognisable social environment. “And This Day” is what happens when the carnival has left, the electricity remains on, and the groundskeeper begins speaking in tongues. There is a kinship with the ecstatic disorder of Albert Ayler and the uncompromising duration of Tony Conrad's drone music, though The Fall achieve their effect with much less interest in the dignity of avant-garde credentials.

The crucial question is whether the piece would improve if reduced to four minutes. Almost certainly it would become easier to enjoy. It would also cease to perform its central act of aggression: demanding that the listener remain in the room long after ordinary rock music has finished making its point. Samuel Beckett's Endgame is not improved by giving everybody a lift home after the first ten minutes.

THE COMMISSION OF VERY PECULIAR CRIMES

Smith's political imagination rarely involved a straightforward declaration of allegiance. His songs more often resemble dossiers compiled by a private investigator who has begun to suspect that every witness is the same person in a different hat. “Oswald Defence Lawyer” is a splendid example. The assassination of John F. Kennedy becomes raw material for a miniature theatrical investigation, with falsetto vocal interventions, historical debris and a jaunty musical surface that refuses to observe the solemnity expected of the subject.

The relationship with Don DeLillo's Libra is illuminating. DeLillo's novel constructs the assassination as an event in which biography, ideology, coincidence and conspiracy become almost impossible to disentangle. Smith, who had no obligation to produce a responsible historical novel, treats the same territory as an opportunity for absurdly compressed performance. The high voice is important: it makes the singer sound as though he is cross-examining himself from another room.

“Get a Hotel”, another Frenz Experiment curiosity, moves the suspicion from the presidential motorcade to temporary accommodation. The hotel is one of modern life's great spaces of displaced identity: a room that belongs to everyone and nobody, full of objects designed to look reassuringly impersonal. Marc Augé's idea of the non-place helps explain the unease, but the Fall version is less academically hygienic. Smith's hotels have the atmosphere of a place where somebody has just left in a hurry and the receptionist is refusing to say why.

There is a particularly English literary tradition behind this: Robert Aickman's lodging houses, M. R. James's seemingly ordinary rooms, the unhelpful hosts of Harold Pinter. These are places where hospitality becomes a method of surveillance. The two songs make an unlikely pair. One is about an assassination that acquired an almost infinite number of explanations; the other about finding somewhere to stay. Both involve the unpleasant discovery that the official account is inadequate.

The comic power of The Fall comes from Smith's refusal to distinguish between these levels of seriousness. A dead president and a disappointing room can occupy the same imaginative register. In both cases, somebody should probably have asked more questions at reception.

WHO OWNS THE ORIGINAL?

The covers expose another aspect of this method. The Fall's relationship with other people's songs was not reverential, and certainly not confined to the accepted canon of respectable rock influence. Frank Zappa and Wanda Jackson make particularly revealing neighbours.

Zappa's “I'm Not Satisfied”, originally recorded by the Mothers of Invention, belongs to a strain of American garage-blues complaint in which adolescent dissatisfaction is already halfway to parody. Zappa's interest in quotation, pastiche and social grotesquery makes him a plausible ancestor of Smith, but their temperaments differ. Zappa often sounds like the cleverest man in the room explaining why everyone else is ridiculous. Smith sounds like a man who has entered the wrong room and decided the entire building must be punished.

The Fall's treatment of “I'm Not Satisfied” is therefore less a tribute than a test of whether an inherited complaint can survive the loss of its original setting. A cover version is a peculiar form of possession: the singer inhabits another person's words, but the host body may be stronger than the spirit.

Wanda Jackson's “Funnel of Love”, by contrast, provides a genuinely magnificent piece of material. Its spiralling melody and dark, circular rhythmic pull were already unusual in early-sixties rockabilly. Jackson's original performance makes romantic surrender sound perilously close to being dragged down a drain. The Fall recognise the essential menace and add their own abrasions, organ textures and electronic strangeness.

The connection to rockabilly matters. Smith's use of the genre was never simple nostalgia for a lost age of authentic guitar music. Rockabilly's clipped rhythms, comic exaggerations and barely restrained aggression were already grotesque. The Fall merely removed the period costume. A Wanda Jackson song can consequently sound more contemporary in their hands than a piece of fashionable electronic production. Chronology is an unreliable guide to modernity.

It is tempting to frame this as a triumph of high art over low culture, but The Fall made such distinctions look especially foolish. The most radical act may be to discover that a three-minute pop single contains an imaginative idea more durable than a ten-minute experimental statement. An avant-garde musician can spend six months constructing a terrifying soundscape; Wanda Jackson managed to make a romantic metaphor resemble a geological disaster.

CHICKENS, NOODLES AND THE COLLAPSE OF PROPORTION

“Tommy Shooter”, from Reformation Post TLC, returns to the less reassuring end of the spectrum. Its thick, menacing guitar attack and pounding momentum suggest a group working by brute force, yet the verbal imagery is as wildly elastic as ever. Chickens, bodily transformation and casual threats become parts of a single unstable vision. The song is not a narrative about violence so much as an environment in which violence has become a grammatical habit.

The recurring poultry is worth noticing. In “2 × 4”, rustic imagery conceals a confrontation with the devil; decades later, chickens return in another threatening context. This is not evidence of a secret, unified chicken mythology. It is evidence that Smith could repeatedly extract menace from the most unpromising material. Hieronymus Bosch filled his hellscapes with animals, household objects and human bodies performing inexplicable tasks. Smith needed only a bird and an amplifier.

There is a more useful comparison with the British comic grotesque of Ronald Searle and Ken Reid: ordinary figures distorted until their physical peculiarities become an entire moral universe. The violence is excessive because excess is the joke. A reasonable punishment would not do. The knees must become noodles. The threat must become ridiculous enough to acquire the dignity of a curse.

What holds this material together is not subject matter but proportion—or rather its systematic destruction. An enormous piece of music may be built from a trivial phrase. A short garage-rock tune may imply centuries of occult history. A conspiracy can be performed as a comedy sketch; a love song can become a descent into a vortex. Smith's art was to give each idea precisely the wrong amount of importance.

THE VILLAGE HALL IS STILL OPEN

It is easy to over-intellectualise The Fall. Their records are littered with suggestive fragments, and the critic's temptation is to convert each into evidence of a coherent philosophical programme. There is no reason to assume that every strange word conceals a thesis about late capitalism. Sometimes an exhaust clip is simply an exhaust clip, even if nobody can agree what one is. Sometimes a song is funny because it is funny.

Yet the opposite mistake is equally serious. The Fall's musical grotesquery was not an excuse for laziness or incoherence. The best recordings depend upon precisely judged interactions between rhythm, texture, voice and duration. Hanley's bass does not become less intelligent because the lyric mentions a plank of wood. “And This Day” does not become meaningless because its musicians appear to be fighting their way out of a bin liner. The jokes work because the music commits to them with terrifying seriousness.

This is why the image of the murderous barn dance is so apt. A dance is a collective ritual governed by rhythm and inherited rules. The Fall keep the rhythm, alter the rules and allow the old social antagonisms to reveal themselves beneath the entertainment. Class, age, property, sex, violence and superstition all crowd into the same small room. Somebody starts a fight. Somebody else keeps playing.

The music never quite tells us whether the violence is real, imagined or simply the inevitable consequence of attending a badly organised village function. That uncertainty is the point. The ordinary has not been replaced by the supernatural. It has been shown to contain the supernatural all along.

The chairs are stacked. The lights have been switched off. The devil has gone home to his Georgian glazed porch.

And from the car park comes the unmistakable sound of somebody hitting something with a two-by-four.
