I Always Come Back to My Dark Masters

Television, counterfeit mysticism and the peculiar pleasures of being deceived by The Fall.

By Neville Counterpane

I. Britain Through the Cathode Ray Tube

The notion of Mark E. Smith as a great observer of British life requires a small qualification: a considerable proportion of the British life he observed was on television.

This is not necessarily a criticism. British television of the seventies and eighties provided an extraordinary collision of institutional authority, regional eccentricity, supernatural speculation and domestic banality. Within the space of an evening, one might encounter a supernatural disturbance in a rural community, a woman explaining how to remove stains from a carpet and a government minister attempting to justify an economic catastrophe.

Smith scarcely needed to invent surrealism. The broadcasting authorities had already established a perfectly serviceable version of it.

The ceremonial opening of ‘Lay of the Land’ belongs to the imaginative territory of Nigel Kneale's Quatermass, but its theatricality also recalls the idiotic medieval pastiche of The Young Ones. The distinction between these two cultural artefacts is considerably less important to The Fall than it might be to their respective admirers.

Kneale's sinister treatment of alternative spiritualities, the renewed enthusiasm for ley lines and the preoccupation with mystical geography among Smith's musical contemporaries provide a rich context for the song. The possibility that Smith is ridiculing these interests scarcely diminishes the possibility that he shares them.

This ambiguity is rather less successfully maintained on ‘A Lot of Wind’. Its attack on television weather forecasting identifies a familiar feature of Smith's writing: an apparently inexhaustible capacity for indignation accompanied by an equally inexhaustible appetite for the material responsible for it.

His irritation with television is frequently the irritation of a disappointed enthusiast.

By ‘I've Been Duped’, the medium has become almost indistinguishable from the world it purports to represent. Historical suffering, television personalities and the reassuring spectacle of other people's existence are assembled into a peculiar account of personal deception.

The appearance of Gretchen Franklin in ‘Telephone Thing’ becomes particularly interesting in this context. An actress associated with the reassuring familiarity of domestic entertainment is suddenly implicated in a narrative of technological persecution.

Smith's imaginative universe operated rather like a television set with a defective aerial. Signals from different channels interfered with one another, producing unexpected relationships between things that the broadcasting schedule had carefully separated.

The resulting picture was frequently more interesting than the programmes themselves.

II. Playing the Idiot

One of Smith's most productive literary devices was his willingness to inhabit characters considerably less intelligent than himself.

Unfortunately, this has encouraged a critical habit whereby every foolish utterance in The Fall's catalogue is retrospectively attributed to an invented persona. Smith's defenders sometimes display greater ingenuity in distinguishing the author from his characters than Smith displayed in constructing them.

Nevertheless, ‘Telephone Thing’ provides an excellent example of the possibilities of this approach. Its narrator's confused understanding of technological surveillance and privacy legislation sits beautifully against Coldcut's sophisticated production. The machinery appears to possess a degree of intelligence unavailable to the person complaining about it.

‘Paranoia Man in Cheap Shit Room’ develops this discrepancy into something approaching a complete dramatic miniature. Its protagonist is caught between fantasies of personal importance and the humiliating practicalities of his existence. Buying vitamin supplements becomes an activity charged with psychological significance.

Smith understands the comic possibilities of allowing a character enormous rhetorical authority whilst systematically undermining the circumstances that might justify it.

The suggestion that the man's neighbours are listening to him is particularly effective because the recording implicates its audience in the conspiracy being described.

The character's psychological deterioration becomes a form of entertainment.

There is something rather uncomfortable about encountering this material in an age when the combination of masculine isolation, technological paranoia and imaginary persecution has become an enormously profitable cultural industry.

Smith's character would no longer need to occupy a cheap room in obscurity.

He could have a subscription service.

The protagonist of ‘In the Park’ suffers from a different discrepancy between his ambitions and his expressive capabilities. His sexual enthusiasm is communicated with the rhetorical sensitivity of somebody submitting a planning application.

The song possesses the economy of flash fiction. There is a recognisable situation, a handful of physical details and a thoroughly unconvincing account of human intimacy. Its characters could belong to a particularly uncomfortable instalment of Play for Today.

The deliberately moronic guitar figure contributes to this effect. Its repetitive insistence resembles a musical equivalent of the protagonist's inability to think beyond his immediate objective.

Whether the emotional inadequacy is entirely intentional is another question. Smith's treatment of sexuality is considerably less assured than his treatment of institutional pomposity. The recording's brevity prevents its limitations from becoming excessively apparent, although its comparatively graceful guitar break suggests a more interesting arrangement than the one eventually realised.

The central question is equally applicable to ‘A Lot of Wind’. Smith can be exceptionally funny when impersonating an idiot. The joke becomes less interesting when the distance between the impersonation and the performer appears to disappear.

III. The Intelligence of Stupidity

The Fall understood something that generations of technically accomplished musicians have struggled to comprehend: a stupid riff can be an extremely intelligent compositional decision.

The guitar figure in ‘In the Park’ possesses the melodic sophistication of a children's television theme. Its effectiveness depends upon the tension established by the musicians around it. The tightly controlled percussion, the peculiar elasticity of the guitar sound and the eventual intrusion of distortion give this apparently elementary construction an unusually volatile character.

There is a similar relationship between simplicity and sophistication in ‘A Lot of Wind’, although the musicians here possess a considerably larger vocabulary. Kenny Brady's violin and the abrasive guitar textures seem almost to be competing for the same frequency range, producing a wonderfully unstable melodic surface above the metronomic rockabilly rhythm.

The whistling is an inspired touch. It introduces an almost offensively cheerful element into a performance otherwise characterised by considerable physical aggression.

The combination anticipates something of the world subsequently occupied by Country Teasers, in which apparently rudimentary popular forms become vehicles for musical abrasion and social hostility.

Smith's contribution is equally interesting. His usual tendency to slide between notes and allow syllables to deteriorate into one another gives way to a sharply articulated, almost auctioneer-like delivery. The performance is considerably more inventive than the complaint about television that provides its subject.

This distinction matters. Smith's abilities as a vocalist are frequently discussed as though they were inseparable from his abilities as a writer. The catalogue provides abundant evidence to the contrary.

Conversely, the extraordinary bass performance on ‘Telephone Thing’ demonstrates how much expressive intelligence can be invested in a comparatively economical musical figure. Steve Hanley understands that repetition establishes relationships rather than merely reiterating information. The placement, weight and duration of each note alter the significance of the percussion surrounding it.

The Fall's supposedly primitive music often requires unusually sophisticated musicians.

This is why the Gene Vincent medley is comparatively disappointing. Rockabilly was one of the sources from which The Fall developed their musical vocabulary, but returning to that source does not necessarily produce anything interesting.

The elemental rockabilly characteristics of ‘Lay of the Land’ have been subjected to far more productive distortion. Ceremonial chanting, abrasive guitars, unusually inflected bass figures and the enormous rhythmic momentum of the group create something recognisably indebted to rockabilly without being adequately explained by it.

The distinction is between musicians who understand the possibilities of primitive music and musicians who merely understand how to play it.

IV. The Production Arms Race

The early nineties presented The Fall with a problem. Several of the musical characteristics they had cultivated throughout the previous decade were becoming commercially fashionable, often in considerably less interesting forms.

The collision between guitar music and electronic dance music produced an enormous quantity of recordings whose principal achievement was demonstrating that guitars and electronic percussion could be recorded simultaneously.

‘Telephone Thing’ is considerably more successful because the relationship between Coldcut and The Fall involves a genuine exchange of musical ideas.

Comparing the recording with Coldcut's earlier ‘My Telephone’ reveals the transformative abilities of Hanley, whose bass becomes the centre of gravity around which the electronic manipulation occurs. Simon Wolstencroft's remarkably disciplined percussion gives the recording a human physicality without compromising the precision demanded by its production.

The contemporary comparison with Public Enemy is instructive. Both groups understood the capacity of abrasive, apparently incompatible sounds to reinforce a rhythmic structure rather than interfere with it.

‘Paranoia Man in Cheap Shit Room’ suggests how easily that intelligence could be diminished by an apparently more conventional approach to production.

Dave Bush's repeated synthesiser chord is arguably the most interesting instrumental element in the recording. Its displaced rhythmic accent offers possibilities that the rather generic guitar sound does little to develop.

The production is full without being particularly substantial. Everything occupies its allotted space, but the relationships between those spaces lack the imaginative character of the Coldcut collaboration.

A distorted cassette recording might genuinely improve the effect.

This creates an interesting problem when considering ‘I've Been Duped’, where the recording's relatively crude and claustrophobic qualities can be understood either as an aesthetic virtue or as evidence of insufficient care.

The temptation is to defend every such recording by reference to The Fall's historical antagonism towards conventional professionalism. But there is a considerable difference between hearing musicians occupying an uncomfortable acoustic environment and hearing musicians whose acoustic environment has simply failed to be captured.

The apparent absence of room around the instruments on some later Fall recordings deprives them of precisely the physical relationships that made earlier incarnations so formidable.

‘Tragic Days’, by contrast, contains almost nothing except acoustic environment.

Its distant guitar and incidental sounds invite the listener to imagine the physical circumstances of their production. The recording's technical limitations are responsible for much of its expressive character.

A sophisticated production can make music sound ordinary. An inadequate recording can occasionally make ordinary sounds extraordinary.

Neither outcome is guaranteed by the equipment involved.

V. Received Wisdom and Found Objects

One of the more unfortunate achievements of the British music press was its ability to persuade people that they had already heard records they had never actually listened to.

Nico provides a particularly useful example. The received description of her as a singer incapable of singing scarcely prepares the listener for the extraordinary authority of her performances on Desertshore. Her measured delivery and unusual melodic instincts are integral to the album's unsettling character.

Comparing that achievement with Eleni Poulou's performance on ‘I've Been Duped’ exposes the inadequacy of applying a single critical category to unconventional voices.

Poulou's nasal synthesisers, chant-like delivery and relatively elementary melodic construction are genuinely charming in places. Elsewhere, particularly when the chorus acquires the repetitive insistence of an enthusiastic local group's first recording, the distinction between deliberate awkwardness and actual awkwardness becomes uncomfortable.

Nico's unconventional delivery opens possibilities unavailable to a more conventional singer. Poulou's performance sometimes appears to close them.

Nevertheless, the recording possesses a distinctive identity within the muscular guitar arrangements of Imperial Wax Solvent. Its value changes considerably when understood as part of an album rather than as an autonomous song.

This is even more apparent with ‘Tragic Days’.

The recording, associated with Martin Bramah's brief return to The Fall in 1990, eventually found its way onto Levitate seven years later. Its survival introduces an element of historical displacement into an album already characterised by violent changes in texture.

The significance of such material lies partly in its refusal to maintain the homogeneous sonic identity expected of commercial albums.

Smith approached the album as a form of bricolage. Fragments, covers, incidental recordings and fully realised compositions could coexist without being required to justify themselves according to the same criteria.

Removing ‘Tragic Days’ from its surroundings and complaining that it is insufficiently developed is rather like removing a corridor from a building and complaining that it contains insufficient furniture.

The borrowing of the Planet People's chant from Quatermass at the beginning of ‘Lay of the Land’ represents another aspect of the same creative method. Material is removed from its original environment and allowed to acquire additional meanings through its new surroundings.

The Gene Vincent medley achieves comparatively little by comparison. Its source material remains perfectly recognisable, but recognisability is not itself a transformation.

The most revealing question about The Fall's appropriations concerns what happens to the material after it has been appropriated.

Borrowing is easy. Making the borrowed object strange again is considerably more difficult.

VI. The Pleasure of Being Duped

There is an inherent danger in becoming sufficiently familiar with Smith's methods that one begins to mistake every inadequacy for an innovation.

A badly recorded guitar becomes a rejection of bourgeois production values. An underdeveloped arrangement becomes an exercise in minimalism. An irritating synthesiser becomes a critique of conventional musical taste.

Sometimes it is simply an irritating synthesiser.

The Fall's admirers are particularly susceptible to this process because the group has repeatedly rewarded the abandonment of conventional critical judgements.

The apparently idiotic guitar figure in ‘In the Park’ genuinely possesses expressive qualities that a more sophisticated arrangement might have destroyed. The fragmentary recording of ‘Tragic Days’ really does contribute something valuable to Levitate. The absurd theatricality of ‘Lay of the Land’ becomes extraordinarily powerful through its relationship with the musicians.

But these achievements cannot be used to defend every comparable gesture elsewhere in the catalogue.

The intellectual demands of listening to The Fall include the ability to distinguish between an interesting failure and an uninteresting success, between an apparently incompetent performance that achieves something remarkable and an apparently incompetent performance that achieves very little.

This is particularly important when considering Smith's celebrated hostility towards contemporary culture. His complaints about television could produce the wonderfully perverse imaginative constructions of ‘Telephone Thing’ or the comparatively pedestrian observations of ‘A Lot of Wind’.

The musicians sometimes rescued material that Smith himself appeared to have exhausted.

There is something fitting about ‘I've Been Duped’ appearing within a catalogue that continually exposes the inadequacy of its admirers' critical frameworks. The apparent deception is reciprocal. Smith invites his audience to invest significance in seemingly insignificant things, whilst his audience occasionally obliges him by investing significance where there is very little to discover.

The Fall's most rewarding admirers are those who retain the capacity to be disappointed by them.

Their recordings repeatedly invite the listener to abandon the critical principles established by the previous recording.

One can spend an afternoon admiring their refusal to accommodate conventional taste and an evening wishing they had employed a competent producer.

The following morning, inevitably, one returns.

The dark masters have not necessarily produced another masterpiece.

They have simply made the alternatives seem a little less interesting.
