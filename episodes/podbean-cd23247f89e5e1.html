Before The Fall Became The Fall

By Stephen Song

1976–1981: four records, several different bands, and the invention of a musical language that had no business surviving.

Episode Nine is our first departure from the knockout competition. No song against song, no winner or loser. Instead, we return to the beginning: the formation of The Fall and the extraordinary sequence of Live at the Witch Trials, Dragnet, Grotesque (After the Gramme) and Slates. It is tempting to treat these recordings as the early chapters of a story whose ending we already know. But this retrospective certainty conceals the most interesting thing about the early Fall. For several years, nobody involved could have known what The Fall were supposed to be. And the records are magnificent precisely because they allow us to hear the group finding out.

1. Live at the Witch Trials: The Sound of Someone Starting an Argument

There is a common misconception that The Fall emerged fully formed, with Smith already dispensing cryptic pronouncements over grinding, repetitive music while an interchangeable collection of musicians attempted to keep up. Live at the Witch Trials demonstrates how misleading this picture is. The record is almost startlingly energetic. Its guitars are scratchy but lively, the rhythms frequently closer to the nervous momentum of punk than the hypnotic repetitions that would later define the group. There are moments of genuine musical exuberance, and Smith's voice possesses a youthful sharpness that makes the older singer's celebrated growl seem almost unimaginable.

Yet something is already wrong with the conventional arrangement of a rock band. Smith doesn't behave like a singer who has been given some music to perform. He sounds like a man who has arrived with an entirely different agenda and is prepared to tolerate the musicians provided they don't interfere too much.

On “Frightened”, the guitars establish a brittle, urgent atmosphere, but Smith's performance is the real disturbance. His delivery is simultaneously accusatory and evasive. The song sounds less like a confession of fear than an investigation into the conditions under which fear becomes a permanent feature of everyday life. “Rebellious Jukebox” offers a different pleasure. Its bright, almost jaunty musical movement seems at odds with Smith's increasingly distinctive verbal imagination. The jukebox itself is a wonderful object for The Fall: a machine that repeats popular culture according to instructions, dispensing approved entertainment to people who have paid for the privilege.

Then there is “Industrial Estate”, perhaps the record's most immediately recognisable declaration of intent. The song's blunt, repetitive structure transforms a familiar feature of the British landscape into something approaching a manifesto. Where much punk music imagined escape from ordinary British life, Smith seemed more interested in examining its neglected corners. He did not need to invent an alternative world. He merely needed to insist that the existing one was stranger than anyone had admitted.

The title Live at the Witch Trials is itself a splendid piece of misdirection. It is not a live album, and the witch trials are not a subject the record systematically investigates. The phrase suggests persecution, irrational authority and an audience assembled to witness punishment. It also suggests that the group has already been accused of something. Perhaps its greatest achievement is that the album makes this accusation feel justified, even though nobody can quite identify the crime.

2. Dragnet: Learning to Sound Wrong

If Live at the Witch Trials is the sound of a band discovering its possibilities, Dragnet is the sound of a band discovering how many of those possibilities can be made deliberately unpleasant. The transformation is extraordinary. The earlier record possesses a comparatively clear, lively sound. Dragnet is murkier, more claustrophobic and frequently more difficult to distinguish from the noise of its own production. This is not merely a matter of recording quality. The music has changed.

The arrival of new personnel, including Steve Hanley and Craig Scanlon, helped produce a different kind of ensemble. The group became less interested in delivering recognisable punk songs and more interested in exploring what might happen if an arrangement were allowed to continue beyond its apparent usefulness.

On “Psykick Dancehall”, repetition begins to acquire an almost supernatural character. The bass and drums establish a persistent rhythmic environment while the guitars contribute abrasive fragments. Smith's voice moves through the music like an unreliable guide describing a place he may or may not have visited. The song doesn't progress towards a conventional climax. It establishes a situation and then makes the listener remain inside it.

Rock music had long used repetition to generate excitement, but The Fall increasingly used it to alter perception. A musical phrase repeated often enough could cease to function as a riff and become part of the environment. Small variations acquired disproportionate significance. An unexpected vocal emphasis or guitar noise could feel like a major event.

“Spectre vs Rector” takes this approach into even stranger territory. Its atmosphere is genuinely unsettling, not because the group employs the elaborate theatrical devices of horror music, but because the performance seems to resist ordinary musical stability. Smith's ghostly narrative, the shifting instrumental textures and the recording's peculiar sense of space combine to produce something closer to an overheard supernatural incident than a conventional song. There is a difference between making music about ghosts and making music that behaves as though it might be haunted.

Yet Dragnet is not uniformly successful. Some of its roughness is productive; some merely sounds rough. Not every deficiency is a strategy. But the most important achievement of Dragnet is that it makes such distinctions unusually difficult. Its best moments are inseparable from the very qualities that make other passages frustrating. The Fall were learning that bad sound could become good music. They had not yet learned how to make that happen reliably. And perhaps they never would.

3. Grotesque: The North of England Becomes a Foreign Country

Grotesque (After the Gramme) is the moment when The Fall cease to sound like a group reacting to the musical culture around them and begin to sound like a group that has invented its own. The transformation is not immediately obvious in technical terms. The guitars remain abrasive, the recording is hardly luxurious, and Smith continues to deliver his words with the enthusiasm of someone correcting a particularly stupid newspaper article. But the musical imagination has expanded enormously.

“Pay Your Rates” announces the change with magnificent economy. The rhythm is tight, the guitar figures are sharply defined, and Smith's vocal has acquired a new authority. The song's bureaucratic title is another small triumph. Rates, payments, obligations: the vocabulary of municipal administration becomes the raw material for a piece of urgent, funny and faintly threatening rock music.

Then comes “English Scheme”, a song that manages to suggest an entire social order through a handful of musical and verbal gestures. The Fall's England is not the England of nostalgic pop songs or romantic national mythology. It is an England of suspicious arrangements, institutional habits, local peculiarities and people who appear to have been given authority for no particularly good reason.

“New Face in Hell” takes this imagination into more explicitly narrative territory. Its tale of intrigue, persecution and institutional menace demonstrates Smith's growing ability to construct stories that feel simultaneously precise and impossible to summarise. The rhythm has a persistent, almost procedural quality, as though the song itself were an investigation whose findings have been predetermined.

And then there is “The N.W.R.A.” For a group associated with short, abrasive songs and the pleasures of musical repetition, this extraordinary extended piece represents an astonishing expansion of ambition. Its imagined northern uprising is both comic and disturbing, a political fantasy assembled from regional grievances, cultural resentment, historical fragments and Smith's peculiar sense of the ridiculous. The song does not offer a coherent revolutionary programme. It offers the sound of a revolution being imagined by somebody who has spent too much time listening to pub conversations, reading obscure books and observing the petty absurdities of British public life.

What makes Grotesque so remarkable is the confidence with which these elements coexist. Rockabilly, post-punk, spoken narrative, political satire and the ordinary language of northern England are no longer competing influences. They have become parts of a single musical world. The Fall have not become more sophisticated by abandoning their limitations. They have discovered how to turn those limitations into a method.

4. Slates: Six Songs and No Way Back

By 1981, The Fall had developed an artistic identity so distinctive that the ordinary categories of popular music were beginning to seem inadequate. Slates is one of the clearest demonstrations of this achievement. Six tracks, too substantial to feel like an ordinary single and too compact to resemble the sprawling statements that rock music traditionally associates with artistic importance. Its very format seems faintly argumentative.

“Middle Mass” opens with an extraordinary sense of purpose. The instruments establish a dense, rhythmically insistent environment, while Smith delivers his words with the detached hostility of someone who has already decided that the people around him are beyond assistance. The music is both primitive and intricately organised. Its repetitive elements establish a system within which small disruptions acquire enormous force.

“An Older Lover Etc.” demonstrates the group's ability to transform an apparently limited musical idea into a miniature social drama. Smith's writing has become increasingly adept at implying relationships and histories without providing the explanatory material that conventional storytelling would require. Characters appear already entangled in circumstances that the listener must reconstruct.

“Prole Art Threat” is among the most exhilarating recordings of the period. Its rhythm drives forward with an almost mechanical insistence, while the guitars create a jagged, unstable surface. Smith's performance is extraordinary: urgent, fragmented and full of the sense that language itself is being forced to keep pace with the music. The title is a masterpiece of compression. Proletarian culture, artistic ambition, political anxiety and the possibility of social disruption are compressed into three words.

And “Leave the Capitol” provides an appropriately magnificent conclusion. The song's repeated injunction to depart becomes increasingly ambiguous. Is Smith demanding escape from London, from cultural authority, from a particular social environment, or from the entire arrangement of modern British life? The music refuses to settle the question. Its repetition makes the command simultaneously more urgent and more absurd.

This is The Fall at their most fully realised. The group has discovered how to make musical repetition, verbal ambiguity and social observation operate as parts of a single artistic process. Nothing needs to be explained because the explanation would diminish the experience.

5. The Band That Refused to Become Itself

The remarkable thing about The Fall's first six years is not simply the speed of their development. It is the nature of that development. Most bands begin with a limited collection of influences and gradually acquire a recognisable identity. Once that identity has been established, their subsequent career becomes a negotiation between preserving it and escaping it. The Fall seem to have approached the problem from the opposite direction. Their identity emerged through the continual rejection of whatever they had just become.

Live at the Witch Trials established a band that could operate within the language of punk while remaining suspicious of its conventions. Dragnet dismantled much of that confidence, replacing youthful energy with repetition, murk and an increasingly unsettling musical imagination. Grotesque transformed these discoveries into a coherent world of northern mythology, social observation and rhythmic obsession. Slates compressed that world into six recordings of extraordinary intensity.

Each record sounds unmistakably like The Fall. Yet none sounds quite like the one before it. This is the central paradox of the group's achievement. The Fall became themselves by refusing to remain themselves.

The personnel changes were part of this process, although they should not be romanticised. Smith's treatment of musicians could be appalling, and the group's instability frequently created difficulties that no artistic theory can excuse. Nevertheless, the changing relationships between the musicians helped prevent The Fall from becoming a fixed arrangement of familiar gestures. Steve Hanley's bass playing would become a crucial source of continuity. Craig Scanlon's guitar work helped establish the group's distinctive balance of rhythmic discipline and abrasive disruption.

The Fall were never simply Mark E. Smith accompanied by anonymous musicians. Their greatest records depended upon the tension between his extraordinary personality and the collective intelligence of the people playing alongside him. Sometimes the musicians seem to be following Smith. Sometimes Smith seems to be struggling to keep up with the musicians. And sometimes the music achieves something that none of them could plausibly have planned.

By the end of 1981, The Fall had produced a body of work that would have constituted a remarkable career for almost any other group. Yet they were still only beginning. Hex Enduction Hour was waiting just around the corner. The temptation is to describe this as the beginning of a golden age. But that would imply that the early records were valuable primarily because they led towards something better. They weren't. These recordings are not sketches for the mature Fall. They are complete, contradictory and frequently magnificent works in their own right.

The Fall were not born fully formed. They were assembled, dismantled and assembled again. And for a few astonishing years, you could hear every screw coming loose.
