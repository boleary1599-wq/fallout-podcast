I Put a Finger on the Weird

The Fall, Henry Cow and the difference between musical invention and simply behaving oddly.

F.A.L.L.O.U.T. · Critical essay

The wrong ingredients

There is something wonderfully inappropriate about The Fall covering Henry Cow. Here was a group whose musical vocabulary encompassed contemporary composition, improvisation, angular chamber music and revolutionary politics, being interpreted by a man whose attitude towards musical proficiency frequently resembled that of a particularly vindictive caretaker towards children playing football in his corridor. Yet The Fall's version of ‘War’ understands something fundamental about Henry Cow: difficulty need not be ornamental. It can be the very substance of the music.

The conventional history of progressive rock tends to divide its participants according to technical ambition. Henry Cow complicate that history because their sophistication frequently involved dismantling the pleasures that sophistication was supposed to deliver. Their arrangements interrupt themselves; instrumental voices disagree; the listener's developing understanding of a composition is liable to become redundant halfway through it. There is a significant difference between demonstrating that you can play in an unusual time signature and making the listener uncertain about where the music is taking them.

The Fall achieved comparable effects through considerably less expensive means. A toy guitar, a cassette recorder, a stubborn bass player and two drummers could produce an experience as disorientating as an elaborate Fred Frith arrangement. These were not interchangeable musical approaches, but they shared an understanding that the relationship between sounds could be more interesting than the sounds themselves.

This helps explain why some apparently insignificant Fall recordings are so rewarding, while others, equipped with all the recognisable ingredients of the group, remain stubbornly uninteresting. Weirdness is not an intrinsic property of Mark E. Smith's voice, nor is it guaranteed by a peculiar lyric or an unpleasant keyboard preset. It occurs when the various components of a recording establish an unstable relationship with one another. The Fall at their best make that instability productive.

Sunshine and sinister moonlight

‘Who Makes the Nazis?’ is one of the great demonstrations of this principle. Its guitar figure possesses an almost pastoral quality, yet the surrounding percussion refuses to let that quality settle into reassurance. The heavy toms, ragged backing voices and intrusions of recorded noise establish different acoustic perspectives. Some elements appear close enough to touch; others seem to be leaking through the wall from an entirely separate recording.

This is where Lee Scratch Perry becomes a particularly useful comparison. Perry's productions frequently allow apparently incidental sounds to acquire structural importance. The studio is not simply somewhere to document musicians performing a composition; it is an instrument capable of reorganising the listener's relationship with the performance. The relatively primitive apparatus available to The Fall on Hex Enduction Hour becomes similarly expressive. The roughness is not merely evidence of limited resources. It creates uncertainty about what belongs inside the song.

The political question at the centre of the recording is consequently delivered in a form that makes a straightforward answer extraordinarily difficult. Smith offers fragments of private behaviour, broadcasting, intellectual pretension and institutional authority. The temptation is to assemble them into a coherent political statement, but every prospective explanation seems to leave another inconvenient detail sticking out.

Imagine a television discussion in which the presenter, invited experts and members of the public have all been deprived of their explanatory context. Their competing voices survive, but the machinery that would ordinarily organise them into an intelligible debate has broken down. ‘Who Makes the Nazis?’ resembles the wreckage of such a broadcast. It is simultaneously fascinated by the question and suspicious of the people who appoint themselves to answer it.

‘Mere Pseud Mag. Editor’, from the same album, attacks a more identifiable specimen. Its rapidly delivered language and concentrated instrumental aggression make intellectual pretension sound like a physical irritant. But the relationship between the two songs is more interesting than a simple distinction between political and cultural criticism. Both are concerned with the manufacture of authority: the means by which somebody acquires the confidence to tell other people what things mean.

That The Fall themselves have subsequently generated an industry of interpretation is a particularly enjoyable historical complication. The pseud magazine editor now has a subscription to the annotated lyrics.

The devil in the provinces

One of Smith's great achievements was to make Lancashire seem as promising a location for supernatural activity as the Carpathian Mountains. Where other writers might have regarded the industrial North as fertile territory for social realism, Smith understood its potential for metaphysical unease. The ordinary landscape was already sufficiently peculiar without the introduction of a gentleman in a cape.

‘Lucifer Over Lancashire’ opens with a recorded discussion concerning the existence of God. The clipped civility of the conversation is important: this is not the theatrical invocation of a demon but an apparently mundane exchange about an extraordinary subject. Its transition into the scratchy guitars, insistent bass and Smith's improbable yodelling establishes an atmosphere somewhere between regional broadcasting and supernatural rockabilly.

There are connections here with the manifestations of evil in The Sentinel, Michael Winner's gloriously unpleasant excursion into supernatural horror. The interesting possibility is that the supernatural does not require its own geographical territory. It can occupy existing buildings, institutions and familiar environments. This is also what makes ‘The Classical’ and ‘Iceland’ so unsettling. The peculiarities of the landscape appear to have developed independently of the people describing them.

Yet ‘Lucifer Over Lancashire’ does not entirely escape the limitations of its genre. Its occult imagery occasionally feels assembled rather than discovered. The threatening musical atmosphere is established relatively quickly, after which the recording has nowhere particularly unexpected to go. Its repetition consolidates the mood rather than altering our understanding of it.

By contrast, The Fall's recording of ‘A Day in the Life’ for the 1988 Sgt. Pepper Knew My Father tribute album is peculiar precisely because of its apparent respectability. Here is Smith attempting something recognisably close to singing, accompanied by a group that has evidently taken considerable trouble to reproduce the arrangement. His little excursions into falsetto are simultaneously touching and ridiculous.

The question is whether this discrepancy between performer and material is sufficient. The Beatles' original recording is already a masterpiece of incompatible musical and narrative forms. Reproducing those forms, however admirably, is less interesting than discovering an equivalent collision within your own musical vocabulary.

The proper way to be influenced by The Beatles is not necessarily to sound like The Beatles. It might be to recognise the creative possibilities that their recordings opened up and then find some entirely inappropriate means of exploiting them. Henry Cow understood this. So, usually, did The Fall.

An embarrassment of presets

Separately considered, several of the instrumental components of ‘Powder Keg’ are almost indefensible. The keyboard fanfares possess the unmistakable flavour of an inexpensive imitation brass section. The guitar occasionally appears to have wandered in from a considerably less interesting rock group. The backing has something of the cheerful insistence of Inspiral Carpets.

Put them together, however, and you have an extraordinarily effective Fall record.

This is not simply an example of something being so bad that it becomes good. The snare has a satisfying physical presence, the synthetic brass creates emphatic rhythmic punctuation and Smith's repetitive exhortations operate as an increasingly distressed form of testimony. The individual elements might suggest uncomplicated entertainment, but their combination produces an atmosphere of agitation.

The reputation of ‘Powder Keg’ as a premonition of the 1996 Manchester bombing risks obscuring its more substantial achievement. Manchester had already experienced IRA attacks, and the anxieties associated with such events were hardly unfamiliar to people living in the North West. Smith did not require supernatural powers to recognise that a mundane journey through Manchester city centre might be violently interrupted.

What is particularly effective is the incorporation of the mundane into the potentially catastrophic. Amid the warnings and geopolitical references, a person finds himself unable to get a bus home. That seemingly trivial inconvenience is not a comic interruption of the larger subject. It is one of the means by which the larger subject becomes intelligible.

Much political songwriting makes ordinary people disappear beneath the importance of the events being described. Smith reverses the relationship. The geopolitical enters the everyday and becomes briefly indistinguishable from the business of getting home, contacting somebody or rearranging the evening.

Consider again the ludicrous keyboard sound. Its incongruous cheerfulness is essential. A conventionally ominous arrangement would have instructed us how to respond. Instead, the song maintains the momentum of a particularly enthusiastic provincial disco while Smith introduces the possibility that the evening's arrangements are about to be considerably inconvenienced.

Dinner with Mark

‘Pumpkin Soup and Mashed Potatoes’ is another matter entirely. On The Unutterable, an album capable of considerable rhythmic violence, Smith finds himself inside a little cabaret arrangement apparently designed to accompany an elderly gentleman's recollections of his favourite meals.

The wavering, breathy wind sound is particularly marvellous. Whether its precise origin is a recorder, whistle or some other means of producing an approximation of one scarcely matters to the effect. It has the slightly precarious quality of somebody attempting to entertain a small audience in a room containing a disproportionate number of antimacassars.

There is a resemblance to George Harrison's ‘Savoy Truffle’, although Harrison's confectionery catalogue becomes an increasingly elaborate joke about appetite and its consequences. Smith's food song is more difficult to place emotionally. Its insistence upon a particular combination of vegetables possesses something of the inflexibility of a private ritual.

The Fall's culinary microgenre deserves more attention. Food appears in Smith's writing not primarily as an opportunity for sensual description but as an expression of preference, intolerance, habit and sovereignty. The individual asserts control over a world of unreasonable demands by establishing what will and will not be appearing on his plate.

This makes the sentimental quality of the arrangement unexpectedly appropriate. Beneath the ridiculous specificity of the meal is an almost touching attachment to continuity. A person might be growing old, becoming increasingly irritated by the recommendations of others and finding the world generally less accommodating. The Halloween dinner remains available.

Whether this belongs halfway through an otherwise muscular Fall album is another question. Its incongruity is both its greatest asset and its principal liability. An eccentric B-side might have acquired an enthusiastic little following without interrupting anything. Its placement on The Unutterable obliges the listener to accommodate it.

But that obligation is part of the experience of listening to The Fall. A group that could move from ‘Dr. Buck's Letter’ to this absurd little supper invitation without seeking permission was still capable of jeopardising its own considerable musical achievements in the interests of something considerably less predictable.

The voice as a foreign object

By the time of ‘Victrola Time’, Smith's voice had acquired properties that would have been difficult to imagine even during the more abrasive performances of the early eighties. Words emerge from a collection of gargles, shrieks, mutterings and abrupt changes of register. His relationship with conventional pronunciation has become increasingly contractual: there are obligations on both sides, but neither party appears particularly interested in fulfilling them.

The electronic arrangement provides an appropriately unstable environment. Squelching keyboards, a breakbeat, intermittent metallic chimes and Elena Poulou's vocal contributions establish a territory somewhere between garage electronics and cheap science-fiction scoring. There is something of John Carpenter in its opening atmosphere and something of Chrome in the subsequent invasion of that atmosphere by Smith.

The recycling of material into ‘Pre-MDMA Years’ on Re-Mit demonstrates how little finality Smith attached to the conventional idea of a finished song. A lyric need not belong permanently to a particular musical setting. It could be withdrawn and introduced elsewhere, where its peculiarities might become more or less apparent.

‘Snazzy’, on the other hand, suffers from the relative stability of its accompaniment. Smith's observations about a performing group, his strange little falsetto interventions and the emphatic breakdown around the refrain suggest possibilities that the musicians rarely pursue. There is almost a successful piece of musical satire here: an aggressively ordinary backing track underneath a vocal apparently contemplating the spiritual consequences of being aggressively ordinary.

The difficulty is determining whether the ordinariness is deliberate. More importantly, does it matter? An uninspired arrangement does not acquire intelligence merely because an intelligent interpretation of it can be constructed afterwards.

This returns us to Henry Cow. At their best, the relationship between musical elements continually generated new possibilities. The Fall could achieve comparable results with a fraction of the technical apparatus. But their achievement depended upon the interactions between musicians, electronics, recorded debris and Smith himself, rather than the mere presence of an eccentric frontman.

Smith could put a finger on the weird. He could not always manufacture it single-handedly. The distinction is what separates the greatest Fall records from the merely peculiar ones.
