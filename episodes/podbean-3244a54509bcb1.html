THE DEPARTMENT OF LOST CAUSES

On the peculiar afterlife of rejected Fall songs, the dangers of musical democracy, and why the records that refuse to behave are sometimes the ones worth saving.

By Dr. Lionel C. Repechage, Visiting Professor of Unsuccessful Rehabilitation

There is a particular cruelty involved in deciding which records deserve to survive. It begins innocently enough, with a list of favourites, and soon becomes an exercise in retrospective violence. Entire periods of a musician's life are reduced to a few preferred titles. Recordings that once represented months of work are dismissed because the bass sounds peculiar, the singer is irritating, or the listener happened to be making a cup of tea when the interesting bit occurred. The Fall present an unusually serious problem for this method of judgement. Their catalogue is not simply large but wilfully uneven, full of recordings that appear to contradict the aesthetic principles established by the records immediately preceding them. To produce a definitive selection is to risk turning one of British music's least accommodating groups into a respectable heritage attraction, complete with approved masterpieces, regrettable experiments and a gift shop selling reproductions of Mark E. Smith's handwriting.

The rejected songs are therefore a useful place to begin understanding The Fall. Their history is usually told through certain familiar landmarks: the abrasive early singles, Grotesque, Hex Enduction Hour, the Brix years, the brief encounters with commercial success, the revolving personnel and the remarkable late-career persistence. These are necessary reference points, but they create an illusion of continuity that the records themselves frequently undermine. The Fall did not advance through a series of increasingly sophisticated musical solutions. They repeatedly discovered something effective, abandoned it, reinvented a less efficient version and occasionally returned years later with an approach that made the original seem timid. A recording dismissed as a minor curiosity may consequently contain a musical idea that the supposedly major works never quite managed to accommodate.

Consider “On My Own”, one of those late-eighties recordings that becomes more interesting the longer it is removed from the accepted story of the group. The familiar narrative presents this period as a collision between Smith's abrasive instincts and an increasingly accessible musical vocabulary. Yet the distinction is often too neat. The melodic elements do not necessarily soften the music; they can make its oddness more conspicuous. A tune that appears almost welcoming may be surrounded by an arrangement that refuses to settle into conventional pop architecture. This is one reason that a song can initially seem unremarkable beside the more obviously confrontational recordings and gradually acquire an authority of its own. The immediate shock has faded, leaving the subtler disturbance intact.

“Life Just Bounces” poses a related problem. It is easy to recognise the pleasures of a strong melodic hook, but less easy to explain why some repetitions become exhilarating while others merely exhaust the listener's patience. Smith's particular gift was to make repetition feel like an argument being conducted with an invisible opponent. The words return not because the song has run out of ideas but because the singer appears determined to force a point through resistance. This is closer to the logic of an obsessive comedian than that of a conventional lyricist. The joke may not become funnier when repeated, but the increasingly unreasonable insistence upon it becomes funny in itself. The Fall frequently achieved something similar with music, transforming a simple figure into an act of collective stubbornness.

The difficulty is that stubbornness can also produce genuinely dreadful results. The group's admirers sometimes approach the catalogue as though every apparent failure must conceal an overlooked masterpiece. This is an understandable response to decades of casual dismissal, but it risks becoming a form of inverted snobbery. A recording does not acquire value simply because it is unpleasant, obscure or unusually long. “Systematic Abuse” presents an especially useful test. Its extended duration and abrasive repetition can produce an almost physical sense of confinement, an effect that may be entirely appropriate to the musical conception. Yet there remains a distinction between creating an oppressive atmosphere and merely oppressing the audience. The listener is entitled to ask whether the duration develops the experience or simply increases the sentence.

Such questions were already familiar to experimental music long before The Fall appeared. La Monte Young, Tony Conrad and the musicians associated with early minimalism explored how sustained tones and repeated patterns alter perception over time. Their work does not provide a convenient intellectual justification for every monotonous rock recording. Rather, it clarifies the importance of small changes within apparently static material. The difference between hypnotic repetition and a malfunctioning washing machine can be surprisingly slight. The Fall understood this instinctively when they were at their best. A single-note bass line might become compelling through changes in drum accents, guitar texture or Smith's vocal timing. At their worst, the same method could resemble a group of musicians waiting for somebody else to suggest an ending.

“Lud Gang” belongs firmly among the more productive experiments. Its bass figure establishes a stubborn centre, but the arrangement refuses to remain a simple exercise in endurance. Different sections introduce contrasting melodic and rhythmic possibilities without entirely abandoning the original pressure. The music has the peculiar excitement of a machine that continues functioning despite several components apparently operating according to separate instructions. What makes it distinctive is not simply the presence of strong individual hooks but their uneasy coexistence. The song's changes do not produce the satisfying sense of arrival expected from conventional rock composition. They suggest that another recording has briefly broken into the room, made itself comfortable and refused to leave.

That kind of productive disorder also explains why “U.S. 80's–90's” can provoke such divided responses. Its muffled, grimy sound suggests music surviving through successive generations of imperfect reproduction, with the recording process itself becoming part of the atmosphere. There is an undeniable appeal in this texture, especially in a period when commercial production increasingly equated clarity with quality. But obscurity is not automatically complexity. A murky recording can conceal an extraordinary arrangement or merely prevent the listener from discovering how ordinary the arrangement is. The Fall were capable of both outcomes. The important question is what remains once the romance of the damaged recording has worn away.

This becomes more complicated when the rejected material includes cover versions. The Fall's repertoire is full of borrowed songs, but the conventional language of tribute is largely useless for describing their approach. They tended to treat an existing composition as a set of instructions that had arrived in the wrong envelope. “Higgle-Dy Piggle-Dy”, their interpretation of the Monks, is particularly revealing because the original group already occupied a territory of aggressive repetition, primitive rhythmic construction and deliberate musical eccentricity. The Monks' mid-sixties recordings sound like garage rock produced by an unusually militant religious order, although their famous tonsures were more theatrical provocation than spiritual declaration. The Fall's version is therefore less an act of transformation than a meeting between musicians who had independently discovered the pleasures of making a simple idea behave badly.

The question of originality becomes awkward here. The Fall's most successful covers often made familiar songs sound as though they had always belonged to Smith's strange universe. But when the source material is already thoroughly Fall-like, the possibilities for reinvention are narrower. A recording can be enjoyable, energetic and intelligently chosen without offering much that its predecessor did not. This does not make it worthless. It merely suggests that the criteria used to assess original compositions and cover versions cannot be entirely identical. A group capable of making a Hank Williams song sound like a message intercepted from an abandoned transmitter deserves credit for the achievement. It should not necessarily receive the same credit for discovering that the Monks were rather good at sounding like the Monks.

“I'm Into C.B.” illustrates a different kind of borrowing: the appropriation of an entire social world. Citizens' band radio, with its specialised language, adopted identities and opportunities for casual self-mythologising, was an ideal subject for Smith. The technology promised communication while encouraging people to perform exaggerated versions of themselves. Its culture involved handles, codes and a peculiar combination of public conversation and private fantasy. Smith's vocal inhabits this territory with considerable relish, producing a character whose enthusiasm is inseparable from his absurdity. The surrounding music supplies precisely the right amount of ungainly momentum. It is funny without being merely a novelty, partly because the song recognises something fundamental about the human desire to acquire a new identity through the purchase of equipment.

The recording also anticipates an increasingly familiar modern condition. Much of contemporary social life takes place through platforms that invite users to construct personas, adopt specialised vocabularies and develop fierce attachments to communities whose members may never meet. C.B. radio was a comparatively innocent version of the same arrangement, although the potential for misunderstanding was already considerable. Smith's fascination with such systems was not particularly technological. He was interested in what happened when ordinary people acquired a new language and began behaving as though it had transformed their social position. The song's comedy depends upon this gap between the grandeur of the adopted identity and the mundane circumstances of the person adopting it. Its protagonist is not so far removed from the small-time tyrants, provincial visionaries and self-appointed authorities who populate the rest of The Fall's catalogue.

Then there is “The N.W.R.A.”, a recording that makes the very idea of relegation seem slightly ridiculous. Its place on Grotesque (After the Gramme) is central to the album's peculiar conception of England as a landscape of regional resentment, political fantasy and grotesque theatrical performance. The title's Northern White Rubbish Army is both a satirical invention and a vehicle for genuine antagonism. Smith's relationship with northern identity was never straightforwardly celebratory. He understood the attractions of regional pride while remaining suspicious of the sentimental mythology that so often accompanies it. The song's sprawling construction allows these contradictions to accumulate without requiring a tidy resolution. It is a political epic performed by people who appear to have misplaced the official programme.

Its musical scale is equally important. The shifting sections, repeated declarations and increasingly agitated performance create the impression of an entire social movement being assembled from fragments of overheard argument. The music does not simply accompany a narrative; it gives the narrative its unstable physical form. There is something of the British comic-strip tradition in the combination of exaggerated characters, local grievances and escalating catastrophe. One might imagine a particularly unpleasant issue of Whizzer and Chips in which the inhabitants of two neighbouring towns form rival paramilitary organisations over the correct pronunciation of a bus stop. Yet the humour never entirely neutralises the hostility. The recording is funny partly because the anger beneath it is recognisable.

“Junger Cloth” offers a revealing contrast. Its appeal is less confrontational, more dependent upon atmosphere and the peculiar interaction between Smith's voice and the surrounding musical textures. The later Fall could occasionally produce recordings of unexpected tenderness, although the word should be used carefully. Smith rarely sounded interested in providing emotional reassurance. Instead, moments of beauty emerged almost accidentally from arrangements that seemed indifferent to conventional expressions of feeling. The result can be more affecting than an explicitly sentimental song because the listener is never instructed how to respond. This quality complicates the assumption that The Fall's greatness resides primarily in aggression. Sometimes their most distinctive achievement was allowing an unfamiliar mood to remain unresolved.

And then comes “Iceland”, a recording whose extraordinary atmosphere makes the language of competition seem especially inadequate. Recorded during the Hex Enduction Hour period, it suggests a group discovering music through collective attention rather than through the execution of a finished composition. The sustained instrumental movement, loose interplay and Smith's incantatory vocal produce something closer to an improvised ritual than a conventional rock performance. Its sense of place is important, but not because the music provides a picturesque description of the country in its title. The recording feels geographically displaced, removed from the ordinary social and musical environment of the group. The familiar northern English voice has been placed inside an unfamiliar landscape, where its habitual references acquire a strange new resonance.

The words are among Smith's most evocative, drawing upon images of myth, supernatural power and spiritual confrontation without settling into a coherent story. Their effect depends partly upon the relationship between his delivery and the musicians' sustained attention to the developing atmosphere. Nothing feels especially hurried. The recording appears to occupy its own time, indifferent to the commercial requirement that a song should establish its purpose quickly and depart before the audience becomes restless. It is tempting to describe this as the sound of The Fall abandoning their usual methods, but the opposite may be closer to the truth. The group are pursuing repetition, improvisation and verbal dislocation with unusual patience. The ingredients are familiar; the proportions are not.

The appeal of “Iceland” also helps explain why supposedly definitive rankings of The Fall are ultimately so unstable. The listener's priorities change. At one moment the compact brilliance of “I'm Into C.B.” may seem the group's ideal achievement: character, humour, rhythm and economy brought together with almost perfect efficiency. At another, the unwieldy political theatre of “The N.W.R.A.” appears to contain everything that makes them extraordinary. Later still, the atmospheric suspension of “Iceland” seems more valuable than either. These preferences are not necessarily contradictory. They reflect a catalogue that repeatedly challenges its audience to reconsider what it expects from music.

The rejected songs consequently perform a useful service. They expose the limitations of the categories by which reputations are constructed. Some deserve their obscurity, others reveal themselves slowly, and a few demonstrate that the distinction between masterpiece and minor work may depend upon little more than the listener's willingness to remain in the room. The Fall never produced a catalogue that could be comfortably reduced to a sequence of universally acknowledged triumphs. Its fascination lies partly in the failures, digressions, repetitions and strange survivals. To listen again to what has been discarded is not necessarily to discover that every judgement was wrong. It is to recognise that a band so resistant to ordinary standards of musical success might also require a less efficient system of justice.
