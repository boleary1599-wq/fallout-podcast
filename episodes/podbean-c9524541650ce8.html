The Bootleg as Time Machine

Poetry idiots, the republic of the riff, political theatre and the impossibility of voting on The Fall.

By Dr. C. R. Hexen, Department of Industrial Folklore (fictional byline)

A Bracket Is a Terrible Way to Listen

A tournament is an absurd way to appreciate The Fall. It asks a body of work that made a virtue of contradiction to submit to a system of clean elimination. A song that once sounded magnificent on a rain-soaked walk can lose to a track that makes more sense at eleven in the morning with five men arguing over a microphone. A great performance may be reduced to a number, while the recording that inspires the most interesting conversation is politely removed from the competition. Yet the absurdity is also the attraction. It forces the listener to explain what an instinctive allegiance actually consists of.

The episode's guest, DJ Brian Turner, has brought live recordings into the room. This changes the terms of the exercise. The usual question—what is the best recording?—is interrupted by a more slippery one: what is the work, exactly? Is it the studio track, the Peel session, a bootleg in which the introduction lasts longer than the song, or a remembered performance whose details have become inseparable from the circumstances of hearing it? The Fall were particularly well suited to such confusion. They could make a familiar number sound newly threatening simply by extending its opening, changing its pace or allowing the words to escape their original arrangement.

The three contests place “C'n'C-S Mithering” against “Words of Expectation”, “Free Range” against “High Tension Line”, and “Hollow Mind” against “Tom Raggazzi”. This is not a balanced menu of comparable objects. It is a collision of decades, production methods, lyrical temperaments and different kinds of incompleteness. The winners are not necessarily the tracks that best represent the group; they are the ones that survive a particular afternoon's argument.

John Peel's celebrated reluctance to choose among Fall records is raised during the first vote, and the difficulty is immediately apparent. The podcast is essentially trying to make a museum curator select the one object that should survive a fire. It is an exercise in critical cruelty, but the cruelty can be productive. A vote reveals the hidden standards by which each listener has been judging the music all along.

The Poetry of People Who Have Had Enough

There is an important distinction between nonsense and language that has stopped behaving itself. “C'n'C-S Mithering” is not simply a collection of funny phrases. Its references to poets, class, artistic pretension and the petty theatre of cultural authority create a world in which the insult and the social observation are almost impossible to separate. The episode returns repeatedly to the words, and with good reason: Smith's comic imagination is sharpest when a phrase seems to have escaped from an ordinary argument and become a miniature institution of its own.

“Words of Expectation” approaches verbal disorder differently. Its extended, shifting construction allows Smith to accumulate fragments rather than deliver a neat sequence of lyrical punches. The discussion notes the collage-like quality of the music and the way a bass figure and changing instrumental textures hold together what could otherwise become an endless monologue. One track seems to prosecute a case; the other seems to empty the entire filing cabinet onto the courtroom floor.

This is where the comparison with James Joyce is both useful and ridiculous. Joyce could turn the accumulated noise of a city into literature, but his admirers rarely have to vote between Ulysses and a seven-inch single after three pints. Smith's technique is not identical, of course. It shares the conviction that ordinary speech, half-remembered slogans, local irritation and apparently trivial verbal accidents can be more revealing than polished confession. The Fall's lyrical world is less a private diary than a public lavatory wall upon which somebody has unexpectedly written a theory of history.

The episode's joking about poetry, vocabulary and what listeners have learned from Smith matters because it reveals the strange educational function of this music. A Fall song can send you towards an unfamiliar word, a historical event or a phrase whose meaning keeps retreating as you approach it. Yet the pleasure does not depend on solving the puzzle. In fact, a definitive explanation might spoil the experience, like discovering that the monster in a cheap horror film is a man from accounts wearing a rubber mask.

“Free Range” and “High Tension Line” demonstrate the same verbal method under more public conditions. Their language is bound to movement, pressure, authority and threat, while the rhythms give the words a momentum that resists close inspection. Smith's phrases are not poems merely placed on top of rock music. They are disruptive elements within the machinery. Sometimes they explain the music; sometimes they behave like a spanner thrown into it.

The Republic of the Riff

The most democratic instrument in The Fall may be the bass guitar. It is frequently responsible for the part of the music that everyone can agree exists, even when they cannot agree what the singer is talking about. In the discussion of “C'n'C-S Mithering”, the hypnotic grind and the bass line become points of shared enthusiasm. Elsewhere the guests listen for the peculiar way a repeated riff can generate its own weather: the guitar dropping away, a walking bass figure appearing unexpectedly, the arrangement becoming more interesting without formally changing its subject.

“Free Range” is a useful counterexample to the idea that repetition means stasis. Its drive depends upon the pressure of the returning figure, but live versions can extend the introduction until the audience is suspended in anticipation. A three-minute song becomes a small endurance event. The rhythm does not merely support the eventual vocal entrance; it begins to constitute an independent argument. The musicians are doing something before Smith arrives, and they might be perfectly happy to keep doing it.

This is a cousin of the minimalist experiment associated with Steve Reich, although the Fall version is less likely to be performed in a concert hall where everyone knows when to applaud. It also recalls the motorik insistence of Neu!, where repetition is not a lack of motion but a way of making motion palpable. The comparison breaks down in revealing ways. The Fall's grooves are often lumpy, argumentative, liable to stumble into a different room. Their precision is social rather than mathematical: several musicians agreeing to keep the same irritation alive.

The discussion of “High Tension Line” brings out the tension between a clean, chiming guitar sound and the expectation that The Fall should remain abrasive. A relatively accessible arrangement can be heard as an achievement or as evidence that the group has strayed too close to ordinary pop. Yet there is nothing inherently conservative about a good hook. The Clash could make political panic danceable; a children's television theme can lodge in the mind with the efficiency of a military command. The question is what the repetition does once it has got inside.

A band that relies on a riff is sometimes accused of having only one idea. In The Fall's best recordings, the idea behaves more like a rumour: it spreads, changes shape, acquires new witnesses and returns with a slightly different accusation. That is why the same song can seem underdeveloped in one recording and inexhaustible in another.

Pop, Propaganda and the Wrong Uniform

The argument over “Free Range” and “High Tension Line” is partly an argument over the moral status of polish. A chiming guitar, a clean rhythmic pattern, a recognisable chorus: these are perfectly respectable musical devices, which is precisely why some Fall listeners distrust them. The suspicion is understandable. This was a group whose identity had been formed in opposition to the tidy promises of the record industry. But treating every moment of accessibility as capitulation turns aesthetic judgement into a loyalty test.

The conversation also wanders into the visual politics of “Free Range”, including the disturbing imagery associated with its video and Smith's explanation of the symbols involved. Such material cannot be treated merely as another piece of lovable eccentricity. Provocation has a history, and the meaning of an image does not disappear because its maker claims to be against the thing represented. The Fall's interest in power, European history and authoritarian spectacle is part of what makes the music compelling, but it also demands attention rather than automatic indulgence.

Here the low-budget British pop video meets the world of political theatre. Bertolt Brecht wanted the audience to remain conscious of the machinery of representation rather than sink comfortably into illusion. Smith's public persona often achieved something similar by accident or design: the performance could feel deliberately unconvincing, its gestures awkward, its authority constantly undermined. But Brecht is not a universal excuse. An image can be critically uncomfortable and still be poorly judged.

The episode's comic aside about the historical villainy of Hitler belongs to another British tradition: making the obvious sound like a daring investigative discovery. It is a joke that would not be out of place in the vicinity of Brass Eye or a particularly deranged panel show. What matters is the friction between laughter and historical unease. The Fall's music often invites that friction, placing absurd language against genuinely menacing textures.

Neither “Free Range” nor “High Tension Line” can be understood simply by deciding which sounds tougher. The former can become a monumental rock spectacle in performance; the latter can locate its unease inside a surprisingly graceful surface. The vote becomes a disagreement about what danger should sound like. Must it announce itself with distortion, or can it arrive wearing a nice shirt?

The Bootleg as Time Machine

Brian Turner's live selections give the episode its second, more interesting structure. Instead of progressing neatly through the bracket, the conversation keeps opening doors into other versions of The Fall: a session performance of “Copped It”, a live “Middle Mass”, memories of seeing the band and of discovering records in shops where such discoveries still felt like private conspiracies. These are not bonus entertainments attached to the real business. They undermine the idea that the real business can be confined to the studio catalogue.

A live recording is a form of imperfect historical evidence. The tape may be too fast, too slow, too muddy or interrupted by an audience member with an unusually powerful cough. Yet the imperfections preserve the sense of an event that was not designed for later scrutiny. Walter Benjamin's account of the artwork's aura is an obvious reference here, but The Fall make the distinction mischievous. A bootleg reproduces an unrepeatable performance, then becomes a new object with its own scratches, circulation history and mythology. The copy acquires an aura of its own.

The discussion of a live “Free Range” with an extended instrumental opening shows how much can happen before the supposedly central figure begins singing. It is an important corrective to the fantasy that The Fall were simply Mark E. Smith plus interchangeable employees. The musicians could build a physical world through repetition, and Smith's eventual arrival changed that world rather than creating it from nothing.

The guest's recollections of record shops, radio and the labour of following an obscure band also belong to the story. A Fall collection was not always something assembled by clicking a list. It could involve journeys, uncertain purchases, conversations with strangers and a growing private map of what might exist somewhere else. That experience was not necessarily more authentic than digital discovery, but it produced a different kind of attention. The record was an object, a rumour and a possible disappointment.

David Lynch's Twin Peaks offers a useful analogy: the town seems fully knowable until another corridor opens behind a familiar room. The Fall's discography works like that. A track you thought you understood is followed by a Peel session, a live arrangement, a half-remembered alternative lyric and a story from someone who was there. The song does not become clearer. It becomes larger.

That enlargement is why the live interludes belong at the centre of the essay. They reveal the Fall catalogue as a network of performances rather than a shelf of finished products. The official version is only one entrance.

A Very British Art of Losing

The final contest, between “Hollow Mind” and “Tom Raggazzi”, makes a useful mockery of the entire voting system. Neither song inspires the unanimous reverence reserved for certain canonical recordings. The conversation turns to odd textures, reggae inflections, vocoder-like treatments and the question of whether a recording is charmingly peculiar or simply not very good. It is the sort of argument that could continue indefinitely without becoming less entertaining.

The temptation is to reward obscurity for its own sake. A cult audience may take more pride in defending a neglected curiosity than in admitting that a popular song is excellent. The Fall invite this behaviour because the catalogue is large enough to make specialist knowledge feel like citizenship. But knowing the back alleys of a discography does not require pretending every alley contains a masterpiece. Some lead to a closed loading bay.

“Tom Raggazzi” wins, while the earlier rounds send “C'n'C-S Mithering” and “High Tension Line” forward. The results are less revealing than the arguments that produced them. In one round, a lyrical and rhythmic favourite survives despite the attractions of a sprawling alternative. In another, the supposedly more obvious pop choice defeats a song that inspires genuine devotion. The tournament does not produce an objective hierarchy. It records a temporary settlement between incompatible forms of affection.

The episode closes with more live music and with Turner's reflection that he is still finding things in The Fall decades after first hearing them. This is the most persuasive answer to the tournament's artificial scarcity. A bracket says that one song must advance and another must disappear. Listening says that the rejected song can return tomorrow, perhaps in a different version, perhaps with a different listener, perhaps sounding better than it has any right to.

The British genius for making a game out of an impossible question is evident here: part pub argument, part University Challenge, part village-hall raffle in which the prize is an obscure live tape. But underneath the nonsense is a serious critical principle. To love a body of work is not to rank it once and for all. It is to keep changing your mind in public.

The vitamins, as the episode title promises, were never going to change anybody's life. Another Fall recording, heard at precisely the wrong moment, just might.
