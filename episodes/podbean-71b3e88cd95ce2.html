The Official Version Is Unofficial

Stefan Cooke, The Fall Online and the struggle to preserve a band that resisted being pinned down.

By Dr. R. J. Kurious, Institute of Unauthorized Documentation

The Official Version Is Unofficial

In Twin Peaks, evidence proliferates faster than explanations. Every witness has a story, and every story opens onto another, stranger one. The Fall's history has a similar resistance to closure. There are recordings whose speed is disputed, songs that exist principally as concert tapes, and lyrics that appear to attack the people preserving them. The mystery is not who killed Laura Palmer. It is whether the Brighton tape is playing at the right speed.

The Fall Online's gigography offers a valuable counterpoint to the mythology of immediate conversion. Its creator, Stefan Cooke, was not initially won over by the group. He heard The Fall on John Peel in the seventies and saw them in 1979, but the group did not initially connect with him. Only after moving to the Boston area in 1985, and encountering the Beggars Banquet material on college radio, did his enthusiasm become obsessive. He began buying records, trading cassettes, joining FallNet and eventually organising live recordings into a gigography.

The resulting paradox is irresistible. A band that celebrated instability, personnel changes and abrupt shifts of direction inspired its listeners to construct elaborate databases, set-list histories and carefully annotated collections. Smith made disorder; the fans made footnotes. Neither enterprise would have existed in quite the same form without the other.

The archive was never a solitary achievement. Collectors exchanged recordings and information, while different early websites gradually came together. A history that might otherwise have disappeared into drawers of cassettes became a shared resource. But the better the documentation, the more questions it exposed. The official story, it turns out, is very often unofficial.

The Evidence Is Playing at the Wrong Speed

“Backdrop” produces a small crisis in musical epistemology. A widely circulated version of its Brighton live recording reportedly runs at the wrong speed, an error carried from CD into online listening. A performance long treated as a stable object suddenly becomes suspect. A slight adjustment to playback changes the apparent weight of the drums, the density of the guitars and the whole sense of momentum. The listener may have to begin the whole exercise again.

The joke conceals a serious point. A live recording is not an unmediated encounter with the past. It has passed through microphones, tape machines, duplication, mastering and playback devices. A listener can give a beautifully accurate description of the sound and still be describing an accidental distortion of the performance.

Yet the recording remains thrilling. Its enormous, dirty riff advances through feedback and clattering percussion like heavy machinery being hauled over uneven ground. Smith's voice adds a sequence of peculiar images that move between historical hallucination and comic menace. It is not polished, but the roughness creates a dramatic environment rather than simply obscuring the music.

The contrast with “The Man Whose Head Expanded” is revealing. That record has a different kind of precision: propulsive bass, bright electronics, and a controlled progression towards something darker. Its oddness is concentrated within a recognisable single. “Backdrop” sprawls; “Expanded” compresses. Neither method is automatically more experimental or more authentic. The question is what the recording requires.

This is the peculiar value of an archive. Without the tape, there is no argument about the speed. Without the argument, the listener might never realise that the familiar version is not the performance as it happened. Documentation does not merely preserve the past; it changes how the present hears it.

The Unreliable Witness Has a Very Good Bass Line

Smith's narrators often suspect that ordinary life is being manipulated by hidden forces. Scriptwriters follow them. Institutions interfere. An everyday incident turns into evidence of an elaborate conspiracy. The title of this episode invokes the black dog as a projection of the oppressor, a suitably excessive formulation for music in which psychological distress is repeatedly transformed into a hostile external agency.

“Sleep Debt Snatches” turns this anxiety into a remarkable musical experience. It begins in a comparatively familiar idiom before entering a repetitive, dislocated landscape of electronic rhythm and spidery guitar. The body is meant to sleep, but the machinery will not stop. Its ingredients suggest skiffle, primitive techno and the experimental electronics of Craig Leon. Those comparisons are useful because the track defeats any single genre description. Its disturbing effect comes from the collision.

“The Man Whose Head Expanded” performs a related trick with different materials. The futuristic textures accompany a story whose mundane social world has become paranoid and absurd. The collision is as though Coronation Street had been invaded by a chrome-plated science-fiction soundtrack; echoes of jazz-funk experiment further disturb the familiar scenery. It is precisely the friction between domestic familiarity and strange electronic sound that makes the record memorable.

Late-period “Two Librans” appears almost to reverse the procedure. Its guitar has a direct, garage-rock force, but Smith's lyrics remain stubbornly resistant to interpretation. The words resist confident decipherment, and that resistance need not be treated as a defect. The voice fits the music. That relationship is sufficient to make the recording extraordinary.

This matters because The Fall's continuity cannot be explained by pretending the backing musicians were interchangeable. The personnel and instrumental vocabulary changed repeatedly. What survived was a recurring tension: a strong musical framework meets a singer who refuses to behave like its conventional centre. Sometimes the musicians sound as though they are challenging him; sometimes he seems to be undermining their carefully constructed groove.

The instruments provide evidence that something is happening. Whether the singer's explanation can be trusted is another matter. In Smith's universe, the bass line may be the only reliable witness.

Borrowed Rhythms and Unpaid Debts

The Fall's originality did not depend upon inventing everything from scratch. Their records repeatedly borrowed, covered, dismantled and misremembered existing forms. “Why Are People Grudgeful?” belongs to reggae's tradition of reusing rhythms and remaking earlier records, with a lineage through Lee Perry and Joe Gibbs. Heard without that history, its jaunty, danceable character can seem an unusually self-contained Fall invention; the source material reveals a more complicated musical conversation.

There is no easy consensus about whether the result is delightful or intolerably cheesy. That disagreement is more interesting than a verdict. The group could approach reggae with affection while producing something that sounded awkward, excessive or wilfully silly. A cleaner imitation might have possessed less personality.

“Junk Man” presents the opposite problem: a performance so rough that its lack of preparation becomes part of its identity. The Groundhogs original is itself experimental, while the Fall version carries a reported story that Smith had the band play without the usual rehearsal or even a fresh listen. The anecdote remains a claim rather than a documented recording instruction. The Fall's clattering version, complete with kazoo, can be heard as an act of creative courage or as a failure to do the work. The answer depends upon whether the resulting disorder becomes musically productive.

“Two Librans” complicates the romantic celebration of incompetence. It is comparatively focused and forceful, with buzz-saw guitar work reminiscent of the Ramones. The band sounds purposeful, and the song loses none of its peculiar identity as a consequence. Professionalism is not the enemy; predictability is.

The same principle explains the uneven pleasures of “Job Search”. Its textures and rhythmic parts do not always seem to belong together. The collision can sound evocative, although the intrusive keyboard figure may just as easily register as an unnecessary distraction. Neither response is disqualified by the group's experimental reputation. A mistake does not become a masterpiece simply because Smith made it.

The archive makes these arguments richer. It allows listeners to compare versions, locate musical ancestors and discover that an apparently unique idea has a complicated history. Acknowledging the debts does not diminish The Fall. It reveals the ingenuity, and sometimes the recklessness, of what they did with the borrowed material.

An Insult Is a Form of Recognition

“Dedication Not Medication” makes the relationship between musician and archivist uncomfortably personal. A lyric has been interpreted as a jab at people associated with the online documentation of The Fall, including Cooke and Conway Paton. Cooke has interpreted the reference as concerning him and has described his delight at the apparent insult. Being attacked by Mark E. Smith has become an honour of a particularly perverse kind.

The absurdity is almost literary. A fan spends years preserving a musician's work, and the musician turns the fan into a character in the very work he is cataloguing. It is as though a novelist had decided to denounce the indexer of his collected writings.

The Fall Online was briefly official before reverting to unofficial status. He wanted to report news fairly and retain editorial independence. Cooke's recollection suggests that Smith disliked the loss of control over what appeared on the website, though the exact administrative reasons for the change remain uncertain. Such careful uncertainty is refreshing in a musical universe populated by unreliable narrators.

The dispute is fundamentally about authority. Who gets to tell the story of a band? Its singer, its management, its former members, or the people who recorded its concerts and assembled its history? An official website may gain a stamp of approval at the price of editorial restriction. An unofficial site can sometimes tell a more faithful story precisely because it does not need permission.

The song's own hard, mechanical groove makes the personal attack feel both absurd and strangely intimate. Its images of bodily decline, embarrassment and hostility are funny without being entirely harmless. Cooke's pleasure in being named does not mean that Smith's cruelty should be romanticised. The relationship between artist and admirer was capable of genuine affection and genuine damage.

“Job Search” offers a parallel case in the group's relationship with John Peel. Recorded as a birthday gift, it suggests a sideways expression of gratitude from a singer who was not inclined to thank his greatest champion publicly. The music is unsettled and awkward; the sentiment, if the lyric is heard as a tribute, is unexpectedly tender. Smith could make appreciation sound like a complaint and turn a compliment into something resembling an accusation.

Affection, hostility and recognition are therefore difficult to separate. The archivist becomes an enemy; the broadcaster receives an eccentric tribute; the listeners continue to love the records while arguing about the person who made them. A comfortable official biography would have difficulty accommodating all of this. The unofficial history has room for the contradictions.

The Archive Is Not the Last Word

The Fall's online archive grew from cassettes and early email lists into a network of resources that outlived the band. The gigography, lyrics, news and discussion forums were built by people who shared an obsession but did not necessarily share every opinion. Their work remains open to correction. Another tape might surface. Another date might be revised. Another witness might remember an event differently.

The recordings themselves demonstrate why that matters. “Backdrop” is a live performance whose playback history is disputed. “Sleep Debt Snatches” is a hidden recording whose strangeness rewards rediscovery. “Two Librans” is evidence that an almost entirely new line-up could make astonishing music. “Job Search” and “Dedication Not Medication” preserve contradictory forms of attachment to the people who supported and documented the group.

None of these facts produces a simple account of artistic progress. They make a more interesting kind of listening possible. A perfectly documented performance can still be dreadful; an obscure cassette can contain something magnificent. The archive can tell us what survives and how it came to survive. It cannot settle whether we should love it.

That is why the work of preservation is not opposed to The Fall's spirit of disruption. It keeps the argument alive. The group were at their best when their music refused to behave, and the listeners have honoured that achievement by refusing to stop asking what happened.

There will always be another recording, another correction, another disputed lyric, another story from somebody who was there. The evidence accumulates. The case remains open.

The owls are not what they seem.
