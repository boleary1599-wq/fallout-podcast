Imperfect in the Best Way

On rebellious machinery, musical anti-climax and the peculiar art of refusing to develop.

By J. Crackerfactory

There is a particular pleasure in watching Glastonbury with the sound off. The apparatus of communal enjoyment remains visible, but the supposedly indispensable element has been removed. Thousands of people continue to behave as though something significant is happening. The camera moves across their ecstatic faces, the performers gesticulate and the flags wave. It becomes a rather interesting documentary about the human need to participate in an event, irrespective of its content.

The Fall's comparison of free festivals with cinemas without films in ‘Two Steps Back’ operates somewhere within this territory. The apparent insult contains the possibility of an alternative entertainment. A cinema without a film might be considerably more interesting than a cinema showing a bad one. Similarly, the jukebox sidling up to a fruit machine in ‘Rebellious Jukebox’ suggests that the furniture might be having a more interesting evening than the customers.

These are not simply examples of Mark E. Smith's peculiar lyrical imagination. They suggest a way of understanding The Fall's musical arrangements, in which the supposedly incidental frequently becomes more interesting than the main attraction. The question is how far this principle can be extended before a fascinatingly unconventional composition becomes an ordinary song that somebody has neglected to finish.

The tyranny of the expected event

The military percussion of ‘The Ballad of J. Drummer’ creates an almost unbearable expectation of development. The choir-like keyboard suggests the imminent arrival of something magnificent or magnificently awful: bagpipes, a penny whistle, perhaps the massed ranks of a Salvation Army band. None materialises. The arrangement continues to circle its modest collection of ideas while Smith recounts the adventures of an outsider whose insistence upon actual drumsticks marks him as an anachronism in a world of computer tricks.

Compare this with the garage-rock stomp of ‘Jim's “The Fall”’. Here, too, the expectation of a dramatic musical intervention goes largely unfulfilled. Yet the repetition feels considerably less purposeful. The riff establishes its credentials quickly and spends much of the remaining recording reminding us of them. The difference is not necessarily one of musicianship or ambition. It is that the peculiar arrangement of ‘The Ballad of J. Drummer’ makes waiting part of the experience, whereas ‘Jim's “The Fall”’ occasionally makes waiting feel like an administrative inconvenience.

‘Two Steps Back’ offers another solution. Its descending bass figure provides a recognisable centre around which the guitar, keyboard and percussion pursue their own interests. There is something of the early Pink Floyd or the Red Crayola in the guitar's refusal to behave like a conventional lead instrument. It intervenes rather than solos. The musicians seem to possess slightly different understandings of the composition, and the friction between those understandings provides the development that the song's basic structure withholds. It anticipates the extraordinary equilibrium of ‘Winter’, where movement and stasis become almost indistinguishable.

The relatively straightforward pleasures of ‘Rebellious Jukebox’ should not be underestimated in this context. Its plinky keyboard, angular guitar figures and vocal hooks reveal a group already capable of considerable economy. Those little instrumental details anticipate some of the pleasures of American guitar pop associated with the dB's. It is an unusually plausible introduction to The Fall for somebody unfamiliar with their music, although Smith's vocal still carries traces of Johnny Rotten's particular brand of indignation.

The contrast between these early recordings is not simply one between accessibility and experimentation. Both demonstrate a sophisticated understanding of what an arrangement needs. That understanding is rather less apparent in parts of ‘Wise Ol' Man’, whose attractive psychedelic garage vocabulary occasionally threatens to become wallpaper. Yet the interplay between Smith and Elena Poulou supplies a constantly shifting foreground. The backing may remain relatively static, but the voices refuse to settle into a predictable relationship.

Repetition, then, is not itself the source of The Fall's peculiar magic. What matters is the activity taking place within it. A repeated figure can become a platform for extraordinary invention or a piece of musical furniture that nobody has bothered to move.

Pop music behaving badly

One of the more entertaining aspects of The Fall's commercial flirtations is the extraordinary disparity between what their records appear to offer and what they actually deliver. Imagine purchasing ‘There's a Ghost in My House’, attracted by its relatively immediate pleasures, and discovering ‘Haf Found Bormann’ lurking amongst the additional material. It is rather like buying a television and finding that one of the channels broadcasts exclusively from an abandoned military installation.

The phased voices, tape hiss, keyboard clusters and emphatic snare create the impression of a transmission intercepted without its accompanying explanatory documents. Its origins in Hey! Luciani provide some context, but the absence of that theatrical framework arguably enhances its peculiar atmosphere. Brix's delivery belongs to a different imaginative world from the exuberance of the single's principal attraction. The recording has affinities with the audio collage of Coil or Negativland, although it is equally possible to hear it as the sound of musicians pissing about in a studio. The two interpretations are not necessarily incompatible.

A comparable displacement occurs in miniature during ‘Loop 41 / Houston’, where electronic interference precedes the familiar contours of a country-pop composition. The effect is almost that of a radio briefly developing consciousness before remembering its contractual obligations. The transition from electronic abstraction to a recognisable song is funny, but it also changes the way the song is received. The conventional arrangement becomes another strange object in The Fall's collection.

‘Gentlemen's Agreement’ approaches the same territory from the opposite direction. Its keyboard sounds evoke the melancholy uplift of early-nineties Manchester pop, the softer edges of Happy Mondays or the less distinguished corners of Electronic's catalogue. There is something wonderfully Granada Reports about its initial atmosphere. Yet the apparent politeness of the accompaniment provides a particularly effective setting for Smith's account of an arrangement whose emotional and moral foundations have collapsed.

The invocation of computer software and the Game Boy belongs to the same imaginative universe as the computer tricks of ‘The Ballad of J. Drummer’. Consumer technology becomes a means of describing human inadequacy. Smith is remarkably good at incorporating the vocabulary of contemporary life without appearing to endorse its claims to progress. The machines in these songs are simultaneously ridiculous, threatening and revealing.

The comparison with Serge Gainsbourg is useful here. Both artists understood the possibilities of placing an uncomfortable voice within an apparently sophisticated arrangement. The piano in ‘Gentlemen's Agreement’ seems to promise strings that never quite arrive, just as the military percussion of ‘The Ballad of J. Drummer’ promises a spectacular climax that remains permanently out of reach. In both cases, the modesty of the arrangement allows the listener's imagination to supply an increasingly elaborate collection of absent instruments.

It would be easy to mistake these qualities for evidence of inadequate production. Certainly, there are moments when The Fall's recordings sound as though the musicians have been informed that the studio is closing in twenty minutes. But the more intriguing possibility is that the apparent deficiencies become part of the compositions' identities. An expensive orchestral arrangement might improve ‘Gentlemen's Agreement’ in every conventional respect while destroying the very tension that makes it worth returning to.

There is a distinction between a band attempting to make accessible music and a band using accessibility as another available texture. The Fall's relationship with pop was rarely a matter of abandoning their identity in pursuit of a larger audience. Their more approachable recordings frequently contain some of their most peculiar ideas, while their apparently experimental recordings can be surprisingly dependent upon familiar musical devices. The jukebox, after all, remains a jukebox even when it starts fraternising with the fruit machine.

The strange humanity of The Fall

For all the emphasis placed upon Smith's misanthropy, some of the most affecting moments in these recordings arise from the vulnerability of his performances. His version of ‘Houston’ is particularly revealing when placed alongside Lee Hazlewood's recording. Hazlewood's arrangement possesses a surprisingly springy quality despite the loneliness and deprivation described by the song. The Fall's version seems physically burdened by its circumstances. Where Hazlewood walks briskly, Smith trudges.

This is not necessarily a more authentic interpretation of the material, but it is a distinctly different emotional proposition. The weight placed upon individual syllables transforms a relatively faithful cover into something closer to an exhausted confession. The electronic disturbance that precedes it makes the emergence of this recognisably human predicament all the more striking.

The closing sentiment of ‘The Ballad of J. Drummer’, warning against cultivating hardness when one's heart is soft, belongs to a similarly unexpected emotional territory. It is tempting to connect the drummer's predicament with Karl Burns, although the recording does not require such a biographical interpretation to be effective. Its combination of absurdity, technological anxiety and emotional directness recalls the narrative pop records of an earlier generation, the miniature tragedies in which somebody called Johnny invariably comes to a terrible end.

There are echoes of ‘Johnny Remember Me’, alongside the peculiar suspicion that the keyboard arrangement might belong to a Christmas record. These associations should be incompatible. Instead, they contribute to the recording's strange emotional atmosphere, just as the almost comically polite piano of ‘Gentlemen's Agreement’ makes Smith's bitterness more conspicuous.

The presence of other voices is equally important. Brix's theatrical contribution to ‘Haf Found Bormann’ and Poulou's comparatively controlled delivery on ‘Wise Ol' Man’ demonstrate how much The Fall's identity depended upon personalities other than Smith's. Neither performance functions merely as decoration. Each establishes a competing centre of attention, allowing the surrounding material to acquire qualities that Smith could not necessarily generate alone.

The early accompanying vocals of ‘Rebellious Jukebox’ achieve something similar on a smaller scale. The pleasure lies partly in hearing Smith accommodated within a recognisable pop arrangement without being entirely domesticated by it. By ‘Wise Ol' Man’, the relationship has become considerably stranger. Poulou's voice supplies a degree of composure against which Smith's increasingly peculiar vocal sounds acquire their own logic. The result occasionally resembles a duet between two people receiving instructions through different earpieces.

This is why the distinction between conventional musical competence and productive awkwardness matters so much. There is no obligation to defend the comparatively beige garage-rock repetition of ‘Jim's “The Fall”’ simply because repetition elsewhere produced extraordinary results. Nor does the theatrical dislocation of ‘Haf Found Bormann’ automatically make it more rewarding than the compact pleasures of ‘Rebellious Jukebox’.

The Fall's catalogue demands a rather more flexible set of critical criteria. A recording can be interesting because of its arrangement, its atmosphere, its emotional intelligence or its sheer ridiculousness. Sometimes the most compelling recordings are those in which these qualities appear to be working against one another.

A military march that refuses to march anywhere. A country song interrupted by malfunctioning electronics. A piece of Manchester pop occupied by a disgruntled contractual negotiator. A jukebox attempting to establish diplomatic relations with a fruit machine.

Perhaps the cinema without a film was showing something after all.
