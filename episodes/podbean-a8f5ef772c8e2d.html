Imperfect in the Best Way

By J. Crackerfactory

There is a particular pleasure in watching Glastonbury with the sound off. The apparatus of communal enjoyment remains visible, but the supposedly indispensable element has been removed. The audience waves, the performers gesticulate and the camera insists upon the significance of the occasion. Without the music, the machinery becomes rather more interesting than the event it was designed to communicate.

The Fall have always been capable of producing a comparable estrangement without requiring anybody to mute the television. A familiar arrangement is permitted to proceed until an apparently minor disturbance reveals how peculiar its assumptions are. Sometimes that disturbance is a keyboard, sometimes a voice, sometimes the absence of the development that the arrangement has been promising. Sometimes it is simply the suspicion that the musicians have better things to do.

The distinction between a song that refuses to develop and one that has merely failed to develop is particularly important. It is also almost impossible to settle by consensus.

The furniture has become sentient

The early Fall possessed a remarkable capacity for animating the apparently inanimate. The jukebox and the dancehall become more than settings for human activity; they acquire a psychological existence of their own. The image of a jukebox sidling up to a fruit machine is funny because it transfers the gestures of the pub's inhabitants to the equipment that surrounds them. The furniture has begun to imitate its owners.

This is not entirely alien to the world of ordinary pub behaviour. The person who programmes the same record fifty times and leaves the premises has effectively delegated an act of antisocial behaviour to a machine. The jukebox becomes the continuing presence of somebody who is no longer there.

Rebellious Jukebox is unusually generous with its musical attractions. The little keyboard figures, the wiry guitar and the repeated vocal hooks suggest a band that could have pursued a comparatively conventional route into post-punk popularity. There are hints of the angular guitar vocabulary that would subsequently become familiar through groups such as the dB's, while the keyboard has the charming insistence of something purchased for a child and appropriated by an adult.

Yet the vocal performance still carries traces of Johnny Rotten. Smith has not entirely escaped the vocabulary of punk indignation. His later work would achieve a much more individual relationship with the material, a development already suggested by the peculiar imagery of Two Steps Back.

The latter's free festivals are compared with cinemas without films. Is this an indictment of the festival or an invitation to enjoy the possibilities created by the absence of the advertised entertainment? The ambiguity is rather more productive than a straightforward dismissal of hippiedom. A cinema without a film might be a failed cinema, but it might also be an unusually interesting place to spend an afternoon.

Decades later, the introduction to Loop 41 / Houston performs a related trick. A small electronic disturbance precedes a recognisable country-pop song, as though the radio has briefly acquired a life of its own before returning to its scheduled programming.

The tyranny of the expected event

Two Steps Back has a descending bass motif around which the other musicians seem to wander. The guitar does not behave like an instrument delivering a conventional solo. Its interventions recall the early Pink Floyd or the Red Crayola, less an exhibition of virtuosity than a disturbance of the arrangement's surface.

There is an embryonic version of the musical attitude that would produce Winter. The individual elements possess a degree of autonomy, yet the composition does not disintegrate. The drums, bass, guitar and keyboard seem to inhabit slightly different understandings of the same event.

This is a different achievement from the tightness of Rebellious Jukebox. The latter is impressive because of the economy with which it delivers its attractions. Two Steps Back is impressive because it begins to question whether those attractions are necessary.

The distinction becomes more difficult when applied to the later garage-rock material. Jim's The Fall begins with a satisfying stomp, but the expectation of a change becomes increasingly conspicuous as the song proceeds. The riff is perfectly serviceable. Indeed, it is sufficiently memorable to lend itself to inadvertent appropriation by another musician. But the repetition does not necessarily produce the accumulation of tension that makes the early material so compelling.

Wise Ol' Man encounters a similar problem. Its psychedelic garage-rock vocabulary is attractive, and the group sounds engaged with the material. Yet a break in the arrangement might have transformed a functional backing into something exceptional.

The Ballad of J. Drummer, meanwhile, is almost entirely constructed around the refusal of such a break. The military snare and choir-like keyboard create the expectation of a dramatic arrival. Bagpipes, a penny whistle or some grand cinematic flourish seem permanently imminent.

They never arrive.

The result can be heard as either a frustratingly incomplete arrangement or a miniature masterpiece of anti-climax. The important distinction is that the absence of development has become the subject of the listening experience. The listener is waiting for an event whose non-arrival constitutes the event.

The middle of the road is a dangerous place

Gentlemen's Agreement presents a different kind of provocation. Its opening keyboard sound belongs to the territory of early-nineties Manchester pop, with the melancholy uplift of a Happy Mondays arrangement or a lesser Electronic production. It is possible to hear the whole thing as a particularly earnest piece of incidental music for Granada Reports.

That does not necessarily constitute a criticism.

The comparison with Serge Gainsbourg is illuminating because the apparent sophistication of the accompaniment creates a space for a voice that is anything but conventionally reassuring. The piano may promise an arrangement of some grandeur, but the strings never quite materialise. Instead, Smith supplies a laconic account of an agreement whose emotional and moral foundations have disintegrated.

The language of contractual obligation collides with the vocabulary of personal betrayal. The extraordinary invocation of computer software and the Game Boy reduces the offending party's mental faculties to the capacities of a piece of consumer electronics. The modern world supplies the metaphors with which an ancient grievance can be expressed.

The drums replace the programmed rhythm, the guitar adds its rather anaemic shimmer and the closing passage introduces a harsher texture. The arrangement can initially seem disappointingly polite, yet repeated listening reveals the peculiar relationship between its constituent elements.

This is one of the attractions of the period's experiments with accessible pop. The musicians behave themselves just sufficiently to provide Smith with a stable surface upon which to misbehave. The melancholy house-derived atmosphere is not a disguise for a more authentic Fall song lurking underneath. It is part of the joke and part of the emotional substance.

Houston operates on a related principle. Lee Hazlewood's recording possesses a surprisingly springy quality despite its narrative of loneliness and deprivation. Smith's performance, by contrast, sounds genuinely burdened by the circumstances it describes. The band dawdles where Hazlewood's arrangement walks briskly.

The difference is not a matter of fidelity. It is a matter of temperament. A relatively faithful cover can become an entirely different proposition through the weight placed upon individual syllables.

Transmission ends

The peculiar strength of Haf Found Bormann lies in its refusal to behave like the other side of a pop single. Anyone purchasing There's a Ghost in My House in the expectation of discovering more of the same would have encountered a remarkably inhospitable collection of additional material.

Sleep Debt Snatches and Mark'll Sink Us are hardly reassuring introductions to a band's commercial aspirations. Haf Found Bormann goes further, presenting something closer to a theatrical fragment intercepted through an unreliable radio.

Its origins in Hey! Luciani help explain the dislocated narrative. Brix's spoken performance, the phased voices, tape hiss, keyboard clusters and forceful snare establish an atmosphere in which the listener seems to be receiving rather than attending a performance. The language of espionage and conspiracy is delivered without the explanatory framework that might render it immediately intelligible.

There is an affinity with the audio collage of Coil or Negativland. The piece is not necessarily going anywhere in the manner of a conventional composition. Its interest lies in the texture of the journey, the impression that fragments of a larger and potentially alarming narrative have become available through accidental interception.

It is equally possible to hear it as musicians pissing around in a studio. The distinction between the two descriptions is not as substantial as it might initially appear. Studio experimentation is capable of producing extraordinary effects without possessing a particularly extraordinary initial intention.

The same question attends the tiny electronic interruptions in Houston and the displaced backing voices of Wise Ol' Man. What might be dismissed as extraneous noise in another group's recording becomes part of the particular imaginative environment in which The Fall operate.

Voices that refuse to agree

The importance of the female voice in The Fall is particularly apparent when comparing the theatrical delivery of Brix with Elena Poulou's contributions to Wise Ol' Man. Neither is simply a decorative accompaniment to Smith.

Poulou's cool, comparatively controlled delivery creates an extraordinary counterweight to the increasingly peculiar sounds emerging from Smith. Their voices weave around one another, occasionally suggesting the intimacy of a duet while refusing its conventional emotional vocabulary. The backing is functional, but the vocal arrangement supplies the personality that the riff alone cannot sustain.

A related tension exists in the early records. The accompanying vocal in Rebellious Jukebox is one of the details that makes its arrangement so effective. Smith's presence is not necessarily enhanced by isolating him. Other voices can provide a context in which his own peculiarities become more audible.

The Ballad of J. Drummer demonstrates a different relationship between voice and accompaniment. The military percussion is sufficiently prominent to become irritating, yet Smith's delivery wraps itself around the rhythm rather beautifully. The narrative suggests an outsider arriving in a community that has forgotten something fundamental about its own existence. The insistence on actual drumsticks in a world of computer tricks is both ridiculous and strangely affecting.

The arrangement recalls old narrative records, the kind of miniature tragedy associated with Johnny Remember Me, although its refusal of melodramatic escalation makes the comparison wonderfully inappropriate. There is even something of the Christmas record about the keyboard texture. The juxtaposition of these associations with Smith's peculiar tale produces an emotional effect that none of them could achieve independently.

Imperfect in the best way

The most revealing disagreement concerns what listeners are prepared to tolerate. The tightness of a song can be an attraction, but it can also limit the possibilities of subsequent encounters. The awkwardness of another can be an obstacle that repeated listening gradually transforms into a source of pleasure.

There is no virtue in pretending that every Fall experiment succeeds. The comparatively beige garage stomp of Jim's The Fall does not automatically acquire the imaginative depth of Two Steps Back merely because both depend upon repetition. Nor does the oddness of Haf Found Bormann oblige anybody to enjoy it.

But the catalogue offers a remarkably wide range of ways in which a recording can be interesting. A song may be commercially viable, theatrically dislocated, emotionally affecting, texturally fascinating or simply funny. Sometimes it is the distance between these categories that supplies the greatest pleasure.

The unlikely proximity of Gainsbourg, a military march, a cheap synthesiser, a country cover and an intercepted radio transmission is not evidence of a band incapable of establishing an identity. It is evidence of an identity capable of accommodating radically different forms of musical experience.

The jukebox is still rebellious. It has simply become rather more difficult to predict what it will play next.
