The Grotesque Engineering of Mark E. Smith

The Fall, the fine art of musical sabotage, and the difference between a glorious mess and a rubbish record.

By Professor Roman Totale, Department of Grotesque Engineering

There is a particular sort of musician who believes that making a record properly is a moral obligation. The instruments should be tuned, the drummer should maintain a consistent tempo, the singer should know the words, and everybody should leave the studio with a reasonably clear understanding of what has been accomplished. The Fall spent forty years demonstrating the limitations of this approach. Their records contain performances that sound unfinished, arrangements that appear to have been assembled without the musicians meeting one another, and vocals whose relationship with the backing track might generously be described as diplomatic. Yet the extraordinary thing is not that Mark E. Smith made so many records that sounded wrong. It is that so many of them sounded wrong in precisely the right way.

The difficulty lies in distinguishing deliberate sabotage from ordinary incompetence. A badly played song does not automatically become an artistic statement simply because the person responsible refuses to apologise. The Fall's catalogue contains enough genuinely disappointing material to make that distinction necessary. But their finest recordings demonstrate how musical conventions can become obstacles rather than guarantees of quality. The band's greatest achievement was not simply rejecting the rules of rock music. It was discovering that some of those rules could be broken in ways that produced entirely new pleasures, while others could be ignored with results that were considerably less interesting.

The distinction was already apparent in 1979. “Dice Man”, from Dragnet, takes a familiar rock-and-roll rhythmic vocabulary and subjects it to an unusually severe examination. Its guitar figure has something of the primitive propulsion associated with Bo Diddley, but the recording is stripped of the smoothness that usually accompanies such borrowing. The instruments appear to scrape against one another, creating a rhythm that feels simultaneously ancient and newly invented. Smith's voice moves between accusation and theatrical declaration, presenting the singer as a figure whose independence is both heroic and faintly ridiculous. He is not merely refusing to follow the crowd. He seems personally offended that a crowd should exist in the first place.

The song belongs to a tradition of self-invented outsiders that stretches from nineteenth-century Romantic literature to the less reputable corners of British popular entertainment. There is something Byronic about Smith's insistence upon his own exceptional status, although Lord Byron would probably have found the rehearsal facilities unacceptable. The reference to chance also suggests Luke Rhinehart's novel The Dice Man, with its fantasy of escaping conventional identity through arbitrary decisions. Yet Smith's performance contains a contradiction. The man celebrating freedom from ordinary expectations sounds extraordinarily determined to tell everybody else what they should be doing. It is the familiar paradox of the authoritarian individualist, a character who demands absolute independence while finding other people's independence intolerable.

“It's the New Thing” approaches the same problem from a different angle. Released as a single in 1978, it attacks the machinery of musical fashion with the impatient energy of a group attempting to avoid becoming fashionable itself. The thin keyboard sound and insistent drumming give the recording an almost comically economical character. There is nothing luxurious about its production. It resembles a band that has been granted twenty minutes in a studio and intends to use every second to complain about the music industry. Yet the accusation contains its own absurdity. Here is a commercially released single condemning the mechanisms by which commercially released singles are promoted. Smith understood the contradiction perfectly well, and the record gains much of its humour from refusing to resolve it.

This was a recurring problem for punk and post-punk musicians. The demand for authenticity quickly became another marketable style, complete with approved clothing, attitudes and musical limitations. The Fall's response was to make authenticity itself unreliable. Their records could be primitive, but they were rarely innocent. Smith drew upon garage rock, literature, television, advertising, rockabilly and overheard conversation with the acquisitive instincts of somebody collecting materials for a particularly hostile scrapbook. What emerged was not an untouched expression of working-class reality but an elaborate artistic construction that deliberately retained the appearance of disorder. The music could sound accidental even when its effects were carefully judged.

By the middle of the eighties, the group's relationship with musical competence had become more complicated. Brix Smith brought a stronger melodic sensibility, greater guitar precision and an enthusiasm for pop structures that could coexist with her husband's more abrasive interests. This did not simply make The Fall better musicians. It made the differences between their competing musical instincts more productive. A recording could contain an inviting melody and a deliberately unpleasant vocal performance without either element cancelling the other. The group's increasing professionalism created new opportunities for misbehaviour, just as a well-organised school assembly provides a more satisfying setting for somebody to release a bag of frogs.

“2 × 4”, from The Wonderful and Frightening World of The Fall, illustrates this productive tension. Its compact, aggressive arrangement has the directness of a garage-rock single, but the instrumental attack is sharper and more controlled than much of the early material. Smith's delivery remains obstinate, refusing the conventional relationship between vocal melody and guitar accompaniment. The result is a recording whose precision makes its abrasiveness more effective. The band are not struggling to play the music. They are playing it well enough to make the listener aware of how unpleasant they have chosen to be.

The contrast with “Butterflies 4 Brains”, a version of the Monks' “I Hate You”, reveals the limitations of treating primitivism as an automatic virtue. The Monks were already masters of deliberate ugliness, using pounding rhythms, abrasive organ sounds and confrontational repetition to create some of the strangest rock music of the mid-sixties. Covering them presents an unusual difficulty: how does a group famous for dismantling conventional rock music transform material that has already been thoroughly dismantled? The Fall's answer involves layering voices and disturbing the original's rhythmic balance, creating a recording whose atmosphere is both sluggish and intermittently violent. The results are not always comfortable, but discomfort is clearly part of the intended effect.

The question is whether that effect is enough. There are moments when the recording's lethargy seems to deepen its menace, and others when the arrangement threatens to collapse under the weight of its own eccentricity. This is where The Fall's aesthetic becomes most difficult to defend. An artist's willingness to produce something unpleasant does not establish that the unpleasantness is worthwhile. Nor does a recording become more sophisticated simply because its pleasures are difficult to locate. The listener must still decide whether the music rewards the attention it demands. Smith's admirers occasionally behave as though the difficulty of listening is itself evidence of achievement, an argument that would make the average broken central-heating system a neglected masterpiece of industrial composition.

“Mad.Men-Eng.Dog”, from The Frenz Experiment, pushes this argument into more interesting territory. The recording initially suggests a collage of disconnected noises, voices and fragments, with the conventional structures of a rock song apparently buried beneath the debris. Yet a closer listen reveals a musical framework that has not disappeared so much as been systematically interfered with. Rhythmic patterns emerge and are disrupted; recognisable instrumental gestures are introduced and then made to sound faintly absurd. The effect resembles a song that has been subjected to an elaborate programme of administrative sabotage, with each department instructed to remove one essential component before passing the recording along.

There is a useful connection here with musique concrète, the tradition associated with Pierre Schaeffer and the manipulation of recorded sound as musical material. But The Fall's approach was considerably less academic. They were not necessarily interested in the philosophical implications of treating recorded noise as an object. They were interested in what happened when a recognisable musical structure was forced to accommodate things that did not belong inside it. The results could resemble experimental composition, comic interruption or simple bloody-mindedness, sometimes within the same minute. The important feature of “Mad.Men-Eng.Dog” is that the disorder never entirely conceals the possibility of a song. Its strange appeal depends upon that frustrated expectation.

This also places The Fall in a broader British tradition of treating failure as entertainment. From the incompetent authority figures of Dad's Army to the elaborate catastrophes of The Goon Show, much British comedy depends upon people attempting to impose order on circumstances that resist them. Smith's music often produces a comparable effect, although the comedy is more aggressive and the consequences less reassuring. Instruments behave as though they have misunderstood their instructions, while the singer continues with the confidence of somebody entirely unaware that anything has gone wrong. The listener is left to decide whether the apparent disaster has been carefully arranged or whether the band have simply developed an extraordinary ability to survive its own mistakes.

“Oxymoron”, from The Light User Syndrome, demonstrates how the same principles can produce less satisfying results. Its electronic percussion, thick instrumental textures and relatively conventional rock framework suggest a group working with a set of production choices that have not aged particularly gracefully. The problem is not simply that the sounds belong to the nineties. Plenty of recordings from that decade retain an extraordinary vitality precisely because they embrace the technological possibilities of their moment. The difficulty is that the arrangement sometimes feels less like a deliberate confrontation between incompatible elements than a collection of familiar ingredients assembled without sufficient conviction.

Smith's vocal remains recognisably strange, but strangeness alone cannot rescue a recording whose musical surroundings offer too little resistance. The Fall were often most compelling when the musicians established a strong identity against which the singer could operate. When the backing becomes generic, his interventions can sound less like acts of sabotage than attempts to distract attention from the material's limitations. This is a crucial distinction. The group's greatest recordings are not simply Mark E. Smith performances with incidental accompaniment. They depend upon the friction between his voice and musicians who have their own ideas about what the music should become.

That friction was still present in the group's final years, although it had assumed a very different character. “Black Roof”, from Sub-Lingual Tablet, contains a surprising delicacy beneath its repetitive structure. The keyboard textures introduce an almost psychedelic lightness, while the rhythm section establishes a steady movement that allows Smith's increasingly fragile vocal presence to occupy its own space. The music is neither sentimental nor especially restrained, but it possesses an openness that distinguishes it from some of the more relentlessly aggressive late recordings. There is a sense of musicians listening to one another rather than simply attempting to maintain forward momentum.

“New Facts Emerge”, from the final studio album of the same name, offers a more confrontational alternative. Pete Greenway's guitar riff belongs to the familiar vocabulary of late-period Fall rock, its repeated figure supported by forceful drumming and a comparatively direct arrangement. What makes the recording remarkable is Smith's voice, by then transformed into something far removed from the sharp, commanding instrument of the early years. His words emerge through gurgles, cries, distorted syllables and sudden bursts of apparent linguistic clarity. The vocal becomes a texture as much as a vehicle for meaning, operating alongside the instruments rather than simply delivering words above them.

It would be easy, and rather dishonest, to turn this transformation into a sentimental narrative of heroic endurance. Smith's deteriorating health was real, but illness does not automatically confer artistic value. What matters is how the recordings accommodate the voice that remained. On “New Facts Emerge”, the band's muscular repetition creates a framework within which his increasingly unpredictable delivery becomes a source of musical instability. The familiar relationship between singer and accompaniment has been reversed. The musicians provide the recognisable structure, while the voice introduces the disorder that earlier recordings sometimes achieved through the instruments themselves.

This brings us to the peculiar problem of judging The Fall across four decades. A conventional account of musical development assumes that artists acquire skills, refine their methods and eventually arrive at a more complete expression of their original intentions. Smith's career offers a less reassuring proposition. Technical improvement could produce extraordinary music, but it could also remove qualities that made earlier recordings distinctive. Primitive production could generate atmosphere, or merely obscure weak material. Deliberate sabotage could reveal unsuspected possibilities within a song, or destroy the very thing that made it worth hearing. No single standard survives contact with the entire catalogue.

The Fall's achievement was therefore not the discovery that doing things badly is preferable to doing them well. It was the recognition that the distinction is frequently less useful than musicians and critics would like to believe. Their finest records make the listener reconsider what counts as a mistake, while their weakest demonstrate that mistakes remain perfectly capable of being mistakes. The band could turn a cheap keyboard into an instrument of menace, a familiar rock riff into an act of hostility, or a damaged voice into an extraordinary source of musical colour. They could also make something tedious, ugly and pointless. The difference was never guaranteed by Smith's presence or by the group's reputation. It had to be discovered anew each time the needle touched the record.
