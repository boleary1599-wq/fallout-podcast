The Fall Were Never a One-Man Band

Mark E. Smith supplied the authority, the mythology and the magnificent nuisance. But the music’s real intelligence often resided in the people who refused — or failed — to give him exactly what he wanted.

By Dr C. R. Eep

Suppose Mark E. Smith had been forced to reduce The Fall to a duo. One accomplice, one instrument, no room for manoeuvre. Who would he have chosen? Craig Scanlon, whose guitar could make a perfectly ordinary chord sound like a structural defect? Steve Hanley, whose bass lines possessed the authority of municipal infrastructure? Brix, who introduced an almost indecent degree of melodic pleasure into the organisation? Or Mark Riley, whose principal qualification, according to one persuasive argument, was that he might annoy Smith sufficiently to make something interesting happen?

The question arises during an episode of the F.A.L.L.O.U.T. podcast, and it is rather better than the usual parlour game of assembling an ideal Fall line-up. It exposes a difficulty at the centre of the group's mythology. We are accustomed to regarding The Fall as the expression of a single extraordinary personality, a band whose members could be exchanged like defective components while the essential machinery continued operating. Smith encouraged this interpretation. So did the remarkable fact that, for four decades, the machinery generally did continue operating.

Yet listen closely to the records and a different picture emerges. The Fall were not simply Mark E. Smith accompanied by musicians. They were an argument between musical personalities, held together by an authority figure whose greatest artistic achievement may have been his inability to settle that argument.

The mythology of the dictatorial genius is attractive because it makes a complicated collective history comprehensible. Smith wanted something; the musicians supplied it; the result was The Fall. But what if the best records were made precisely when the musicians supplied something other than what he wanted? What if Smith's most productive role was not that of an architect but that of a provocateur, continually creating conditions under which other people's peculiarities became impossible to suppress?

This is not a sentimental attempt to rehabilitate every former member or distribute artistic credit according to an enlightened administrative formula. The Fall were not a cooperative. Smith's language, taste, timing and personality were indispensable. Remove him and the music becomes something else. But the reverse is equally true, and the records provide rather more evidence for that proposition than the mythology generally permits.

The difference between a great Fall record and a competent one is often the difference between musicians making a piece of music together and musicians making a plausible representation of what The Fall ought to sound like.

The music that refuses to explain itself

Consider Impression of J. Temperance, a song that makes nonsense of the idea that The Fall's early greatness resulted from an inability to play properly.

From 1980's Grotesque (After the Gramme), it is a miniature horror drama of extraordinary formal intelligence. The rhythm has the forward propulsion of a march, but it never quite settles into the reassuring symmetry that military music promises. The guitar shifts register, introducing figures that appear to comment upon the narrative rather than merely accompany it. Then Hanley's bass changes the emotional weight of the whole thing. What had seemed grotesquely comic becomes ominous, almost ceremonial.

This is composition, not incompetence. More precisely, it is composition achieved by musicians whose contributions retain their separate identities. The bass is not simply supporting the guitar, nor is the guitar dutifully illustrating Smith's words. Each part seems to possess its own understanding of the story.

The story itself concerns a monstrous birth, a hideous replica and the collapse of distinctions between the human and the animal. Smith tells it with the enthusiasm of a provincial raconteur who has discovered an especially revolting anecdote and cannot decide whether to frighten his audience or make them laugh.

That uncertainty is essential. The horror is simultaneously elaborate and ridiculous. The music behaves accordingly, approaching something almost orchestral before returning to the sound of several people making an unreasonable amount of trouble in a small room.

Mark Fisher understood Temperance as a weird tale subjected to the compressions of modernism, its narrative resembling damaged material recovered from an industrial waterway. The comparison is illuminating, not least because the record itself seems to contain several competing versions of what kind of music it ought to be. The rhythm section is conducting a march; the guitar is providing melodramatic punctuation; the keyboards introduce noises that seem to belong to an entirely different environment. Smith is telling a story whose principal events might have been better left unexplained.

The result is not chaos. It is a highly organised refusal of conventional musical hierarchy.

Compare it with Iceland, recorded during the Hex Enduction Hour period, and the same collective intelligence operates through almost opposite means.

Where Temperance continually announces its dramatic intentions, Iceland seems indifferent to whether the listener understands what is happening. The percussion moves through the recording with a loose, unhurried momentum. Discordant piano notes appear without offering the satisfaction of development. The instruments seem to occupy a physical space rather than a conventional arrangement.

Smith's voice is unusually revealing. He sounds less like a performer imposing himself upon the material than a man who has wandered into an environment already in progress. There is a peculiar calm to his delivery, an absence of the familiar antagonistic pressure.

Fisher's distinction between the weird and the eerie becomes useful here. Temperance presents an impossible presence, a thing that should not exist. Iceland creates uncertainty about agency itself. Who is responsible for these sounds? What is happening beyond the audible limits of the recording? Why does the music appear to continue according to principles that nobody has bothered to explain?

The distinction is not merely literary. It is audible in the different relationships between the instruments.

Neither record would be improved by replacing its musicians with more accomplished players instructed to reproduce the parts accurately. The point is not the notes in isolation but the individual approaches that generated them: the timing, the attack, the apparent indifference of one instrument to the priorities of another.

The Fall's greatest early recordings are full of such moments. They sound as though the musicians are cooperating without necessarily agreeing.

That is a much rarer achievement than simply playing badly.

The dictatorship of intimacy

There is another complication. The Fall's collective intelligence was not confined to the aggressive recordings that established their reputation. It could also produce moments of remarkable tenderness, although Smith generally behaved as though tenderness were a contagious condition requiring immediate treatment.

Frenz, the opening track of The Frenz Experiment, is built from remarkably little. A repetitive bass figure, spare drums, rimshots, an inexpensive-sounding keyboard and Brix's gentle backing vocals create an atmosphere that is simultaneously intimate and faintly inhospitable.

The recording has something of dub's attitude towards space. Instruments do not simply occupy positions within an arrangement; their absences become part of its rhythm. The percussion suggests an awareness of echo and delay without transforming the song into a stylistic exercise. The keyboard sounds almost embarrassingly modest, as though somebody had discovered an unsatisfactory preset and decided that replacing it would be an admission of defeat.

Yet the recording is complete. It needs no additional melodic invention, no grander arrangement, no demonstration of the musicians' abilities. Every component contributes to its atmosphere.

Smith's lyric is ostensibly about friendship, but its emotional direction is deliberately unstable. For a moment he appears to be confessing loneliness. Then the confession becomes a declaration of independence. The listener is invited to sympathise with a man who immediately suggests that his isolation may be a consequence of everyone else's inadequacy.

It is a particularly Smithian manoeuvre: the hand extended in vulnerability becomes the hand withdrawing permission.

Brix's voice is essential because it prevents this from becoming a purely rhetorical performance. Her contribution establishes a warmth that Smith can neither entirely accept nor extinguish. The arrangement allows two emotional positions to coexist without resolving their disagreement.

Here the collective character of The Fall becomes almost domestic. The song is not an argument about musical direction so much as a small drama of incompatible temperaments. Smith's voice insists upon its own interpretation; the music quietly proposes another.

It is tempting to hear this as evidence of the supposedly softening influence of Brix, but that would be another version of the solitary-genius myth, with a female collaborator reduced to a psychological function within the male artist's development. Her contribution is musical. The particular quality of her backing vocal changes the emotional possibilities of the recording. That is not therapy. It is arrangement.

The failure of The Fall's Lost in Music provides a revealing contrast. Their version of the Sister Sledge song has plenty of recognisable musical competence. The clean disco guitar, accessible rhythm and professional production are all perfectly intelligible. What is missing is a convincing reason for The Fall to be performing it.

Smith's interruptions and lyrical alterations cannot disguise the fact that much of the arrangement remains untransformed. The original's elegant rhythmic interdependence has been replaced by a serviceable indie-dance approximation. The recording knows what disco sounds like but seems less certain what to do with that knowledge.

The problem is not that The Fall have ventured into commercial territory. Their greatest music borrowed shamelessly from rockabilly, garage rock, disco, electronics and whatever else happened to be available. The problem is that borrowing only becomes artistically meaningful when the borrowed material is subjected to some imaginative pressure.

On Lost in Music, the pressure is insufficient. Smith is present, but the musical personality of The Fall seems strangely absent.

The singer has survived the transplant. The organism has not.

The strange business of playing The Fall correctly

By the late 2000s, Smith had achieved something close to the impossible: he had made The Fall into a recognisable musical institution without ever allowing them to become a stable institution in the ordinary sense. Musicians arrived, learned the repertoire, discovered the particular demands of the organisation and eventually departed. The name survived. So did the unmistakable voice, although it had become increasingly ravaged by the passage of time.

But the band's longevity created a problem that had scarcely existed in 1980. By now, The Fall possessed an established musical vocabulary. A musician joining the group could have a reasonably sophisticated idea of what a Fall song was supposed to sound like.

That knowledge was not necessarily an advantage.

Wolf Kidult Man, from Imperial Wax Solvent, offers a particularly revealing example. Its drums are powerful, its guitar parts sharply defined, its changes executed with admirable precision. The performance has the solidity of musicians who understand exactly what is required of them.

Nothing is conspicuously wrong. Indeed, almost everything is conspicuously right.

And yet something essential appears to have disappeared.

The issue is not simply that the musicians are technically accomplished. Scanlon was an exceptionally distinctive guitarist; Hanley's playing required an intelligence and rhythmic discipline that the language of punk amateurism has never adequately acknowledged. The difference lies in the relationship between technique and imagination.

Scanlon's guitar frequently sounds as though it is approaching a song from an angle that nobody else has considered. Its figures can be brittle, awkwardly placed, rhythmically obstinate. Rather than confirming the bass line, they may contradict it. Rather than supplying conventional excitement, they introduce irritation, instability or a peculiar kind of negative space.

The music becomes interesting because the players do not automatically arrive at the same conclusions.

In Wolf Kidult Man, by contrast, the instrumental parts seem to share a remarkably consistent understanding of their duties. The guitars announce the riff; the drums reinforce its authority; the arrangement proceeds through its changes with professional assurance. The resulting sound is forceful, but its force is unusually predictable.

This is where the romantic language of the amateur becomes both useful and dangerous.

It is useful because amateurism can describe an approach to music unconstrained by received professional habits. A musician may not instinctively choose the most efficient or conventional way of playing something. The resulting deviation can create a new musical possibility.

It is dangerous because the word can become a sentimental excuse for poor playing, or a means of denying the considerable skill involved in producing apparently primitive music.

The early Fall were not great because their musicians couldn't play. They were great because their musicians had developed ways of playing that resisted easy substitution.

There is a considerable difference.

The distinction also exposes the limits of Smith's authority. A bandleader can demand aggression, repetition, economy or discipline. He cannot necessarily demand the peculiar musical intelligence that makes those qualities interesting. He can tell a guitarist to play something more simply, but he cannot manufacture Scanlon's particular conception of simplicity.

Indeed, a musician who follows such instructions too faithfully may produce exactly the wrong result.

This makes the late Fall's apparent professionalism a more complicated phenomenon than decline. The band had become exceptionally good at reproducing certain qualities of The Fall. But reproducing those qualities was not always equivalent to discovering new ones.

The danger was not that the musicians had become too skilled. It was that the band's history had supplied them with too many correct answers.

The sound of an organisation losing control

And then there is (Birtwistles) Girl in Shop.

It is difficult to imagine a better corrective to the solemn mythology surrounding The Fall. The recording, associated with Spencer Birtwistle and the Protein Christmas EP, is loose, ridiculous, rhythmically infectious and apparently uninterested in satisfying the requirements of respectable rock music.

Its electronic textures suggest the cheap end of dance music rather than the elaborate machinery of experimental composition. The groove has an almost insolent simplicity. Smith's voice, increasingly slurry and disreputable, seems to occupy a separate level of consciousness from the musicians.

Yet the recording possesses something that the more accomplished Wolf Kidult Man struggles to sustain: surprise.

Its pleasure is immediate, but not exhausted by that immediacy. The roughness of the arrangement makes the groove feel discovered rather than designed. The electronic elements are not polished into an impressive display of production technique. They retain the faintly absurd character of equipment being made to do something it was probably not intended to do.

Smith's contribution is similarly ungovernable. His vocal does not appear to be directing the recording towards a coherent statement. Instead, he becomes another disruptive element within its rhythmic environment.

This is an important reversal of the conventional understanding of The Fall. Smith is not always the authorial intelligence imposing order upon the musicians. Sometimes the musicians establish a musical situation sufficiently compelling that Smith's most productive contribution is to misbehave inside it.

The result is not a triumph of chaos over craft. The groove works because somebody understands how to construct a groove. The electronic sounds are effective because their placement, duration and relationship to the rhythm have been judged. The recording's apparent casualness conceals real musical decisions.

What distinguishes it from the more professional late-period performances is the absence of any anxiety about demonstrating those decisions.

It sounds like music made by people enjoying the possibilities of the material rather than fulfilling the expectations of an established artistic identity.

That may explain why the recording feels so peculiarly liberating. By 2003, The Fall already had a formidable history behind them. They could have spent the remainder of their existence producing increasingly faithful approximations of that history. Instead, here was a recording that seemed cheerfully unaware of the burden.

The group had once again found a way of sounding unlike its own imitators.

The significance of Girl in Shop is not that it represents some definitive artistic summit. It is that its very existence makes a nonsense of the idea that The Fall's essential achievement can be located in a fixed line-up, a particular period or a stable set of musical techniques.

What matters is the process through which a recording becomes recognisably theirs without merely reproducing the characteristics of earlier recordings.

And that process is necessarily collective.

The myth of the irreplaceable man

There is an obvious objection to all this. If The Fall's greatness depended upon the contributions of their musicians, why did the group remain recognisable through so many personnel changes? Why did Smith's departure from the world in 2018 bring the organisation to an end in a way that the departures of Riley, Scanlon, Hanley, Brix and numerous others had not?

The answer is that Smith was indispensable. But indispensability is not the same as sole authorship.

He provided the continuity of taste, language and antagonism that allowed The Fall to survive successive transformations. His presence made it possible for radically different musical arrangements to be understood as expressions of a single artistic identity.

He was also the person most responsible for disrupting those arrangements.

The contradiction is fundamental. Smith's determination to maintain control helped preserve the band's independence, but it also destroyed relationships that had produced some of its greatest music. His refusal to settle into a comfortable professional routine prevented stagnation, while sometimes replacing genuinely distinctive collaborators with musicians who were better equipped to satisfy his immediate demands.

The consequences were neither uniformly productive nor uniformly disastrous. The catalogue contains masterpieces, failures, accidents, repetitions, startling recoveries and recordings whose existence remains difficult to justify. It is the history of an extraordinary artist working through a succession of equally real, if less mythologised, musical relationships.

To describe The Fall as Smith's band is therefore correct but insufficient. It identifies the organisation's centre of authority while obscuring the processes through which its music acquired meaning.

The question of the ideal two-person Fall is ultimately impossible to answer because the group's finest recordings depend upon relationships that cannot be reduced to a single partnership. Hanley's bass could transform the emotional meaning of a guitar figure. Brix's voice could introduce tenderness that Smith's lyric seemed determined to reject. Scanlon's playing could turn a simple arrangement into something resistant and unstable. Birtwistle's electronic experiments could provide an environment in which Smith became delightfully unpredictable.

None of these contributions was merely decorative. They changed what the music was capable of expressing.

The greatest Fall records are not monuments to the successful imposition of one man's will. They are documents of what happened when that will encountered other musical intelligences, sometimes accommodating them, sometimes resisting them, occasionally attempting to extinguish them.

Smith's genius was real. So was his capacity for making other people's lives difficult. Neither fact explains away the other, and neither should prevent us from hearing the musicians whose contributions made his artistic identity possible.

The Fall were never a democracy. But they were never a one-man band either.

The tragedy, and occasionally the magnificence, was that Mark E. Smith seemed determined to prove otherwise.
