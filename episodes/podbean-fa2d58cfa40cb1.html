The Fall Were Never Going to Grow Up

By Mr Pharmacist

From the bingo halls of 1978 to the disintegrating garage rock of their final years, The Fall spent four decades refusing to become the band they were supposed to be.

1. The First Fall Wasn’t Quite The Fall

There is something wonderfully unsatisfactory about “Bingo-Master’s Break-Out!”. It announces the arrival of one of the most extraordinary groups in British music by sounding like a fairly ordinary punk band attempting to become a rather less ordinary one. The guitars are thin and awkward, the rhythm section hasn’t yet discovered the peculiar pleasure of repetition for its own sake, and Mark E. Smith is still recognisably a young man with literary ambitions. He sounds as though he has something important to tell us, which is perhaps the least characteristic thing he could possibly do.

The subject, however, is magnificent. A bingo caller driven to despair by the mechanical repetition of his existence: numbers, cards, rituals, an entire life organised around a petty system of authority. It is an absurdly small tragedy, and therefore exactly the sort of tragedy Smith would spend the next forty years investigating. The bingo master is a minor functionary in a world of minor functionaries, a man whose tiny jurisdiction has become indistinguishable from his personality.

The remarkable thing isn’t that Smith noticed him. It’s that he considered him worth writing a song about.

Yet the recording itself remains curiously conventional. There is a narrative, a recognisable protagonist, even something approaching a moral. Smith is still describing the peculiarities of British life rather than constructing a musical universe in which those peculiarities become the governing laws.

By the time of “Pat-Trip Dispenser”, six years later, the transformation is astonishing. The Fall have become an entirely different proposition. The song doesn’t so much begin as materialise: an ugly, insistent piece of machinery, its guitar figures scraping against one another while Smith delivers his words with the authority of someone who has no intention of explaining himself.

The title suggests both psychedelic liberation and a particularly unpleasant piece of industrial equipment. That ambiguity is important. Where the early Fall sometimes depicted strange people doing strange things, the mid-eighties Fall increasingly made strangeness an organising principle. The music doesn’t illustrate the lyrics. It occupies the same disturbed territory.

The difference between these recordings is not simply one of sophistication. It is the difference between a band with interesting ideas and a band that has discovered an entirely personal method of making records.

Most groups improve by learning to eliminate their weaknesses. The Fall improved by discovering which weaknesses were worth keeping.

2. The Machinery of Nonsense

One of the persistent misunderstandings about The Fall is that their music is deliberately difficult. Much of it is, in fact, extraordinarily direct. The bass plays something. The drums play something alongside it. A guitar introduces a noise that may or may not constitute a riff. Smith talks, shouts or mutters over the resulting commotion. Repeat until someone decides to stop.

The difficulty lies in what happens when these simple components refuse to behave as expected.

“Noel’s Chemical Effluence” is a particularly splendid example. Its ungainly synthesiser sounds, creeping momentum and peculiar atmosphere make it resemble an industrial process that has developed a minor personality disorder. The music is almost comically unglamorous. It neither explodes nor resolves. It continues, apparently satisfied with its own unpleasantness.

There is a story, repeated in various forms, that the title concerns a tour-bus driver and the emptying of chemical toilets. Whether this is true hardly matters to the experience of the record. The title already performs its work. It combines the personal, the bureaucratic and the disgusting in three words, turning a mundane inconvenience into something resembling a scientific phenomenon.

This is one of Smith’s most characteristic achievements: the transformation of ordinary rubbish into private mythology.

A lesser writer might have made a joke about a chemical toilet. Smith could make the same material suggest a whole civilisation of petty indignities, malfunctioning equipment and people who really ought not to be trusted with responsibility.

Set against it, “It’s a Curse” demonstrates a different kind of intelligence. Here the band sounds muscular, assured and almost outrageously accessible. The guitars have weight, the rhythm has propulsion, and the whole recording possesses the confidence of a group that has learned how to turn its eccentricities into something approaching a commercial proposition.

Yet the record is no less peculiar for being comparatively welcoming.

By 1993, The Fall had achieved something that should have been impossible: they had developed a recognisable sound without developing a stable musical identity. The Infotainment Scan could enter the British album charts at number nine without requiring Smith to become a conventional rock frontman. The music had acquired a certain polish, but the personality behind it remained gloriously resistant to improvement.

This is why the familiar distinction between experimental Fall and accessible Fall is often misleading. Accessibility was simply another material the group could use, abuse and eventually discard.

The genuinely experimental thing about The Fall was not that they made difficult records. It was that they refused to accept any permanent definition of what a Fall record ought to sound like.

3. The Art of Being Slightly Rubbish

There is an argument that The Fall’s greatest achievement was their refusal to distinguish reliably between inspiration and incompetence.

This is not quite the same as suggesting that their bad records are secretly good. Some are genuinely bad. Some sound unfinished, some sound exhausted, and some give the distinct impression that nobody involved was particularly interested in what was happening.

But the group’s willingness to release such material created an unusual relationship with its audience. You could never be entirely certain whether the next record would be magnificent, dreadful or magnificent in precisely the way it was dreadful.

Consider “Scareball”. Its primitive garage-rock construction and rough recording seem almost provocatively indifferent to conventional standards. There is little sense of a band attempting to demonstrate its abilities. Instead, the music appears to be testing how little refinement a song can survive.

For some listeners, this is intolerable. For others, it is the entire point.

The Fall’s garage-rock obsession was never merely nostalgic. Smith understood that the most interesting thing about primitive rock and roll was not its simplicity but its instability. A poorly played riff can sound more threatening than a perfectly executed one because the listener is never quite certain whether the musicians are in control.

That uncertainty gives the music life.

Yet “Janet vs Johnny” offers a useful corrective to the notion that The Fall were only interested in abrasion. Its comparatively restrained, mournful atmosphere reveals a band capable of creating genuine emotional space without making a sentimental announcement about it.

The song belongs to a more elusive side of the group’s work: music that seems to have wandered into the room from another record entirely. Rather than attacking the listener, it invites them to overhear something.

There is a particular beauty in these moments because Smith rarely allows them to become comfortable. Tenderness in The Fall tends to arrive disguised as an accident. A melody emerges from the rubble, a strangely affecting phrase appears amid the usual verbal debris, and then the machinery starts up again.

The contrast between these recordings suggests that the group’s artistic freedom depended on something more complicated than a commitment to ugliness.

They could be ugly, funny, touching, tedious, thrilling and completely ridiculous, sometimes within the same song. Their refusal to arrange these qualities into a respectable hierarchy was fundamental to their achievement.

The Fall were not consistently brilliant.

They were consistently willing to risk not being brilliant, which is a considerably rarer quality.

4. The Future Is Still Misbehaving

By the time of “Get a Summer Song Goin’”, the question of whether The Fall were any good had become almost impossible to separate from the question of what anybody expected them to be good at.

The title promises seasonal pleasure, perhaps even a modest attempt at pop music. What emerges is something closer to a stubbornly repetitive rehearsal-room idea that has somehow escaped into public circulation.

It is difficult to imagine anyone mistaking it for a lost masterpiece.

Nevertheless, its very existence reveals something about Smith’s attitude to the recording process. The Fall’s catalogue was never curated with the careful self-consciousness of artists protecting their reputations. Songs appeared in unlikely places, versions multiplied, half-formed ideas survived, and material that might have been quietly abandoned by another group was allowed to become part of the official record.

There is something almost democratic about this, although Smith himself would probably have objected to the suggestion.

The catalogue contains the failures as well as the triumphs. Nothing is tidied away to make the story more flattering.

“Age of Chang” offers a rather different late-period proposition. The recording has an abrasive energy, and Smith’s ageing voice has become an instrument of considerable fascination. The younger singer’s sharp, argumentative delivery has thickened into a formidable snarl, full of gravel, impatience and unexpected rhythmic emphasis.

There is no attempt to disguise the passage of time. Smith sounds old because he is old. His voice carries the physical evidence of the years, and the band makes that evidence part of the music.

This is more interesting than the usual rock-and-roll fantasy of eternal youth.

Popular music has traditionally struggled with ageing. Performers are expected either to preserve the illusion of their younger selves or to mature into dignified elder statesmen, exchanging danger for wisdom and volume for tasteful restraint.

Smith did neither.

He became increasingly peculiar, increasingly difficult to understand and increasingly uninterested in satisfying anybody’s expectations of what an ageing rock musician should be.

The late Fall could be infuriating. Their performances could collapse, their records could sound obstinately underdeveloped, and Smith’s behaviour could make the entire enterprise seem barely sustainable.

But there was something extraordinary about watching a group continue without accepting the conventional narrative of artistic decline or respectable maturity.

The Fall never offered the comforting spectacle of a great band growing old gracefully. They grew old awkwardly, noisily and in public.

And perhaps that is the most convincing explanation of their longevity. They did not preserve a youthful rebellion like a museum exhibit. They allowed it to become older, stranger, less fashionable and occasionally less effective.

The original impulse survived precisely because it was permitted to change.

The bingo master of 1978 had been trapped by repetition, condemned to perform the same ritual until it destroyed him. Forty years later, Smith was still standing in front of a band playing repetitive music, still issuing instructions, still turning the petty absurdities of everyday life into something approaching an alternative cosmology.

The difference was that he had somehow made repetition into a means of escape.

The Fall never really grew up.

They simply found increasingly inventive ways of growing old.
