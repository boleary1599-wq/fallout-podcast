The Smith-Shaped Hole

The Fall, the dubious pleasures of completism and the difference between an unfinished song and a finished piece of rubbish.

F.A.L.L.O.U.T. · Critical essay

I. THE DUBIOUS PLEASURES OF COMPLETISM

There comes a point in the appreciation of any sufficiently prolific artist when the relationship between pleasure and obligation becomes inverted. You are no longer listening to music because you enjoy it; you are listening because you have discovered that somebody recorded it. The Fall offer almost unlimited opportunities for this particular form of self-abuse. Somewhere, on a cassette of questionable provenance, there is a performance of a song that nobody in the group subsequently thought worth performing again. Naturally, you must hear it.

The magnificent thing about The Fall's recorded history is that this archaeological impulse occasionally produces genuine rewards. The terrible thing is that it encourages you to believe that it always will. Every abandoned composition becomes a potential lost masterpiece, every instrumental rehearsal a glimpse into the creative process, every drunken assault upon somebody else's repertoire an act of radical cultural intervention. Eventually, you find yourself listening to Mark E. Smith repeatedly announcing the presence of werewolves in London and wondering whether the afternoon might have been better spent putting up some shelves.

Warren Zevon's ‘Werewolves of London’ is a particularly instructive casualty of The Fall's approach to other people's material. The original recording possesses a beautifully economical relationship between its piano figure, rhythm section and vocal delivery. Zevon understands that the ridiculousness of his subject requires an almost exaggerated musical assurance. The musicians establish an environment of considerable sophistication within which a man can discuss the dietary preferences of supernatural creatures without appearing unduly troubled by them. The Fall's performance dispenses with much of that assurance while retaining the ridiculousness. Unfortunately, ridiculousness is not something of which The Fall were experiencing a shortage.

Yet the failure of a Fall cover is rarely attributable simply to its incompetence. Their treatment of Deep Purple's ‘Black Night’, occasionally appended to ‘C'n'C-S Mithering’, is enjoyable precisely because of the enthusiasm with which they mishandle its enormous riff. Imagine The Shaggs attempting to become Metallica: Shag-tallica, a group whose technical limitations have become so intricately entangled with its musical ambitions that any improvement in proficiency might destroy the project altogether. There is a physical pleasure in hearing a familiar piece of hard-rock machinery being operated by people who appear to have received only the most cursory instructions.

The relative success of this performance exposes the limitations of more respectable efforts. The New York Dolls' ‘Jet Boy’ receives a perfectly serviceable treatment, its riff retaining something of the adolescent urgency that connects the Dolls to the MC5. ‘Race With the Devil’, performed for John Peel's fiftieth birthday, is similarly competent. The guitar sounds are broadly appropriate, the musicians understand the idiom, and the performance reaches its conclusion without anybody having to apologise. These are not inconsiderable achievements for The Fall, but they are hardly the reasons one has spent decades listening to them.

A good Fall cover establishes a peculiar relationship with its source material. ‘Victoria’ retains the exhilaration of Ray Davies's original while acquiring the peculiar forward momentum of the late-eighties Fall. ‘Mr. Pharmacist’ becomes almost inseparable from the group's own identity. Their treatment of Henry Cow's ‘War’ finds common ground between apparently incompatible musical traditions. In each case, something more interesting occurs than the transplantation of Mark E. Smith into an existing arrangement. The original composition becomes available for a different kind of musical thinking.

The Four Seasons' ‘Walk Like a Man’ provides a more literal demonstration of this process. The distance between Frankie Valli's astonishing falsetto and Smith's late-period vocal apparatus is sufficient to sustain a certain amount of comedy, but the greater interest lies in the resemblance between the group's arrangement and the subsequent ‘Breaking the Rules’. The cover becomes a kind of temporary accommodation for a musical idea that will eventually acquire different occupants. Smith's apparent disregard for the sanctity of a composition was not merely destructive. It allowed songs to remain available for further use.

II. THE ORGANISING DISTURBANCE

This brings us to the peculiar problem of The Fall without Mark E. Smith. There is an entirely understandable desire, particularly among those who have spent years listening closely to Steve Hanley, Craig Scanlon, Karl Burns and the group's numerous other musicians, to establish that The Fall were considerably more than their singer. The instrumental recordings ought, therefore, to provide an opportunity to appreciate the musicians independently of the personality that so frequently overshadowed them.

What a disappointment it is to discover that many of them sound like backing tracks.

The two instrumental pieces associated with The Unutterable possess recognisable characteristics of that album: muscular rhythmic figures, reasonably engaging interplay and the occasional suggestion of something that might develop into an interesting song. What they lack is not necessarily a lyric. They lack an organising disturbance. Smith's contributions to the finished recordings frequently establish relationships between musical events that would otherwise appear entirely unrelated. His arrival at an unexpected point in the bar, his refusal to conclude a phrase where the arrangement suggests he should, or his decision to pursue a subject seemingly unrelated to the emotional character of the music can transform an adequate instrumental performance into something considerably more complicated.

‘The Boss’, a live instrumental associated with the Reformation Post TLC period, offers a more immediately attractive proposition. Its riff possesses something of The Yummy Fur's compressed indie-rock energy, with a suggestion of the more animated end of Country Teasers. But these comparisons inadvertently expose the problem. The Yummy Fur were not interesting merely because they could produce angular, economical guitar figures. Their songs acquired much of their personality from the collision between those figures and the peculiar social and intellectual world articulated by John McKeown. Remove that element and one is left with a surprisingly ordinary proposition: some people playing indie rock quite well.

The more spacious ‘Dramatic’ presents a different difficulty. Its guitars and rhythm section establish an environment suggestive of Can or the expansive instrumental architecture associated with Godspeed You! Black Emperor. But the latter group understand that an instrumental composition of this nature requires its own internal drama. The accumulation of sound, the manipulation of duration and the eventual transformation of a musical figure are the composition, rather than preparations for somebody to sing over it. ‘Dramatic’ establishes the possibility of such a development without doing a great deal to realise it.

One can imagine Smith hearing the recording and wondering why everybody was taking so long to get to the point.

This is why ‘Outro’, the little electronic piece from The Twenty-Seven Points, is such an unexpected delight. Its mechanical pulse, sparse synthesiser sounds, guitar interference and apparent tape deterioration create a complete miniature environment. There are echoes of Suicide and Throbbing Gristle, not simply in the sounds themselves but in the understanding that a limited collection of sounds can establish an atmosphere of extraordinary specificity. The slight fluctuations in texture become events. What fades into the recording is as significant as what disappears from it.

Unlike the other instrumentals, ‘Outro’ does not seem to be waiting for Mark E. Smith. It has already acquired the qualities that his presence might otherwise have supplied: ambiguity, interruption, atmosphere and a productive uncertainty about the relationship between its constituent parts. It could sit quite comfortably among the electronic experiments of Pander! Panda! Panzer! without requiring an explanation of its presence. The Smith-shaped hole has disappeared because there is no hole to fill.

Something similar, though achieved through almost opposite means, occurs in the group's version of Nancy Sinatra's ‘The City Never Sleeps at Night’. Lucy Rimmer's vocal performance possesses a charming lightness, accompanied by keyboards whose unapologetically inexpensive sounds occasionally threaten to turn the arrangement into a demonstration of the more adventurous facilities available on a domestic synthesiser. The breathy artificial choir is particularly lovely. Lee Hazlewood's composition survives this treatment remarkably well, partly because the musicians have temporarily abandoned the obligation to sound like The Fall.

It is tempting to imagine a little single coupling this performance with the Idle Race's ‘Birthday’, another Rimmer-fronted recording. Its appeal would depend upon precisely those qualities that make it difficult to accommodate within the conventional Fall discography. There is something liberating about hearing musicians ordinarily associated with Smith's considerable demands participating in an entirely different musical enterprise. The question of whether it constitutes a great Fall recording is less interesting than the possibility that it might constitute a rather good record.

III. THE WRONG PEOPLE, THE WRONG TEMPO

The abandoned ‘Inferior Product Man’ introduces another danger of archival listening: the assumption that an earlier manifestation of an idea is necessarily inferior to its subsequent development. Its resemblance to ‘50 Year Old Man’ is apparent, but the difference in rhythmic emphasis is sufficiently substantial to make the comparison worthwhile. There is a rough, Billy Childish-like quality to the performance, with the drums establishing a relationship with the garage-rock guitar figure quite distinct from the heavier, more expansive treatment of the later song.

There is also a danger of confusing artistic development with the recycling of a successful formula. Consider the seventh series of Red Dwarf, when the departure of Rob Grant exposed the extent to which a recognisable comic vocabulary could be reproduced without necessarily generating the pleasures of the original collaboration. The constituent elements remained available. Their arrangement became less rewarding. The Fall were occasionally susceptible to an equivalent process, particularly when an accumulation of recognisable stylistic gestures failed to establish a convincing musical identity.

But their willingness to revisit material also produced some of their greatest achievements. An abandoned riff was not necessarily a failed riff; it might simply have been involved with the wrong people, the wrong tempo or the wrong set of words.

‘Surrogate Mirage’, performed in March 1982, is fascinating in this respect. Its languid guitar figure and spacious arrangement suggest a musical territory that Spacemen 3 and Spiritualized would subsequently explore at considerable length. There is a Velvet Underground-like patience to its movement, quite different from the more abrasive manifestations of the contemporary Fall. Smith's emerging lyrical imagery suggests that something interesting was developing alongside it.

What makes the recording particularly tantalising is its proximity to Hex Enduction Hour. Here is a group capable of sustaining the extraordinary duration and internal tensions of ‘Hip Priest’ and ‘And This Day’, attempting another extended piece whose possibilities remain largely unrealised. The surviving performance is not evidence of a suppressed masterpiece. It is evidence of a group whose masterpieces were emerging from a considerably wider field of experimentation than the finished albums reveal. Sometimes the experiment worked. Sometimes everybody went home and nobody mentioned it again.

At the opposite ends of The Fall's history stand two extraordinarily modest compositions. ‘Last Orders’, preserved on the 1978 Short Circuit: Live at the Electric Circus compilation, contains much of the group's fundamental musical identity before its more sophisticated characteristics had fully emerged. The energy, vocal insistence and rhythmic directness are already apparent. The language and angularity that would distinguish Live at the Witch Trials are still developing. It is recognisably The Fall without yet possessing all the reasons for which The Fall would become indispensable.

Four decades later, ‘Re-Stick’ presents the extraordinary possibility of Smith attempting to reduce his contribution to a single rapidly repeated expression. The name of a chocolate bar becomes the basis of a vocal performance. The contours of his voice, rather than the development of a literary argument, supply the variation. There is something simultaneously ridiculous and rather moving about this late experiment in reduction. A writer responsible for ‘The N.W.R.A.’ and ‘Wings’ has arrived at the possibility that one expression might be sufficient.

Between these two recordings lies an enormous body of work that has encouraged its admirers to mistake abundance for inexhaustibility. The discarded songs reveal something rather more interesting than a secret alternative Fall discography. They reveal the limits of the group's methods, the importance of its musicians, the occasional inadequacy of its singer and the remarkable precision with which those inadequacies could sometimes be made to complement one another.

And then there is ‘Outro’, quietly getting on with its business while everybody else argues about whether it is even a song.

It might be the most complete thing here.
