WHO HURT YOU, MARK? THE NORTHERN REPUBLIC AND THE ART OF BEING UNREASONABLE

On failed revolutions, Pavement’s musical larceny, bald-headed conspiracies, jungle rhythms, suburban hauntings and the strange possibility that ten minutes of amplifier noise might constitute a masterpiece.

By Dr. Maurice Hexen, Institute of Unpopular Music

There are two great traditions in rock music. The first involves musicians attempting to produce something nobody has ever heard before. The second involves musicians hearing that thing and immediately stealing it. The Fall belonged enthusiastically to both. Mark E. Smith's particular genius was not the invention of an entirely new musical language but the construction of an aesthetic in which virtually anything could be appropriated, distorted and made to behave unpleasantly. Garage rock, rockabilly, German experimental music, country, dub, disco, jungle and the contents of a particularly defective keyboard were all subjected to the same peculiar transformation.

At one extreme stands “The N.W.R.A.”, an enormous revolutionary hallucination whose ambitions approach those of an underground national epic. At the other stands “Das Boat”, a long, ominous piece of electronic experimentation whose principal achievement may be the successful removal of nearly everything conventionally understood as a song. Between them lie two varieties of winter, bald-headed conspirators, a punk cover, jungle-inflected occultism and the unsettling possibility that Smith's final domestic arrangements had become a subject of genuine artistic importance. The question is whether The Fall's most valuable quality was their ability to produce masterpieces or their refusal to distinguish reliably between a masterpiece and a load of old bollocks.

I. PAVEMENT: THE PLAGIARISTS WHO LEARNED TO DRIVE

Pavement's relationship with The Fall presents an instructive problem in musical inheritance. Their early recordings adopted enough of Smith's vocal mannerisms, jagged rhythms and wilfully disconnected lyrics to invite accusations of outright imitation. “Conduit for Sale!” makes the case almost embarrassingly easy. Stephen Malkmus delivers his words with a Smith-like combination of irritation and comic theatricality, while the band lurches through an arrangement recalling “New Face in Hell”.

Yet “Box Elder” demonstrates that Pavement were never merely a Fall tribute act. Its melodic directness and wiry guitar belong to a broader American independent-rock tradition. The Verlaines' “Death and the Maiden”, Swell Maps and Sonic Youth offer more useful coordinates than Smith alone. By Wowee Zowee, Pavement had developed a distinctive language of fractured American pop, simultaneously casual and meticulously arranged. Their capacity to produce beautiful melodies while pretending not to care was something The Fall rarely attempted in quite the same way.

Smith's irritation at their success is understandable but philosophically inconvenient. The Fall themselves borrowed liberally from Can, the Velvet Underground, the Stooges and innumerable garage-rock obscurities. Harold Bloom's The Anxiety of Influence offers one possible explanation: the younger artist struggles against the imaginative authority of a predecessor to establish an independent identity. Pavement eventually succeeded by making their borrowings part of a broader vocabulary. The distinction between influence and theft is often merely a question of who happens to own the BMW.

II. THE N.W.R.A.: THE REVOLUTION WILL BE BADLY ORGANISED

“The N.W.R.A.” is among the most extraordinary pieces of political fiction in British popular music. Its title is usually understood as “The North Will Rise Again”, although the alternative suggestion of a North-West Republican Army adds an appealingly sinister dimension. The song constructs an imagined northern uprising through declarations, characters, grotesque images and contradictory historical fragments. This is not the heroic regionalism of a conventional protest anthem. The revolution is confused, compromised and defeated.

Musically, the composition rests upon an apparently inexhaustible rhythmic foundation. The bass and drums establish a hypnotic movement while guitars and vocal fragments accumulate around them. The relationship with the Velvet Underground's “Sister Ray” is revealing: both recordings turn duration into pressure, allowing repetition to generate an increasingly unstable narrative world.

But Smith's insurrection belongs to a distinctly British literary tradition. Alasdair Gray's Lanark combines political geography with grotesque fantasy, while Michael Moorcock's Jerry Cornelius novels offer another parallel. The ostrich-feathered headdresses, underworld imagery and mysterious participants suggest magical realism stripped of its customary lyricism. Gabriel García Márquez might have recognised the method, although he would probably have requested better weather.

Smith's revolution is neither an uncomplicated socialist fantasy nor a straightforward expression of northern resentment. Its failure seems embedded within its conception. The uprising cannot escape the cultural and psychological structures it seeks to overthrow. This recalls Philip K. Dick's late fiction, in which reality becomes a prison maintained by institutions whose existence may be political, metaphysical or imaginary. The North rises. The North discovers that rising is insufficient. Somebody has already sold the television rights.

III. WINTER: A GHOST STORY WITHOUT THE COURTESY OF A GHOST

“Winter”, from Hex Enduction Hour, offers a different narrative disturbance. Where “The N.W.R.A.” announces its political mythology, “Winter” creates uncertainty through restraint, repetition and accumulating atmosphere. Its two-part structure allows the music to unfold as an extended psychological condition. Drums and bass establish a measured motion, while guitars introduce details that seem less like development than changes in temperature.

There is something peculiarly northern about its emotional geography: the sharpness of winter light, deserted streets, distant buildings and the sense that ordinary surroundings conceal information. Robert Aickman's strange stories provide a useful comparison. In “The Hospice”, the supernatural emerges through small dislocations in recognisable environments. Explanation is withheld. “Winter” achieves a similar effect through sound: something has happened, or is about to happen, without permitting the listener to identify the event. Freud's unheimlich, the uncanny, is relevant, but Smith makes the uncanny seem less like an exception than an ordinary feature of provincial existence. The ghost does not appear. The ghost has probably been there all along.

IV. THE LEAGUE OF BALD-HEADED MEN: FOLLICULAR FASCISM

“League of Bald-Headed Men”, from The Infotainment Scan, is a miniature masterpiece of paranoid social classification. Smith takes an ordinary physical characteristic and transforms it into evidence of a sinister organisation. Baldness ceases to be a biological condition and becomes an ideological affiliation.

The method resembles Thomas Pynchon's conspiracy fiction, in which apparently insignificant details reveal hidden systems of power. But Smith's conspiracy is magnificently petty. The enemy is not a multinational corporation or intelligence agency. It is a collection of men with insufficient hair. The song's muscular guitar and insistently repetitive rhythm give the absurdity almost military authority. There is something of J. G. Ballard here: the transformation of bodily characteristics into political pathology. The crucial question is not why Smith disliked bald men. It is why his dislike should require a song of such force. A minor irritation is entitled to the full resources of artistic expression.

V. SO WHAT ABOUT IT? THE BEAUTY OF INSUFFICIENT EXPLANATION

“So What About It”, from Shift-Work, is more musically generous than its title suggests. Its accessible arrangement offers melodic and rhythmic pleasures that “League of Bald-Headed Men” largely refuses. The song moves with easy confidence, allowing the instruments to establish a coherent argument even when Smith's words remain elusive.

This raises an uncomfortable question: when is lyrical ambiguity an artistic achievement, and when is it merely evidence that the writer could not be bothered? Susan Sontag's “Against Interpretation” corrects the assumption that every strange lyric conceals an elaborate symbolic structure. Sometimes the important question is not what a song means but what it does. “So What About It” works through musical movement, vocal texture and the pleasure of repeated phrases. The title itself becomes a challenge to the critic. What about it? Quite.

VI. TEN HOUSES OF EVE: JUNGLE FEVER IN THE GARDEN OF EDEN

“Ten Houses of Eve”, from Levitate, belongs to the period when The Fall's relationship with contemporary dance music became especially unstable. Its electronic percussion and jungle-inflected rhythms place Smith's voice in an environment radically different from early garage rock. Jungle's breakbeat architecture, developed by producers such as Goldie, 4hero and Remarc, depends upon rhythmic complexity, interruption and manipulation of recorded percussion. The Fall's approach is cruder, but productive.

Smith's vocal does not accommodate the electronic rhythm politely. It moves around and against it, suggesting an elderly prophet attempting to interrupt a rave. The title introduces the biblical Eve and an obscure architecture of temptation and expulsion. One might invoke the Sefer Yetzirah, with its mystical relationship between creation, language and number. Alternatively, the title may simply be another example of Smith making an arbitrary phrase sound like the name of a lost religious text. The elements do not always fit together comfortably, but the friction is more interesting than a seamless imitation of electronic music. The Fall did not need to become a jungle act. They needed to demonstrate that jungle could survive being subjected to The Fall.

VII. THIS PERFECT DAY: THE SAINTS GO TO PRESTWICH

The Saints' “This Perfect Day” is one of the great records of the original punk explosion. Released in 1977, it combines Chris Bailey's ferocious vocal with extraordinary momentum. The Fall's version, on The Marshall Suite, transforms the song without abandoning its essential structure. Where the original possesses sharp, urgent aggression, The Fall introduce a more battered, sardonic atmosphere. Smith's voice seems to have lived through decades of the disillusionment Bailey anticipates.

There is a distinction between youthful anger and experienced contempt. The Saints sound as though they have discovered that the world is intolerable. The Fall sound as though they have known this for years and are increasingly annoyed that nobody has done anything about it. Smith's finest covers relocate their sources within his imaginative universe. The song remains recognisable, but its emotional centre shifts. The perfect day has become another administrative disappointment.

VIII. SECOND HOUSE NOW: DOMESTIC REALISM AT THE END OF THE WORLD

“Second House Now”, from New Facts Emerge, collides personal history with musical grotesquery. Its rough rockabilly elements suggest familiar territory, but the arrangement refuses to settle into straightforward revival. The title invites a literal reading: Smith's later domestic life, including his affection for home and garden, provides unexpectedly intimate context. The connection with “My New House”, from This Nation's Saving Grace, is suggestive.

A house is never simply a house. It is occupation, identity, memory and potential haunting. Gaston Bachelard's The Poetics of Space examines the house as a repository of imagination and personal history. Smith's houses are less reassuring. They seem permanently vulnerable to ghosts, social obligations and the inconvenience of other people. The music's uneven construction mirrors this instability. Domestic security becomes another form of strangeness.

IX. DAS BOAT: KOSMISCHE MUSIK FOR THE TERMINALLY IMPATIENT

“Das Boat”, from Reformation Post TLC, is an unexpectedly persuasive argument for The Fall as an experimental electronic group. Its extended duration, dark synthesised textures, throbbing low frequencies and fragmentary vocals place it closer to early Cluster than conventional rock. The comparison with Neu! is tempting but insufficient. Neu!'s motorik rhythm depends upon forward propulsion. “Das Boat” is more static and oppressive. It creates a landscape rather than a journey.

The early recordings of Hans-Joachim Roedelius and Dieter Moebius provide a more useful parallel: dark, unstable environments where repetition and texture replace conventional melody. There are traces of the BBC Radiophonic Workshop, particularly Delia Derbyshire's transformation of electronic sound into psychological disturbance. An incongruous guitar figure introduces almost Pink Floyd-like melodrama, as though David Gilmour has wandered into the laboratory and been told to make himself useful.

The result is not entirely successful. Its experimental elements sometimes seem assembled rather than integrated. Yet the refusal to develop conventionally becomes hypnotic. Can's “Yoo Doo Right” is another useful reference: Malcolm Mooney's improvisations demonstrate how repetition creates a trance in which words cease to function primarily as semantic units. Smith's vocal interventions approach the same territory. The voice becomes a physical event.

The title evokes Wolfgang Petersen's Das Boot, though the relationship with submarine warfare is atmospheric rather than literal. Imagine an underwater expedition whose crew have forgotten why they left port. The sonar has failed. The captain is arguing with a synthesiser. Nobody is prepared to surface.

X. WHO HURT YOU, MARK?

The remarkable thing about these recordings is not their diversity but the consistency of the imaginative method underlying them. “The N.W.R.A.” constructs political mythology from repetition and grievance. “Winter” turns landscape into psychological horror. “League of Bald-Headed Men” transforms a minor physical characteristic into conspiracy. “Ten Houses of Eve” places occult imagery inside dance music. “Second House Now” makes domestic security threatening. “Das Boat” discovers a different form of Fall music in German experimental electronics.

These are not unrelated eccentricities. They are variations upon one principle: the ordinary world is never quite what it claims to be. Smith's great subject was not politics, literature, class, the supernatural or popular music, although all supplied material. His subject was the instability of interpretation itself. A song could be a manifesto, a private joke, musical sabotage and an apparently meaningless collection of phrases simultaneously. The listener's desire to determine which interpretation was correct became part of the experience.

The Fall remain an inexhaustible critical problem. Their recordings invite explanation while undermining the explainer. They are frequently brilliant, occasionally terrible and sometimes both within the same four minutes. To separate masterpieces from failures would misunderstand the enterprise. The Fall's achievement was not simply the production of extraordinary records. It was the construction of a universe in which extraordinary records remained possible, even when the evidence suggested that somebody had spent ten minutes making an amplifier hum.

The North may never rise again. The bald men may remain undefeated. The second house may be haunted. But somewhere, beneath electrical interference, a rhythm begins to establish itself. And Mark E. Smith is preparing to tell everybody why they are wrong.
