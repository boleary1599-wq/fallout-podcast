The Decline and Fall of the Decline Narrative

There is something suspiciously comfortable about the phrase “the difficult middle period”. It is the cultural critic's equivalent of putting unwanted furniture in the loft. The records have not disappeared; they have merely been moved somewhere nobody expects visitors to go. For The Fall, the years 1993 to 1996 have acquired precisely this status: four albums, several different ideas of what the group should be, and a convenient reputation for running out of steam. It is an oddly linear judgement to impose upon a band whose greatest contribution to rock music may have been its refusal to travel in straight lines.

Edward Gibbon gave us the monumental decline-and-fall narrative; British pop criticism has been applying its miniature version to bands ever since. First comes the heroic primitive period, then the imperial peak, then the decadent phase of expensive production and increasingly questionable personnel decisions. There is a hint of Gibbon in every record-collector's claim that things went wrong after a particular drummer left. The Fall complicate this because they seemed to be collapsing almost continuously, including during the years now retrospectively regarded as their golden age.

The Infotainment Scan, Middle Class Revolt, Cerebral Caustic and The Light User Syndrome do not present a smooth movement from vitality to exhaustion. They repeatedly contradict one another, and sometimes contradict themselves. A groove can be more powerful and less interesting; an apparently minor song can be more affecting than a noisy declaration of intent. A record can sound contemporary without sounding alive. Another can be horribly unfashionable yet contain the band's most convincing music in years.

The useful distinction is not between good and bad eras but between change and development. Development promises direction, improvement, the progressive unfolding of a plan. Change merely announces that the furniture has been moved, possibly while someone was sitting on it. The Fall were prodigious changers. Whether they were developing was another matter, and one they seemed magnificently uninterested in resolving.

This is not an invitation to excuse everything. The worst cult criticism converts every failure into proof of the artist's integrity. A bad mix becomes an assault on bourgeois taste; an unfinished arrangement becomes an act of resistance; a singer's absence becomes a radical interrogation of presence. Sometimes a record is simply less good. The point is to discover why, rather than award it a medal for being difficult.

The Television Is Watching You

In the nineties the television stopped being a box in the corner and became the atmosphere. News, advertising, politics and celebrity circulated through the same glowing surface, each borrowing the other's methods. The title The Infotainment Scan catches the moment with almost indecent accuracy. The record's electronic pulse and nervous verbal fragments suggest not a protest singer standing outside the media circus, but a man who has been locked in the studio after the presenters have gone home.

“Paranoia Man in Cheap Shit Room” and “A Past Gone Mad” make paranoia sound like a production technique. Their repetitions are tight enough to dance to and uncomfortable enough to suggest that somebody has already sold the dancers' personal details. The more forceful moments of The Light User Syndrome retain that suspicion but make the room physically heavier, with guitars and electronics jostling for space. The same anxiety has acquired different machinery. The comparison matters more than the calendar: electronic precision does not automatically mean progress, and guitar noise does not automatically mean authenticity.

Chris Morris's Brass Eye would later perfect the spectacle of official voices delivering ludicrous information with terrifying authority. The Fall had long inhabited a neighbouring world, where the most deranged statement could be delivered with the confidence of a council notice. There is also something of Kafka's bureaucratic nightmare here, except that the clerk has discovered a drum machine and is charging admission. Smith's language often resembles an instruction manual from which half the instructions have been torn out.

The unnerving thing is that the music can be cheerful. “Behind the Counter” has an almost brisk public-facing efficiency, as if a complaint is being processed at a shop that sells grievances. The comparative restraint of “The Reckoning” exposes another kind of surveillance: the feeling of being observed by one's own past, of having every new gesture checked against a file of earlier performances. Paranoia does not require maximum volume. It requires the sense that the ordinary world has developed a hidden system of judgement.

One can hear these records as a precursor to the media saturation that later became banal. But that should not turn them into prophetic monuments. The pleasure is more immediate and physical: the way a repeated electronic figure makes a room feel smaller, the way a bass line turns suspicion into momentum, the way a voice seems to be reporting from several incompatible locations. This is not merely a band commenting on the information age. It is a band trying to make information itself misbehave.

The Department of Creative Sabotage

The romantic image of The Fall as a permanent state of productive chaos has always needed qualification. Chaos is only artistically useful when somebody can shape its consequences. Otherwise it is simply a terrible day at work. The distinction becomes urgent in these records, where the musicians' ability to construct convincing arrangements is frequently matched by the singer's willingness to interfere with them.

“Bonkers in Phoenix” is the most spectacular exhibit. A recognisable musical foundation is treated until it resembles a transmission picked up from the wrong planet. Is the damage the composition, or has the composition been rescued from its own conventional possibilities? The answer depends partly on how much pleasure one takes in watching something being dismantled. Compare that with the sleek, almost efficient propulsion of parts of The Infotainment Scan: there, electronic treatment can intensify an idea rather than obscure it. The same technological vocabulary produces opposite effects.

Theodore Adorno distrusted the standardisation of popular music; Mark E. Smith, had he been appointed to explain the matter on daytime television, might have demonstrated his opposition by unplugging the studio monitors and insulting the presenter. Yet deliberate anti-polish is not automatically a victory over standardisation. Destruction can become a routine just as readily as a chorus. Once the listener expects a perfectly good song to be mangled, the mangling is itself a convention.

The working methods behind these albums invite a less glamorous analogy: The Office, several years before David Brent appeared on television. Imagine a workplace in which the staff have spent the week preparing an intricate presentation and the manager arrives on Friday to replace the slides with insults and a photograph of a horse. The result might be visionary. It might also be evidence that the staff need a union. Accounts of Smith's late interventions should not be mistaken for a universal studio rule, but the recurring tension between composed music and imposed disruption is audible.

Brix Smith's return for Cerebral Caustic complicates any simple story of restoration. Bringing back a musician associated with earlier melodic successes does not recreate the earlier chemistry. Nor does the heavier sound of The Light User Syndrome prove that the problem has been solved. Sometimes the arrangements push back effectively against Smith's interventions; sometimes they seem to be waiting for a verdict from an absent chairman.

The real achievement of a Fall recording is not that it sounds unfinished. It is that its disagreements become musically necessary. The best tracks make it impossible to imagine the rough edges being removed without destroying the architecture. The weaker ones leave us hearing the architecture and the vandalism as two separate events.

Pop Music in a Hostile Environment

It is easy to mistake The Fall's hostility to respectability for hostility to pleasure. But this was a band that understood hooks, dance rhythms and the comic efficiency of a repeated phrase. The early nineties made the contradiction particularly visible. Electronic production could make the group more accessible; returning guitar players could make it sound less conventional; a pop-shaped song might carry more menace than an elaborate noise experiment.

The relationship between “The Reckoning” and “Behind the Counter” is instructive. One finds its force in restraint and melancholy, the other in a brighter rhythmic surface that keeps the listener moving. Neither needs to resemble the abrasive density of Hex Enduction Hour to justify itself. In the same way, the sheer overloaded physicality of The Light User Syndrome can be thrilling without being automatically more substantial than a thinner, more economical recording. Loudness and seriousness are not synonyms.

The BBC game show Bullseye offers an unexpectedly useful aesthetic model. Its appeal lay in the collision of strict format, provincial awkwardness and the possibility that something genuinely bizarre would occur between the questions and the prizes. Much of The Fall's best pop music works similarly: a perfectly serviceable musical framework is interrupted by a voice that seems to have wandered in from another programme. The form is essential because it gives the disruption something to interrupt.

That is why “Bonkers in Phoenix” is not simply the radical alternative to the more direct material. Its extreme treatment can become so dominant that the listener loses the tension between song and sabotage. Conversely, an apparently straightforward riff on The Infotainment Scan can sustain more productive uncertainty because the rhythm remains intact while the language keeps opening trapdoors beneath it.

There is also a danger in the fan's preference for difficulty. If the experimental track receives automatic credit for being experimental, while a catchy one must apologise for its pleasures, criticism has become another kind of snobbery. John Peel's enthusiasm for the strange was never reducible to a hatred of pop; the thrill lay in hearing the two categories unexpectedly exchange clothing. The Fall understood that transaction better than most groups, even when they occasionally got the garments hopelessly tangled.

A song should be allowed to be funny, melancholy, catchy or ugly without first passing an authenticity examination. These four albums are at their most rewarding when they remind us that The Fall's identity was an argument between these possibilities, not a fixed choice among them.

A Band Is Not a Dictatorship, Except When It Is

The temptation to explain every Fall record through Mark E. Smith is enormous. It is also a way of making the other musicians disappear. Dave Bush's electronics, the shifting guitar textures, Brix's melodic instincts and the long-developed rhythmic intelligence of the group were not decorative services supplied to a lone genius. They were the substance upon which his voice operated.

There is a bleak comedy in the idea of a band required to be simultaneously independent and obedient. Musicians are expected to generate ideas but must remain prepared for those ideas to be rejected, altered or ignored. The arrangement resembles a constitutional monarchy in which the monarch has retained the right to sack Parliament after lunch. The Fall's frequent personnel crises make this more than a metaphor, but the music is the better evidence of the costs and rewards.

Listen to the space between Smith and the players on Middle Class Revolt, then to the densely populated sound of The Light User Syndrome. One can hear different answers to the same organisational question: how much room should the singer occupy, and what happens when the instrumentalists fill the remaining space? The Infotainment Scan offers another solution, using electronic regularity as a kind of impersonal authority. Cerebral Caustic often makes the conflict more obvious, particularly when its competing musical instincts refuse to reconcile.

The history of bands is full of charismatic leaders whose reputations have swallowed their collaborators. Yet the mythology of The Fall is peculiarly self-defeating. If Smith could make any collection of players sound like The Fall, that is evidence of an extraordinary sensibility; it is also evidence that those players repeatedly contributed the rhythms, textures and tensions that made the transformation possible. A magician without a stage is merely a man explaining his tricks in a car park.

By 1996 the long-standing relationships within the group were approaching another rupture. Retrospective listening gives ordinary instrumental gestures a valedictory glow they did not necessarily possess when recorded. That is a useful emotional response but a dangerous critical shortcut. The music should not be reduced to a farewell card signed by people who had not yet left.

The most convincing account of these records is therefore neither heroic individualism nor a sentimental celebration of teamwork. It is the account of a collective artistic process made unstable by power, talent, resentment and a sometimes extraordinary ability to convert those forces into rhythm.

The Museum of Unfinished Business

The real curse of a prolific band is that every new release arrives accompanied by its own museum. Fans bring comparisons, preferred line-ups, lost possibilities and carefully maintained rankings. A song has scarcely begun before it is being measured against an earlier version of a vaguely similar idea. By the mid-nineties The Fall had accumulated enough history to make almost any new gesture seem like either repetition or betrayal.

This is why the argument about whether the records resemble Hex Enduction Hour is so revealing. The comparison makes sense as an expression of what a listener values: urgency, density, apparent risk. It becomes useless when treated as a universal standard. To demand the same kind of danger from a different musical situation is like criticising a late Hitchcock film for not being Rear Window. The work may fail, but it must be allowed to fail on its own terms.

The records also expose a peculiarly British discomfort with the middle-aged avant-gardist. Youthful perversity is charming; mature perversity is frequently diagnosed as bad management. There is something of Alan Partridge in the nightmare version of Smith's public persona: a man who has acquired authority without acquiring the social habits expected to accompany it. The comparison is comic, but the music resists being reduced to the joke. Its best moments retain the capacity to make the listener feel genuinely unsafe.

A more helpful image comes from Walter Benjamin's angel of history, blown forward while looking back at accumulating wreckage. The Fall's career was not quite so tragic, and its records contain far too much laughter for solemn allegory. Still, the image catches something about a group that kept moving while its discarded line-ups, abandoned styles and unresolved grievances piled up behind it. The past was never left alone; it was constantly being dragged into the next recording.

The Infotainment Scan can sound fresh where Cerebral Caustic sounds clogged; Middle Class Revolt can find emotional precision where The Light User Syndrome chooses blunt force. Then the relationships reverse on the next track. There is no clean hierarchy of the four records, only a set of recurring problems solved and unsolved in different ways.

To call these the wilderness years is to mistake an argument for an absence. The Fall had not run out of things to say. They had become increasingly uncertain about how their competing voices should be organised, and that uncertainty was sometimes disastrous. It was also, on the right recording, still capable of producing music that sounded like nobody else. The empire was not declining in a straight line. It was refusing to produce a usable map.
