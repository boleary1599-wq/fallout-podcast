THE MISSING WINNER: SIXTEEN SONGS IN SEARCH OF AN ALIBI

The Fall’s outtakes, covers, radio curiosities and apparently abandoned experiments reveal an uncomfortable truth: sometimes the rubbish is the point, and sometimes it really is rubbish.

By Dr. J. Temperance, Department of Unfinished Business

The archive is a dangerous invention. Once a band has been dead long enough, the contents of every cupboard begin to acquire the dignity of historical evidence. A discarded rehearsal becomes a document of artistic transition. An instrumental that nobody finished becomes an audacious rejection of conventional song form. Somebody coughs near a microphone in 1983 and forty years later a man in spectacles writes about the subversion of the respiratory system under Thatcherism.

The Fall are unusually susceptible to this disease, not because their rejects are always magnificent but because their best records already sound like the sort of thing another group would have rejected. Their entire discography undermines the distinction between finished and unfinished, accident and intention, inspiration and a musician being told to keep playing while Mark E. Smith goes somewhere else.

The sixteen obscurities gathered under the magnificent grammatical calamity *Is It Are Missing Winner?* pose a question more interesting than whether an outtake deserves inclusion on a deluxe reissue. What happens to our judgement when the identity of the artist becomes a guarantee of significance? Can a poor Fall recording still be an interesting Fall recording? And what happens when a genuinely excellent one has been left to rot among the Christmas novelties?

The answer begins with an apparently mundane instrument: the bass guitar.

THE SOUND OF SOMEBODY REFUSING TO LEAVE

“Session Musician” is the kind of recording that makes the term *rarity* seem insufficiently respectful. It has the virtues of the group’s best marginal material: a compelling rhythmic idea, an immediately recognisable personality and the sense of an arrangement that is discovering itself in real time. The episode’s scoring gives it 73 points, the clear winner of the initial sixteen-song assessment, ahead of “Ponto” on 64, “Afternoon Disco” on 62 and “Crack House” on 56. This is not simply collectors’ sentimentality. The track has something that many respectable studio recordings lack: a reason to continue existing after its first thirty seconds.

The title is a provocation. A session musician is supposed to be an interchangeable professional, somebody capable of reproducing an approved sound without contaminating it with too much personality. The Fall’s entire method depends on the opposite proposition. Their players are distinctive precisely because they cannot be mistaken for anonymous technicians. Steve Hanley’s bass does not politely support the music; it makes the argument, draws up the plans and occasionally locks everybody else outside the building.

In the conventional rock narrative, musicianship is a ladder towards fluency. In The Fall, fluency can become an impediment. The most valuable performance may be the one that refuses to smooth out the edges. This is why an unfinished-sounding piece can sometimes possess more life than a fully realised single. Its musical problem remains audible.

“Ponto” and “Afternoon Disco” extend the argument. Their obscurity does not make them automatically superior to familiar Fall material, but their rhythmic identities survive without the assistance of a grand historical reputation. The latter title suggests some dreadful municipal attempt at organised pleasure: a dance floor in a community centre, the lights on, a man in a polyester shirt announcing that refreshments will be available until half past three. The Fall were always good at making entertainment sound like an administrative threat.

The ranking matters less as a league table than as evidence that the archive contains genuine alternatives to the official canon. These are not merely scraps from the cutting-room floor. Some could have sat comfortably on records whose more famous tracks now receive unquestioning reverence.

CHRISTMAS IN A BUILDING THAT HAS BEEN CONDEMNED

The opposite end of the selection offers the necessary corrective. “Brand New Cadillac” receives a spectacularly hostile reception. The transcript records the economical judgements “Unlistenable” and “Shit”, responses that possess a refreshing critical precision. The track finishes on ten points. No amount of historical contextualisation is likely to transform that result into a concealed masterpiece.

The original “Brand New Cadillac”, recorded by Vince Taylor and His Playboys in 1959, is a ferocious little rock-and-roll record, later brought to a wider punk audience by the Clash on *London Calling*. Its virtues are compression, momentum and the reckless certainty of its central riff. A Fall version has to do more than demonstrate familiarity with rockabilly. Smith’s interest in primitive rock-and-roll is well documented, but the difference between possessing an affinity for a form and producing a worthwhile recording of it is considerable.

The covers here expose the problem with unusual clarity. Frank Zappa and the Mothers of Invention’s “Hungry Freaks, Daddy” brings with it the countercultural satire of *Freak Out!*; the Fall’s relationship with Zappa is not an obvious marriage of minds. Zappa’s arrangements often demonstrate an almost punitive degree of control. Smith’s preferred aesthetic is more likely to make the listener wonder whether anybody was in charge at all. That tension might produce something thrilling, or merely two incompatible kinds of sarcasm occupying the same room.

“Birthday”, associated with the Idle Race, belongs to another species of eccentric British pop, one in which whimsical songwriting and psychedelic studio invention coexist. It is possible to hear a historical family resemblance between this late-sixties oddness and Smith’s taste for the peculiar. But resemblance does not guarantee transformation. A cover is most revealing when it tells us what the new performer cannot help doing to the original.

Then there is “Blue Christmas”. The title carries an entire burden of American seasonal melancholy, Elvis Presley, shop-floor sentiment and supermarket loudspeakers. The episode’s tally puts it on 32 points, with a conspicuous contribution from Ezra. This is exactly the kind of recording that forces a Fall listener to confront the possibility of affection without aesthetic justification. Sometimes the attraction is not that a song has been brilliantly reimagined, but that the existence of the performance itself is so magnificently unnecessary.

Walter Benjamin wrote about the aura of the original work of art. He did not, so far as anyone can establish, consider what happens when Mark E. Smith wanders into the Christmas repertoire and leaves muddy footprints on the carpet. The resulting artefact may possess a different aura: not artistic transcendence but the faint smell of a pub function room at closing time.

THE MAN WHO DREAMED THE WRONG REALITY

Before the rankings begin, the recording stages a little séance for Philip K. Dick, complete with pentagrams, questionable ritual equipment and a wonderfully solemn account of the affinities between Dick and Smith. It is more than a diversion. Dick’s *Ubik* imagines advertising slogans and everyday objects slipping out of their appointed realities; *The Three Stigmata of Palmer Eldritch* makes even the apparent escape from a manufactured world another trap. Smith’s newspaper fragments, half-remembered encounters and suddenly authoritative nonsense operate on a comparable fault line.

The transcript explicitly connects “How I Wrote ‘Elastic Man’” with a writer who loses access to the mysterious source of his inspiration, and “Wings” with an encounter that breaks the rules of reality. That is more illuminating than simply declaring Smith a science-fiction enthusiast. Both artists are interested in the moment when an ordinary life ceases to obey its own instructions. The important difference is that Dick often gives the paranoia an elaborate metaphysical architecture; Smith supplies a bus timetable, a hostile neighbour and the feeling that the pub has been subtly replaced by an identical but inferior pub.

THE REJECTED CITY

The more interesting marginal Fall recordings often resemble field reports from places that may not exist. “Crack House”, “Gapa”, “Pop Stickers” and “My Condition” sound, even before one hears them, like fragments of a private urban geography. The titles could be handwritten labels on tapes discovered in the office of a failed municipal planning department.

This is where Smith’s work connects with the British tradition of the uncanny rather than merely with post-punk. Robert Aickman’s stories rarely explain the mechanisms of their disturbances. Nigel Kneale’s *Quatermass* serials find horror in ordinary institutions, familiar landscapes and the frightening possibility that the modern world has misunderstood its own foundations. Smith’s imagination operates in the same neighbourhood, although it is considerably more likely to stop at the off-licence.

His lyrical fragments frequently produce a peculiar illusion of documentary authority. A name, an address, a complaint or a stray technical term is delivered as though it belongs to a complete account whose other pages have been lost. “He Talks”, “Countdown” and “Perverted by Language” belong to a catalogue in which language itself can become an object of suspicion. The words seem to be reporting something; the thing being reported remains stubbornly unavailable.

There is a temptation to call this Burroughsian, and William S. Burroughs is certainly relevant. The cut-up method exposes the unexpected relationships created when language is severed from its original context. But Smith’s approach is less a literary procedure than a performance practice. His voice gives authority to the disconnected phrase. He can make a piece of linguistic debris sound like the crucial evidence in a criminal trial.

That does not mean every fragment is a coded masterpiece. “Jazzed Up Punk Shit” is an admirably unpretentious title, almost an advance review. Its existence raises a basic question about the Fall aesthetic: if a recording is knowingly crude, is that enough? No. The difference between deliberate ugliness and uninteresting ugliness remains important, however much the band's mythology encourages listeners to collapse the two.

One can admire the refusal of polish without being obliged to admire every unpolished object.

THE TYRANNY OF THE FINISHED ALBUM

The conventional album creates a misleading impression of artistic inevitability. Songs appear in a particular order, wearing particular production choices, and gradually become fixed in memory as though no other version of events had been possible. The outtake destroys that illusion. Suddenly the album becomes one arrangement among many.

Brian Eno’s conception of the recording studio as a compositional instrument is helpful here. Recording does not simply preserve a performance; it creates a new object from selection, repetition, editing and accident. The Fall’s marginal recordings reveal those decisions in a peculiarly naked form. One hears the path not taken, the idea that survived without being domesticated, or the experiment that perhaps deserved to be abandoned.

The question is especially sharp with a band whose leader made change a governing principle. A rejected track might be excluded because it was weak, because the personnel changed, because a label disliked it, because a better idea appeared, or because Smith had developed a sudden and irreconcilable objection to something about it. The archive does not always tell us which.

This is why the sixteen-song exercise is more than an amusing contest between oddities. It restores uncertainty to a catalogue whose accepted landmarks can seem impossibly settled. The top four—“Session Musician”, “Ponto”, “Afternoon Disco” and “Crack House”—are not a newly discovered alternative greatest-hits album. They are reminders that the official history of a group is always incomplete.

There is an important distinction between the collector’s pleasure and the critic’s obligation. The collector wants everything, including the dreadful material, because completeness is the point. The critic must still distinguish between what rewards attention and what merely survives inspection. The two positions are compatible, but they are not identical.

A recording can be historically fascinating and musically feeble. Another can be a great piece of music without contributing a single useful fact to the biography of its creator. The Fall produced enough of both to keep archivists and critics in separate states of agitation for decades.

A SMALL VICTORY FOR THE UNNECESSARY

What makes The Fall uniquely suited to this kind of excavation is their refusal to establish a reliable hierarchy between the important and the ridiculous. The same musical universe can accommodate a revolutionary epic, a gothic incantation, a ferocious garage-rock performance and an apparently casual rendition of a Christmas song.

The result is not a democratic utopia in which every recording deserves equal praise. Quite the opposite. The archive makes the differences more apparent. “Brand New Cadillac” can be dreadful and “Session Musician” excellent without either verdict threatening the broader achievement. In fact, the willingness to admit failure is essential to appreciating the successes.

The title *Is It Are Missing Winner?* is therefore more appropriate than its grammar permits. The missing winner is not necessarily the lost masterpiece. It is the possibility that value has escaped the system designed to recognise it: the studio album, the single, the official retrospective, the respectable critical narrative.

The Fall's most characteristic achievement was to make the listener suspicious of such systems in the first place. They were never entirely interested in behaving like a band with a completed body of work. Even their most definitive recordings sound as though something might interrupt them, or that the next record could render the previous one obsolete.

An archive of rejected Fall songs is consequently not a museum of dead ends. It is a collection of doors, most leading to disappointing cupboards, a few opening onto extraordinary rooms, and at least one concealing a man playing “Blue Christmas” for reasons nobody can satisfactorily explain.

That, too, is a kind of artistic freedom.
