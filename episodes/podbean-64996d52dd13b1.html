THE BALLET OF THE DISCONTENTED: FOUR ALBUMS AND THE DEATH OF THE OLD FALL

How a group built on repetition, suspicion and the productive misuse of equipment became a pop proposition, a dance company and a small industry in manufacturing its own contradictions.

By Professor C. R. Scanlon, Institute of Hostile Aesthetics

I. A MACHINE THAT LEARNS TO SMILE

The most suspicious word in the Fall's vocabulary is progress. Between 1985 and 1988 the group acquired a sheen, an audience beyond the permanently converted, a more conspicuous melodic instinct and, eventually, an invitation into the world of contemporary dance. Four albums—This Nation's Saving Grace, Bend Sinister, The Frenz Experiment and I Am Kurious Oranj—form neither an uninterrupted ascent nor the familiar rock narrative of a band going soft. They describe something more interesting: an organism developing new faculties while periodically trying to bite them off.

The obvious story is that Brix Smith brought pop into a band previously dedicated to making ugly noises. It is also the sort of story that collapses the moment you listen to Grotesque or Perverted by Language. The Fall had always understood hooks. They simply treated them as dangerous objects, liable to be repeated until the listener could no longer remember whether the original pleasure was genuine. The early records' rockabilly jolts, chant-like choruses and disreputable dance rhythms were pop music seen through the wrong end of a municipal telescope. Brix did not introduce melody so much as alter its visibility.

This Nation's Saving Grace makes that visibility almost indecent. “Spoilt Victorian Child” turns an implausibly nimble guitar figure into a piece of nasty historical theatre. “L.A.” is all economical momentum and vacant glamour, as though a Hollywood boulevard had been designed by someone whose only previous experience of California came from a defective photocopier. “Paintwork” allows domestic recordings and stray voices to interrupt a song without destroying its strange tenderness. The production is clearer, the surfaces less abrasive. Yet clarity here is not the same thing as reassurance: the better you can hear the details, the more peculiar they become.

The group's arrival at a certain critical respectability creates a problem. A record can be acclaimed precisely because it appears to resolve tensions that made its predecessors vital. Sonic Youth's Daydream Nation would eventually acquire a similar canonical status: the supposedly definitive statement that some devotees find less immediately combustible than the awkward records on either side. A canon is a useful thing for journalists, record shops and people who need to buy a present for a cousin. It is less useful for understanding a band that treated consistency as a personal insult.

II. THE SOUND OF THE WALLS MOVING

What changes most dramatically across these records is not the songwriting but the relationship between sound and space. The Fall's earlier recordings often seem to take place inside a room whose dimensions are being contested by the musicians. Two drummers pull at the pulse, guitars scratch against the edges, Smith occupies the doorway and shouts at everyone trying to leave. By the middle eighties, production becomes a more deliberate architectural activity. Instruments are positioned, layered, occasionally compressed into something dangerously close to good taste.

That is why the recurring argument about the polish of This Nation's Saving Grace matters. Its strengths are obvious: the performances have force and definition; the arrangements reveal the precision of Steve Hanley's bass and Craig Scanlon's guitar. But there is also a loss of the sensation that the whole contraption might collapse before the final chorus. To hear that as an uncomplicated improvement is to misunderstand the peculiar economics of Fall music. Risk was not an unfortunate by-product of limited resources. It was one of the resources.

Bend Sinister answers with mud. The sound is darker, less hospitable, sometimes as if the tapes had been stored in a damp cellar beside a family of territorial badgers. The title, shared with Vladimir Nabokov's 1947 novel, offers an unexpectedly appropriate literary companion. Nabokov's book describes a mind resisting the grotesque simplifications of an authoritarian state; the record's world feels similarly populated by systems whose rules are both arbitrary and lethal. The connection should not be inflated into a claim that every lyric encodes Nabokov. Its value lies in the atmosphere: oppression made ridiculous, ridicule made oppressive.

“Gross Chapel—British Grenadiers” makes a particularly formidable argument for the Fall as makers of environments rather than merely songs. Its ceremonial undertow and heavy pacing suggest a national ritual conducted after the nation has forgotten the purpose of the ceremony. “Living Too Late” finds something wounded beneath Smith's defensive scorn. “R.O.D.” brings a hard, relentless pressure that refuses the idea that darkness requires the theatrical accessories of goth. Lee Hazlewood's dry fatalism is sometimes a more helpful comparison than the Sisters of Mercy. The Fall could be sinister without purchasing a fog machine.

Here the distinction between dark and gothic becomes important. Gothic rock frequently announces its atmosphere; the Fall often make the atmosphere appear to be an accidental consequence of everyday life. Their horror is administrative, local, almost practical. Something terrible has happened in the next street, but the council has not yet supplied the correct form.

III. THE POP RECORD THAT REFUSES TO BE ONE

The late-eighties temptation is to divide the catalogue into acceptable experiments and regrettable concessions. The Frenz Experiment frustrates this distinction because it is simultaneously accessible and wildly uneven. It contains songs that sound almost eager to entertain, passages of stubbornly unlovely repetition, and material that seems to have escaped from a rehearsal tape while the responsible adults were absent.

“Hit the North” is the obvious candidate for a pop breakthrough, although its slogan is so blunt and its arrangement so insistently odd that it resembles a campaign anthem for a political party with no agreed policy. “Victoria”, the Kinks cover, is a more revealing choice than any amount of speculation about a new commercial direction. Ray Davies's original song turns imperial nostalgia into satire; Smith's version finds an additional layer of manic patriotic malfunction. A record that appears to celebrate the nation is also a record about the impossibility of trusting what the nation says about itself.

Elsewhere the band pursues a kind of ramshackle variety-show logic. “Athlete Cured” takes a riff suspiciously close to the one associated with Spinal Tap's “Tonight I'm Gonna Rock You Tonight” and subjects it to the Fall's customary abrasive rearrangement. Whether the borrowing is homage, opportunism or an accident of shared musical vulgarity is less interesting than what happens to it: a piece of knowingly stupid hard-rock theatre becomes a Fall song, and therefore an entirely different category of stupidity.

“Oswald Defence Lawyer” belongs to the group's enduring fascination with disputed histories and the odd characters who gather around them. The assassination of John F. Kennedy becomes less an occasion for definitive conspiracy theory than a reminder that public history is always being rewritten by private obsessives. Don DeLillo's Libra, published in 1988, performs a grander literary version of a related operation: the historical event dissolves into competing stories, networks and interpretations. Smith rarely supplied a stable account of anything. His method was to throw fragments into circulation and let the listener become another unreliable witness.

There is a reason that this album can seem weaker when approached as a single grand statement than when its individual songs are encountered in isolation. The Fall were never particularly interested in the rock album as a coherent novel with a satisfying ending. Sometimes their LPs are collections of mutually antagonistic pamphlets. The Frenz Experiment simply makes the antagonism unusually audible.

IV. THE BODY IS AN UNRELIABLE INSTRUMENT

The collaboration with choreographer Michael Clark introduces a genuinely radical question: what happens when music that seems designed to resist interpretation is given bodies that must interpret it in public?

Clark was no polite visitor from an immaculate high-art institution. His work brought punk, club culture, transgression and deliberate awkwardness into contemporary dance. The alliance with the Fall was therefore less a meeting between opposites than the recognition of a distant relative. The two practices shared a suspicion of respectable technique, even as both depended upon considerable technique to produce their effects.

I Am Kurious Oranj grew from the dance work associated with the 300th anniversary of William of Orange's arrival in England. The historical material is itself an invitation to trouble. William's accession can be narrated as constitutional liberation, dynastic manoeuvre, sectarian conflict or the successful rebranding of a foreign intervention. The Fall's version refuses to choose the schoolbook interpretation. The past becomes a set of props, rhythms and aggressively unstable jokes.

The album title nods toward Vilgot Sjöman's I Am Curious (Yellow), a film whose mixture of politics, sexual provocation and formal play once attracted controversy far beyond its artistic merits. Clark's choreography and Smith's history lessons likewise make the supposed division between serious culture and disreputable entertainment look faintly absurd. One might even invoke Bertolt Brecht's insistence that performance should interrupt habitual ways of seeing. But Brecht wanted audiences to recognise the machinery of ideology; Smith sometimes seems content to replace that machinery with a more entertaining malfunction.

“Jerusalem” is the most revealing collision. William Blake's visionary words, already converted into a patriotic hymn by Hubert Parry, are fed into a recording that treats national uplift as an invitation to heckle. Blake's poem is not a straightforward celebration of England; it asks whether a spiritual Jerusalem can be built amid the consequences of industrial modernity. The Fall's treatment restores some of that unease by refusing the smooth ceremonial confidence of the familiar hymn.

The music also exposes the peculiar limitations of a soundtrack heard without its intended visual partner. A repeated passage may be dramatically necessary when dancers are negotiating a stage, yet seem overextended when encountered between two other album tracks. That does not make the music a failure. It means the record is an incomplete document of a larger event. Judging it solely by the standards of a self-contained rock LP is rather like reviewing the scenery of a play after the actors have gone home.

V. HISTORY, HUMOUR AND THE MISUSE OF RESPECTABILITY

These four records are connected by a fascination with institutions that pretend to be stable: the nation, the household, the recording studio, the music industry, the art establishment. Each is exposed as a system held together by habit, performance and a surprising amount of bluff.

Smith's humour is central to the operation. The title Bend Sinister sounds forbidding; the record contains moments of genuine absurdity. The Frenz Experiment sounds convivial; its friendliness is often booby-trapped. I Am Kurious Oranj announces a conceptual project of historical ambition, then proceeds as though the historical consultant has been locked in a cupboard. The jokes are not decoration added to serious work. They are one of the ways the work thinks.

Mikhail Bakhtin's account of the carnivalesque offers a useful, if imperfect, framework. In carnival, hierarchies are inverted, the grotesque body disrupts official dignity, and established authority is temporarily made ridiculous. The Fall's carnival is less generous: the king is mocked, the crowd is mocked, and the man selling tickets is suspected of working for the king. There is no uncomplicated community waiting beneath the masks.

This is also why the period's apparently commercial gestures cannot be dismissed as capitulation. A pop hook is another institution, a compact agreement between performer and audience about what pleasure should sound like. The Fall accept the agreement just long enough to tamper with its wording. Their version of accessibility retains a fundamental discourtesy. The chorus arrives; the singer behaves as though the chorus has arrived without permission.

The most instructive comparison may be the Stooges, whose great records achieve their violence through an almost primitive economy of means. Iggy Pop's body became an instrument of confrontation, just as Smith's voice was an instrument of refusal. Yet the Fall's late-eighties music has more moving parts, more historical debris, more contradictory impulses. It is not simply louder or softer, stranger or more conventional. It is a band trying on incompatible futures without discarding its previous selves.

VI. FOUR FALSE ENDINGS

The temptation is to locate the point where the Fall stopped being the Fall: when Brix made the guitars prettier, when a producer cleaned up the mix, when “Hit the North” became danceable, when Michael Clark invited the group into a theatre. Such dates are attractive because they make history easy to file. They are also wrong.

The 1985–88 records are uneven, sometimes frustrating and occasionally magnificent. Their contradictions are not symptoms of an identity crisis that must be solved by the critic. They are the identity. The Fall could be a pop group, a garage band, a literary nuisance, an electronic experiment and the house orchestra for a deranged historical pageant, sometimes within the same side of vinyl.

What makes This Nation's Saving Grace endure is not that it finally perfects an earlier formula, but that its clarity reveals how much disturbance the formula can contain. What makes Bend Sinister valuable is its refusal to tidy up the resulting mess. The Frenz Experiment tests the limits of eclecticism and sometimes loses the argument. I Am Kurious Oranj makes the argument irrelevant by moving the whole business onto a stage.

Progress, in other words, is the wrong word. Mutation will do.

By the end of 1988 the Fall have not become respectable. Respectability has briefly made the mistake of inviting them indoors. The carpets will never be quite the same again.
