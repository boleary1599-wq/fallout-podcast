THE HEX, THE HAG AND THE HORRIBLE POSSIBILITY OF SUCCESS

On kitchen-sink occultism, William Burroughs, two drummers, a severed relationship with the past and the terrible discovery that a pop song need not constitute an act of treason.

BY PROFESSOR C. R. SCANLON, INSTITUTE OF HOSTILE AESTHETICS

There is a particular kind of artistic catastrophe that occurs when a group produces its masterpiece. The record is acclaimed, the critical vocabulary hardens, and what was previously an ongoing experiment becomes a monument. The musicians are now expected to spend the remainder of their careers preserving the very qualities that made them interesting in the first place.

In March 1982, The Fall released Hex Enduction Hour, an album so comprehensively equipped with the characteristics of The Fall that it might reasonably have rendered further Fall albums unnecessary. It contained the repetitions, the incantations, the cheap electronics, the literary violence, the inexplicable narratives, the contempt for contemporary taste and the sensation of an entire northern industrial landscape having developed a collective nervous disorder.

There were two drummers. There were songs that sounded like municipal machinery. There was a man called the Hip Priest. There were references to Nazis, hotels, Iceland, winter and the peculiar social position of the culturally marginal intellectual.

The thing was practically a university department.

And then, in an act of spectacular perversity, The Fall spent the next two years dismantling it.

Between Hex Enduction Hour and The Wonderful and Frightening World of The Fall, the group produced four studio albums, underwent significant personnel changes, encountered the possibilities of increasingly sophisticated production, and began to discover that musical accessibility might be compatible with their continued existence.

This was not a simple movement from experimental authenticity towards commercial compromise. It was something considerably more interesting: the transformation of The Fall from a particular musical configuration into a method capable of surviving the destruction of that configuration.

The central question is not whether they became better or worse.

It is whether Mark E. Smith could remain Mark E. Smith when the band stopped sounding quite so much like Mark E. Smith's idea of The Fall.

I. HEX ENDUCTION HOUR: THE SERGEANT PEPPER OF PEOPLE WHO HATE SERGEANT PEPPER

The comparison between Hex Enduction Hour and Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band is ridiculous, which makes it unexpectedly useful.

Both albums have acquired the status of canonical achievements, works whose reputations threaten to overwhelm the actual experience of listening to them. Both are treated as statements of artistic completeness. Both encourage critics to discuss their historical importance before remembering to mention what the music sounds like.

The difference is that Sgt. Pepper imagines the recording studio as a place where unlimited resources might produce unlimited possibility. Hex Enduction Hour sounds as though unlimited possibility has been discovered inside a building that ought to have been condemned.

The album's extraordinary coherence emerges from the interaction of musicians who had developed an almost telepathic understanding of one another's limitations and possibilities. Steve Hanley's bass supplies not merely harmonic support but architectural authority. Craig Scanlon's guitar cuts across the arrangements with an intelligence that resists conventional rock virtuosity. Mark Riley's keyboards and guitar provide texture, disruption and occasional comic grotesquery.

And then there are the two drummers.

The relationship between Paul Hanley and Karl Burns is essential to the album's physical character. Rather than merely doubling the beat, the percussion frequently creates the impression of several related rhythmic systems operating simultaneously. The music advances with the momentum of an industrial procession whose participants have received slightly different instructions.

The opening “The Classical” is an extraordinary declaration of intent. Its bass and percussion establish an almost military forward movement, while Smith immediately attacks the assumptions of cultural refinement. The title itself is a provocation: here is the classical, presented as a piece of abrasive, vulgar, gloriously undisciplined rock music.

The comparison with Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring is not entirely fanciful. Both works derive much of their violence from rhythm rather than harmonic complexity. Both produce the sensation of an ancient ritual being conducted with modern machinery.

Stravinsky caused a scandal in Paris. Smith managed to make the scandal sound as though it had happened in Prestwich and been reported to the council.

“Hip Priest” is perhaps the album's most remarkable achievement. Its slow, ominous accumulation of instrumental detail produces an atmosphere of extraordinary psychological pressure. The bass does not so much accompany the song as occupy it. Smith's vocal becomes a form of social and spiritual interrogation, the Hip Priest simultaneously an outsider, a charlatan, an intellectual and a figure of genuine authority.

The song's appearance in Jonathan Demme's The Silence of the Lambs subsequently supplied an almost too-perfect visual interpretation. Its ritualistic menace became associated with the cinematic spectacle of Buffalo Bill, although the original recording's social unease is considerably more complicated than straightforward horror.

Mark Fisher's writing on The Fall helps explain this atmosphere. Smith's work repeatedly evokes a strange relationship between the ordinary and the supernatural, as though occult forces are not external to working-class everyday life but already embedded within it.

This is not the Gothic of ruined castles and aristocratic vampires.

It is the Gothic of betting shops, housing estates and suspiciously deserted pubs.

The two-part “Winter” pushes this method towards narrative abstraction. Its imagery suggests a story, perhaps several stories, without supplying the conventional mechanisms by which those stories might be reconstructed. The music's repetition creates a suspended temporal environment in which events seem simultaneously imminent and already completed.

One might invoke Alain Robbe-Grillet, whose nouveau roman experiments undermined conventional narrative certainty. Or Robert Aickman, whose strange stories construct atmospheres of overwhelming significance while refusing to explain precisely what has happened.

Smith's version is more aggressive and less polite. The listener is not invited to contemplate ambiguity but physically trapped inside it.

And then there is “And This Day”, the album's monumental conclusion.

Its sprawling, unstable construction is less a song than an endurance test conducted by musicians who have discovered that stopping is a bourgeois convention. The performance appears perpetually on the verge of collapse, yet its cumulative force is undeniable.

The comparison with the Velvet Underground's “Sister Ray” is irresistible. Both pieces use duration, repetition and instrumental excess to produce an experience that cannot be reduced to conventional songwriting.

But where “Sister Ray” is a sordid urban carnival, “And This Day” resembles an emergency meeting of a provincial occult society.

The recording's very excess becomes its meaning.

Hex Enduction Hour is not flawless because every element is perfectly executed. It is extraordinary because its imperfections have become inseparable from its identity.

It is an unsliced loaf of bread, enormous, dense, inconvenient and entirely uninterested in becoming a convenient packet of individual portions.

You do not consume it.

You survive it.

II. ROOM TO LIVE: THE UN-DILUTABLE SLANG TRUTH

The usual complaint about Room to Live is that it followed Hex Enduction Hour too quickly, was insufficiently developed and contained material that did not justify its existence. It is an accusation that depends upon the peculiar assumption that The Fall were supposed to behave like a respectable album-oriented rock group.

They were not.

Released later in 1982, Room to Live is shorter, scruffier, more sarcastic and considerably less interested in the monumental grandeur of its predecessor. Its subtitle, Undilutable Slang Truth!, suggests that Smith regarded the record not as a diminished version of Hex but as a corrective to the very possibility of refinement.

Where Hex constructs an imposing artistic universe, Room to Live returns to the immediate business of finding things objectionable.

“Joker Hysterical Face” and “Solicitor in Studio” possess the quality of satirical sketches whose targets have been subjected to an unnecessarily elaborate process of psychological disfigurement. “Hard Life in Country” undermines the pastoral fantasy that rural existence represents some form of moral or spiritual escape. “Detective Instinct” offers the sort of fractured procedural narrative that might have resulted if Raymond Chandler had been commissioned to write an episode of Z-Cars after three days without sleep.

The album's roughness is essential to its effect. The arrangements are often more direct than those on Hex, but their apparent simplicity allows the grotesque humour to become more prominent.

“Marquis Cha-Cha” introduces Latin-inflected rhythms into a political nightmare, while “Papal Visit” reduces religious and institutional spectacle to a form of belligerent provincial entertainment.

The comparison with Captain Beefheart is useful, particularly the deliberately angular, disjointed structures of Trout Mask Replica. But where Beefheart's apparent chaos was often the result of obsessive rehearsal and precise compositional instruction, The Fall's roughness could arise from a more volatile combination of rehearsal, improvisation and deliberate indifference.

The Country Teasers would later explore a related territory: a form of deliberately degraded country and garage rock in which ugliness becomes both an aesthetic choice and a means of social provocation.

Yet Room to Live is not merely a collection of amusingly unpleasant songs. Its importance lies in its refusal to consolidate the achievements of Hex into a permanent musical formula.

The group had discovered how to make a masterpiece. Smith immediately insisted that they were still entitled to make something that sounded like an argument in a public lavatory.

There is something admirable about this.

The record's reputation as a minor or failed work may tell us more about the expectations of rock criticism than about the music itself. The conventional narrative demands a sequence of increasingly accomplished statements. Room to Live offers instead a collection of sharp, unfinished and frequently hilarious observations.

It is not a cathedral.

It is a particularly unpleasant pamphlet distributed outside the cathedral.

III. PERVERTED BY LANGUAGE: THE BODY OF THE VOICE

By 1983, the musical landscape had changed again.

Mark Riley had departed, and Perverted by Language emerged from a leaner instrumental configuration. The absence of Riley's keyboards and guitar altered the group's internal balance, while the arrival of Brix Smith introduced a new creative possibility that would become increasingly significant.

The album is often characterised as difficult, austere or comparatively inaccessible. Its songs are long, its arrangements frequently repetitive, and Smith's lyrics appear increasingly resistant to ordinary narrative interpretation.

Yet it contains some of the group's most extraordinary music.

“Eat Y'self Fitter” begins with a rhythm so insistently mechanical that it resembles an industrial fitness programme administered by an organisation with a deeply suspicious understanding of human health. Its vocal fragments, rhythmic interruptions and apparent instructions turn the body into a site of comic and political struggle.

The title is itself a miniature masterpiece of linguistic violence. Eating oneself fitter is an impossible proposition, an instruction that collapses consumption, self-improvement and bodily destruction into a single phrase.

Theodor Adorno's critique of the culture industry seems almost embarrassingly relevant. Modern consumer society continually promises liberation through the purchase of commodities, while the individual becomes increasingly integrated into the mechanisms of consumption.

Smith reduces the entire process to a grotesque dietary command.

“Smile”, meanwhile, is one of the most physically intimidating recordings in The Fall's catalogue. Its grinding, repetitive guitar figure and relentless rhythmic propulsion establish an atmosphere of extraordinary aggression.

The title promises warmth, sociability and reassurance.

The music offers the opposite.

There is something of Antonin Artaud's Theatre of Cruelty in this contradiction. Artaud sought a form of performance that would operate directly upon the audience's nervous system, bypassing the reassuring conventions of psychological realism.

“Smile” does not merely describe hostility. It creates a physical environment in which hostility becomes almost architectural.

“Garden” occupies a more mysterious territory. Its long, unfolding structure allows Smith's vocal to move through a succession of images and associations without establishing a stable narrative centre.

The song resembles a modernist prose poem delivered over a rhythm section that has decided to remain entirely indifferent to literary sophistication.

This is where the relationship with William S. Burroughs becomes particularly illuminating.

Burroughs's cut-up experiments treated language as a material substance that could be rearranged, disrupted and made to reveal unexpected relationships. But Smith's approach differs in one crucial respect: his language is inseparable from the act of vocal performance.

A Fall lyric on the printed page can appear fragmentary, awkward or even banal. Delivered by Smith, the same words acquire rhythmic authority, comic violence and a peculiar sense of narrative inevitability.

The voice becomes an instrument of organisation.

Consider the apparently mundane phrase in “Hexen Definitive/Strife Knot” concerning a journey taking an hour. In another context, it would be a piece of information of no conceivable artistic interest. Smith's delivery transforms it into an incantation.

The ordinary becomes ominous not because its meaning has changed but because its performance has altered the conditions under which meaning is produced.

This connects Smith not only with Burroughs but with the broader tradition of oral poetry, from the repetitive structures of Homeric epic to the spoken-word experiments of the Beats.

The comparison with Allen Ginsberg is particularly instructive. Ginsberg's Howl relies upon breath, repetition and the accumulation of images to create a poetic experience that exceeds the significance of individual statements.

Smith's method is less expansive and considerably more sarcastic, but the relationship between rhythm and verbal accumulation is unmistakable.

Perverted by Language may therefore represent the point at which The Fall most fully embraced the possibilities of the voice as an independent artistic medium.

The musicians establish the conditions. Smith constructs the disturbance.

The record is not an unsuccessful attempt to make conventional songs.

It is a successful attempt to make conventional songs increasingly irrelevant.

IV. THE WONDERFUL AND FRIGHTENING WORLD: BRIX SMITH AND THE HORROR OF A GOOD CHORUS

In 1984, The Fall committed an act of artistic treachery: they began to sound as though somebody might enjoy listening to them.

The Wonderful and Frightening World of The Fall introduced a more expansive and polished production aesthetic, associated with John Leckie, and a musical vocabulary increasingly receptive to pop, garage rock and conventional melodic structure.

The arrival of Brix Smith was central to this transformation.

Brix did not simply make The Fall more commercial. Her guitar playing, melodic instincts and broader musical sensibility introduced new possibilities into a group that had developed a formidable but potentially restrictive aesthetic identity.

The crucial distinction is between accessibility and conformity.

A group can become more accessible without abandoning its essential character. Indeed, the ability to incorporate new musical forms may be evidence of greater artistic confidence rather than diminished integrity.

The comparison with Miles Davis is particularly illuminating.

Across the decades, Davis moved from bebop and cool jazz through modal experimentation into electric fusion, repeatedly changing the musical environments in which his trumpet operated. His artistic identity survived because it did not depend upon a single instrumental configuration.

Smith's relationship with The Fall was similarly flexible. The surrounding music could change dramatically while his voice, language and peculiar understanding of rhythm remained unmistakable.

This is not to suggest that Smith possessed Davis's musical technique, compositional sophistication or particular genius.

It is to suggest that both artists understood identity as something capable of surviving stylistic transformation.

“The Lay of the Land” opens The Wonderful and Frightening World with an extraordinary collision of menace and accessibility. Its chant-like introduction and driving garage-rock arrangement suggest that the group has discovered a new capacity for theatricality.

The song's apocalyptic imagery invites comparison with Nigel Kneale's Quatermass, particularly the 1979 television serial starring John Mills. Kneale's work repeatedly imagines modern society as vulnerable to ancient, irrational and technologically mediated forces.

The connection is particularly appropriate because Smith's Gothic imagination also locates catastrophe within recognisable British environments.

The apocalypse does not require a distant planet.

It can occur in a field just outside the ring road.

The comparisons with No Blade of Grass and Z.P.G. are similarly revealing. Both films belong to a tradition of British dystopian science fiction in which social collapse emerges not from supernatural spectacle but from the breakdown of familiar institutions and assumptions.

The Fall's music frequently occupies the same territory.

“2 × 4” demonstrates the group's growing ability to produce concise, rhythmically compelling rock music without surrendering its abrasive character. The guitar sound possesses a muscular directness, while the rhythm section retains the group's characteristic insistence.

The song is almost suspiciously enjoyable.

“Copped It” and “Elves” similarly suggest a group increasingly willing to exploit the pleasures of repetition, hooks and recognisable rock forms.

The relationship between “Elves” and Iggy Pop's “I Wanna Be Your Dog” is particularly instructive. Both depend upon a primitive, repetitive riff whose apparent simplicity becomes the source of its physical power.

The Stooges had already demonstrated that musical repetition could operate as both a form of bodily excitement and an assault upon conventional notions of sophistication.

The Fall inherited this principle but subjected it to Smith's peculiar linguistic imagination.

There is also a significant change in the vocal performances.

Earlier Smith frequently delivers words as though he is engaged in an argument with the music. On The Wonderful and Frightening World, his delivery occasionally becomes more relaxed, rhythmic and almost melodic.

This does not mean that he has become a conventional singer.

It means that the distance between his voice and the surrounding arrangement has begun to change.

The production, however, introduces a fascinating contradiction.

John Leckie's more polished sound can make the instruments clearer while simultaneously rendering Smith's words more difficult to decipher. The singer's voice becomes another component of the overall texture rather than an aggressively foregrounded source of information.

The result is a peculiar form of commercial progress.

The Fall have become more accessible, but their lyrics have become harder to understand.

It is the musical equivalent of installing better lighting in a room and discovering that the furniture has disappeared.

V. THE JOHN LECKIE PROBLEM: WHEN DOES POLISH BECOME VARNISH?

The relationship between The Fall and John Leckie raises a broader question about the role of production in experimental popular music.

Production is often discussed as though it were a neutral process of improving fidelity, clarity and balance. But every production decision implies an aesthetic judgement.

To make an instrument clearer is to alter its relationship with the others. To separate the components of an arrangement is to change the way they interact. To reduce distortion, noise or accidental interference may be to remove precisely the qualities that made the recording interesting.

The Fall's early records derived much of their character from the apparent absence of conventional studio refinement. Instruments bled into one another, voices occupied uncomfortable positions in the mix, and the recordings frequently preserved the sensation of musicians performing in a shared physical space.

Leckie's approach offered a different possibility: a sound in which the group's constituent elements could be more clearly articulated.

The question is whether this clarity constituted revelation or domestication.

The comparison between the studio recording of “2 × 4” and its more ferocious television performances is revealing. The live version can possess an immediacy and physical force that the studio arrangement partially contains.

But the studio recording also makes audible the band's increasing rhythmic precision and capacity for controlled dynamics.

Neither is necessarily superior.

They represent different versions of the same artistic problem.

Brian Eno's distinction between the studio as a recording device and the studio as a compositional instrument is useful here. Once recording technology becomes part of the creative process, the producer is no longer merely documenting a performance.

The producer is helping construct the musical object itself.

The Fall's transition into the Leckie period therefore involves more than improved sound quality. It represents a shift in the relationship between performance, arrangement and recording.

The group is no longer simply making records that preserve its peculiar internal chemistry.

It is beginning to explore what that chemistry might become when subjected to more deliberate studio construction.

This development would prove enormously important for the remainder of the decade.

But it also introduces a danger.

A beautifully recorded Fall album might become so beautifully recorded that it ceases to sound sufficiently unpleasant.

There are limits to civilisation.

VI. THE UNWRITTEN FIFTH ALBUM

The most intriguing aspect of the period is not any individual recording but the alternative history suggested by the transition between Perverted by Language and The Wonderful and Frightening World.

What would have happened if The Fall had continued in the direction established by Perverted by Language?

The group might have produced increasingly long, repetitive and abstract compositions, developing the relationship between Smith's vocal performances and the musicians' rhythmic structures into something approaching a uniquely British form of experimental spoken-word rock.

The possibility is tantalising.

Imagine an album incorporating the sprawling theatricality of “Backdrop”, the verbal extremity of “Words of Expectation” and the political aggression of “New Puritan”, all subjected to the increasingly confident musicianship of the early-eighties lineup.

Such a record might have been extraordinary.

It might also have been unbearable.

There is a tendency in rock criticism to romanticise artistic trajectories that were never pursued. The abandoned experiment becomes a lost masterpiece, precisely because nobody has had to endure its actual execution.

The Fall's subsequent development suggests that the arrival of Brix and the increasing sophistication of their production were not simply commercial concessions. They may have been necessary disruptions to an aesthetic that was approaching its own limits.

A group cannot spend eternity producing variations upon Hex Enduction Hour without eventually becoming a tribute act to itself.

Smith understood this, whether consciously or instinctively.

His achievement was not the preservation of a particular sound but the maintenance of a particular attitude towards sound.

The musicians could change. The arrangements could become more melodic. The recordings could become more polished. The songs could even acquire choruses.

But the underlying hostility towards convention, sentimentality and artistic complacency remained intact.

The Fall had discovered that they could survive their own reinvention.

This was considerably more important than producing another masterpiece.

VII. FOUR ALBUMS, ONE VERY UNPLEASANT CIVILISATION

Between 1982 and 1984, The Fall moved through four distinct artistic conditions.

Hex Enduction Hour represents the culmination of a collective musical language. Its musicians possess sufficient confidence and mutual understanding to construct an extraordinary system of rhythm, repetition and verbal disruption.

Room to Live dismantles that system almost immediately, replacing monumental construction with satirical fragmentation and an apparent refusal to behave responsibly.

Perverted by Language develops the possibilities of extended form, vocal performance and linguistic abstraction, producing music that increasingly resembles a series of literary events conducted under hostile electrical conditions.

The Wonderful and Frightening World of The Fall introduces a new relationship with melody, production and popular musical conventions, demonstrating that the group's identity can survive considerable stylistic transformation.

This is not a straightforward progression from primitive to sophisticated, experimental to commercial, or authentic to compromised.

It is a succession of different answers to the same question: what happens when a group refuses to accept that its previous achievements constitute a set of instructions for its future?

There is a wonderfully revealing story about Smith's method of writing. His supposed six-day programme involves isolation, drinking, visiting the pub, becoming increasingly irritated by the people encountered there and eventually producing literature through sheer accumulated vexation.

It is an artistic method that would have horrified F. R. Leavis.

It also possesses a peculiar relationship with the theories of the Situationist International. Guy Debord's dérive proposed a form of urban wandering through which the individual might encounter the psychological geography of the modern city.

Smith's version involves going to the pub until everybody becomes intolerable.

The results are not entirely dissimilar.

Both approaches treat ordinary social experience as material capable of exposing the hidden structures of everyday life. The difference is that Debord hoped to contribute to revolutionary consciousness, while Smith frequently appeared more interested in discovering why the man at the next table was such a bastard.

This tension between intellectual sophistication and ordinary irritation is central to The Fall's achievement.

Their music is full of literary and cultural references, but it never submits to the authority of those references. William Burroughs, Captain Beefheart, the Velvet Underground, Nigel Kneale, the Stooges and the entire apparatus of European modernism are available for use, but none is treated as sacred.

Smith's artistic universe is simultaneously learned and aggressively anti-academic.

A cheap synthesiser can be as important as a literary theory. A provincial anecdote can possess the same imaginative weight as a Gothic novel. A ridiculous vocal inflection can transform an otherwise banal sentence into an event of considerable artistic significance.

This is why the distinction between the experimental and the commercial ultimately proves inadequate.

The Fall were not experimental because they refused to write accessible songs. They were experimental because they refused to accept that accessibility, difficulty, sophistication or primitiveness possessed any permanent artistic value.

The record had to justify itself on its own peculiar terms.

Sometimes that meant an immense, grinding rhythmic construction resembling the collapse of an industrial civilisation.

Sometimes it meant a scruffy political joke.

Sometimes it meant a long, abstract vocal performance whose narrative logic remained permanently inaccessible.

And sometimes, to the horror of everybody involved, it meant a rather good pop song.

The great achievement of the period is therefore not simply Hex Enduction Hour, extraordinary though that album remains.

It is the discovery that The Fall could move beyond Hex Enduction Hour without ceasing to be The Fall.

By the end of 1984, the group had survived its masterpiece, dismantled its own aesthetic assumptions, incorporated new musicians and recording techniques, and begun to explore an entirely different relationship with popular music.

The future would contain more changes, more arguments, more dismissals, more reinventions and an almost inconceivable quantity of records.

But the essential principle had been established.

The Fall were not a sound.

They were a method of making sounds behave badly.

And there was still an enormous amount of bad behaviour to come.
