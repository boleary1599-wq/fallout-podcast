THE CASTLEFORD CRYPT: EIGHT WAYS TO FAIL AT CIVILISATION

On municipal tyranny, incestuous bureaucracy, the metaphysics of football, defective machinery and the possibility that God lives inside a snare drum.

By Dr. M. Hexen, Senior Correspondent for Unlicensed Metaphysics

I. THE REPUBLIC OF THE SLIGHTLY AGGRIEVED

The history of British civilisation can be understood as a series of increasingly elaborate attempts to prevent people from enjoying themselves. The Romans introduced taxation. The Victorians perfected repression. The twentieth century invented football hooliganism, electronic dance music and the local government complaints department. Mark E. Smith, observing these developments from Greater Manchester, concluded that the whole enterprise required a considerable amount of shouting.

Across eight recordings spanning more than thirty years, The Fall present a society in which practically everything has gone wrong, although nobody can agree precisely when the trouble started. The family is unreliable. Romantic relationships resemble minor legal disputes. Pop music has developed serious structural defects. Football has become a substitute for organised warfare. Technological progress has produced synthesiser arrangements that sound like malfunctioning kitchen appliances.

And somewhere, somebody is still refusing to pay his rates.

What makes this universe compelling is its refusal to distinguish the trivial from the catastrophic. The indignity of a council demand receives approximately the same emotional intensity as confronting an incomprehensible cosmic force. The Fall's world is Kafka's The Trial relocated to a Lancashire post office, where Josef K. discovers that the charges concern an unpaid electricity bill and an allegation of inappropriate behaviour at a family christening.

There is no escape, but there is an excellent rhythm section.

II. THE TYRANNY OF ORDINARY THINGS

In Discipline and Punish, Michel Foucault describes institutions regulating behaviour through observation, classification and control. Smith's characters would probably find this insufficiently paranoid. For them, oppression begins with the council, the family, the workplace and the expectation that one should behave reasonably towards other people.

The psychobilly assault of “Pay Yer Rates” transforms a mundane economic grievance into revolutionary theatre. Guitars bend in the wrong directions, the rhythm threatens to escape from the recording, and an angry letter becomes a significant political achievement. The transcript's observation about the snotty letter gets to the heart of the comedy: an entire class war fought by post.

Yet “Entitled” approaches the same territory from the opposite direction. Its gentle arrangement suggests reconciliation. Guitars chime; the rhythm has something of Buddy Holly's innocence; the whole performance occasionally resembles an eccentric Kinks B-side. But the friendliness is deceptive. The language raises questions about possession, expectation and the right to approach another person. Who is entitled to what, and who decides?

The distinction between public and private authority collapses. The man refusing to pay his rates and the man negotiating romantic privileges inhabit the same bureaucratic universe. Both believe they have been wronged. Both are uncertain about the rules. Both are probably exhausting to deal with.

Jonathan Swift understood this distinction. The narrator of A Modest Proposal is horrifying because he presents monstrous reasoning with impeccable administrative confidence. The Fall perform the reverse manoeuvre: ordinary grievances are expressed with such disproportionate hostility that they sound like declarations of war. The revolutionary and the man complaining about the bins may be the same individual.

III. THE FAMILY AS A DEFECTIVE INSTITUTION

If the state cannot be trusted, perhaps salvation lies in the family. Unfortunately, the evidence is not encouraging.

The unstable identities suggested by “Mother-Sister!” introduce a world in which even elementary relationships require clarification. A story raised in the recording links the song to Martin Bramah's childhood confusion about family roles; it is an illuminating possibility rather than a definitive explanation. Domestic life may contain arrangements stranger than those acknowledged by official genealogy.

Freud's uncanny describes the frightening return of something familiar. The family home becomes a haunted house without requiring any ghosts. Karl Burns's agitated drumming and the collision of punk aggression with reggae-inflected rhythms create the impression of a domestic argument conducted inside a collapsing dancehall.

The alternatives are scarcely more reassuring. “On My Own” promises independence, but its synthetic atmosphere suggests isolation rather than liberation. “One Day” offers future resolution, although its distorted vocals imply that the message has been transmitted through a damaged CB radio. Even the apparently affectionate “Entitled” turns intimacy into a dispute over boundaries.

There is a Beckettian quality to these relationships. People wait for explanations, recognition or reconciliation that never arrive. In Waiting for Godot, existence becomes an endless sequence of postponements. The Fall offer a provincial variation in which Godot has gone to the pub, borrowed somebody's money and failed to return.

Smith rarely treats these situations with straightforward despair. Their ridiculousness becomes its own liberation. If family identity is unstable, romantic love suspicious and independence sounds like a defective synthesiser, perhaps the only sensible response is to laugh at the entire structure. Or write a particularly unpleasant song about it.

IV. THE ART OF GETTING IT WRONG

One of the great misconceptions about popular music is that technical improvement necessarily produces artistic improvement. The Fall spent decades disproving this proposition. Their recordings contain elements conventional production wisdom would remove: distorted voices, badly balanced instruments, repetitive structures, awkward transitions and performances seemingly assembled during an argument.

Consider the contrast between the synthetic textures of “On My Own” and the ramshackle spectacle of “The Legend of Xanadu”. The former approaches the language of 1990s dance music, suggesting Underworld and the ecstatic possibilities of the nightclub, while mysteriously withholding the drums that might make it function properly. Somebody has constructed an elaborate discotheque but forgotten to install the dance floor.

The latter takes a spectacular piece of 1960s theatrical pop and reduces it to something resembling an outsider-art reconstruction. The original, by Dave Dee, Dozy, Beaky, Mick & Tich, is a monument to dramatic orchestration and exotic fantasy. The Fall's version sounds like an amateur theatrical society reproducing the spectacle using a malfunctioning tape machine and a man impersonating a laser gun.

The transcript's comparisons with Daniel Johnston, Jandek and Wesley Willis are illuminating. Outsider music can derive fascination from the friction between intention and conventional technique. But Smith was far too knowing and strategically perverse to be an innocent outsider. His achievement was to make professionalism itself appear suspicious.

The delicate “Entitled” demonstrates the other side of the principle. Its charm, with echoes of R.E.M., the Velvet Underground and the Housemartins, seems almost too attractive for The Fall. Smith's vocal promptly introduces disorder. Meanwhile, the garage-rock attack of “One Day” achieves its force through the distortion more respectable musicians might eliminate.

Marcel Duchamp placed a urinal in an art exhibition and challenged the definition of art. The Fall placed a cheap sound effect inside a 1960s pop cover and challenged the definition of a worthwhile afternoon. Both gestures caused distress among respectable people.

V. FOOTBALL IS THE CONTINUATION OF POLITICS BY OTHER MEANS

Carl von Clausewitz described war as the continuation of politics by other means. British football supporters have spent generations exploring the reverse proposition.

The world of “Theme from Sparta F.C.” is one in which collective identity, tribal aggression and communal celebration become almost impossible to separate. Its glam-rock stomp recalls Slade and T. Rex, but its atmosphere is considerably more threatening. The football terrace becomes a miniature nation-state, complete with rituals, enemies, heroic mythology and the occasional desire to inflict grievous bodily harm.

Its connection with “Pay Yer Rates” is closer than it appears. Both recordings involve people confronting institutions that define their place in society. One expresses resentment through economic obligation; the other transforms belonging into collective aggression. The distinction is largely one of volume.

Mikhail Bakhtin's carnivalesque offers a framework: carnival temporarily overturns hierarchies through bodily excess, grotesque humour and collective disorder. But Smith's carnival is never entirely innocent. The crowd may be challenging authority or reproducing its stupidity with greater enthusiasm.

The same ambiguity haunts the domestic accusations of “Entitled” and the romantic posturing of “One Day”. Assertion and insecurity become indistinguishable. The person shouting loudest may be the person least certain of his position.

There is something theatrical about the whole enterprise. Football violence becomes glam rock. Economic protest becomes psychobilly. Domestic confusion becomes punk-reggae. The Fall treat genres as costumes to be worn badly, exchanged without warning or stolen from somebody else's dressing room. This is cultural vandalism conducted with considerable intelligence.

VI. THE MYSTICAL PROPERTIES OF DEFECTIVE MACHINERY

Machines promise efficiency, precision and control. Smith's recordings frequently employ them to produce uncertainty, repetition and elaborate disorder. The electronic atmosphere of “On My Own” suggests a future in which humanity has escaped conventional rock instrumentation. Unfortunately, the future appears to have been programmed on a cheap keyboard during somebody's lunch break.

“Slippy Floor”, or the sprawling “Y.F.O.C.” sequence heard here, offers something more ambitious. Shifting structures, accumulating rhythms, distorted guitars and disembodied speech suggest a composition assembled from separate musical organisms. A drum flourish becomes an event of almost religious significance. The transcript returns to the phrase “snare rush” so obsessively that the instrument begins to resemble a minor deity.

William S. Burroughs's cut-up technique provides one analogy. By disrupting conventional sequences of language, Burroughs sought hidden relationships and an escape from habitual thought. The Fall achieve something comparable through musical construction. Fragments collide. Voices interrupt. The listener must reconsider what kind of recording is being heard.

The strange transitions also suggest theurgy, the ancient mystical aspiration to ascend beyond ordinary existence through ritual. Repetition accumulates until it begins to resemble transcendence. This is where “Sparta F.C.” and “Slippy Floor” converge: one employs the collective chant of the football terrace, the other repetition and studio construction to suggest a journey beyond ordinary consciousness.

The football supporter and mystical initiate are closer relatives than either might admit. Both seek release from individual identity. Both participate in rituals involving shouting, repetition and peculiar clothing. Both may eventually require medical attention.

H. P. Lovecraft's cosmic horror imagines humanity confronting forces beyond comprehension. Smith's universe offers a bureaucratic variation in which the eldritch presence is accompanied by telephone messages, hospital references and the possibility that somebody has slipped on the floor. The universe may be infinite and indifferent, but somebody still has to complete the accident report.

VII. THE DEMOCRACY OF BAD TASTE

Conventional criticism depends upon stable criteria: melody, arrangement, performance, originality and emotional impact. The Fall continually undermine them. An awkward cover becomes irresistible after repeated listening. A beautifully melodic recording provokes suspicion because it sounds too accomplished. Crude garage rock may achieve greater expressive force than elaborate electronics.

Even the distinction between favourite and best collapses. This is appropriate for a group whose catalogue contains alternative versions, contradictory performances and recordings that disagree with themselves. The different incarnations of “Sparta F.C.” show how a supposedly fixed artwork becomes a moving target.

The appeal of “The Legend of Xanadu” depends on whether it is approached as failed cover, comic performance or outsider-style pop. “On My Own” may sound like underdeveloped dance music or an intriguing refusal of convention. “One Day” can appear comparatively ordinary or gloriously direct.

The transcript's final discussion offers the perfect demonstration: a defeated song can still be somebody's favourite, while the winning selection need not inspire the deepest affection. Judgement is not a tidy knockout bracket. It is a democracy in which every voter has misunderstood the question.

Walter Benjamin argued that mechanical reproduction transformed art's relationship with its audience. The Fall suggest that mechanical reproduction also provides an opportunity to make the same song sound completely different on successive occasions. Their music invites listeners to establish standards of judgement and then produces evidence that those standards are inadequate.

VIII. THE SNARE RUSH AT THE END OF HISTORY

What connects these recordings is a refusal to allow anything to remain in its proper category. The state behaves like a criminal conspiracy. The family becomes an unreliable historical document. Romance resembles a disciplinary hearing. Football turns into warfare. Pop music becomes controlled failure. Electronic technology produces primitive ritual.

And the snare drum, normally employed to maintain rhythmic order, becomes an object of metaphysical fascination.

The Fall's apparent chaos conceals a consistent worldview. Across changing personnel, production methods and fashions, Smith returns to the same suspicion: civilisation is a fragile arrangement maintained by people who have no idea what they are doing.

The minor absurdities of daily life are not distractions from the great philosophical questions. They are the great philosophical questions. Kafka's bureaucracy, Freud's unstable identities, Bakhtin's carnival, Burroughs's sabotage and Lovecraft's indifferent cosmos find counterparts in provincial Britain.

The Fall do not resolve these contradictions. They make them dance, stumble, shout and occasionally produce a perfectly decent pop melody before immediately ruining it.

Somewhere, a man is composing a letter about his rates. A family is rearranging its genealogy. A football crowd is preparing for battle. A cheap synthesiser is impersonating Underworld. A telephone message has arrived from another dimension.

And in the distance, a snare drum begins its ascent through the celestial spheres. Nobody knows where it is going. Nobody knows when it will stop.

But somebody is already complaining that it has gone on too long.
