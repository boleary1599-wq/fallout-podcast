THE CASTLEFORD CRYPT: EIGHT WAYS TO FAIL AT CIVILISATION

On municipal tyranny, incestuous bureaucracy, the metaphysics of football, defective machinery and the possibility that God lives inside a snare drum.

By Dr. M. Hexen, Senior Correspondent for Unlicensed Metaphysics

I. THE REPUBLIC OF THE SLIGHTLY AGGRIEVED

The history of British civilisation can be understood as a series of increasingly elaborate attempts to prevent people from enjoying themselves. The Romans introduced taxation. The Victorians perfected repression. The twentieth century invented football hooliganism, electronic dance music and the local government complaints department. Mark E. Smith, observing these developments from Greater Manchester, concluded that the whole enterprise required a considerable amount of shouting.

Across eight recordings spanning more than thirty years, The Fall present a society in which practically everything has gone wrong, although nobody can agree precisely when the trouble started. The family is unreliable. Romantic relationships resemble minor legal disputes. Pop music has developed serious structural defects. Football has become a substitute for organised warfare. The promise of technological progress has produced synthesiser arrangements that sound like malfunctioning kitchen appliances. And somewhere, somebody is still refusing to pay his rates.

What makes this universe compelling is its refusal to distinguish between the trivial and the catastrophic. A council-tax demand receives approximately the same emotional intensity as confronting an incomprehensible cosmic force. The Fall's world is Kafka's The Trial relocated to a Lancashire post office, where Josef K. discovers that the charges against him concern an unpaid electricity bill and an allegation of inappropriate behaviour at a family christening. There is no escape, but there is an excellent rhythm section.

II. THE TYRANNY OF ORDINARY THINGS

In Discipline and Punish, Michel Foucault describes institutions regulating human behaviour through observation, classification and control. Smith's characters would probably find this analysis insufficiently paranoid. For them, oppression begins considerably earlier: with the council, the family, the workplace and the expectation that one should behave reasonably towards other people.

The furious psychobilly assault of Pay Your Rates transforms a mundane economic grievance into revolutionary theatre. Its guitars bend in the wrong directions, its rhythm threatens to escape from the recording, and its protagonist appears to regard the composition of an angry letter as a significant political achievement. Yet Entitled approaches the same territory from the opposite direction. Its unexpectedly gentle arrangement suggests social reconciliation. The guitars chime, the rhythm has something of Buddy Holly's early rock-and-roll innocence, and the performance occasionally resembles an eccentric Kinks B-side. But the apparent friendliness is deceptive.

The song raises questions about possession, expectation and the right to approach another person. The title becomes an accusation: who is entitled to what, and who decides? The distinction between public and private authority collapses. The man refusing to pay his rates and the man negotiating romantic privileges inhabit the same bureaucratic universe. Both believe they have been wronged. Both are uncertain about the rules. Both are probably exhausting to deal with.

Jonathan Swift understood this distinction. The narrator of A Modest Proposal is horrifying because he presents monstrous reasoning with impeccable administrative confidence. The Fall perform the reverse manoeuvre: ordinary grievances expressed with such disproportionate hostility that they resemble declarations of war. In Smith's Britain, the revolutionary and the man complaining about the bins may be the same individual.

III. THE FAMILY AS A DEFECTIVE INSTITUTION

If the state cannot be trusted, perhaps salvation lies in the family. Unfortunately, the evidence is not encouraging. The unstable identities suggested by Mother-Sister introduce a world in which even elementary relationships require clarification. Its possible origins in Martin Bramah's childhood confusion about family roles suggest domestic arrangements stranger than those acknowledged by official genealogy.

Freud's uncanny becomes useful here. The familiar is frightening because something supposedly known reveals itself to be uncertain. The family home becomes a haunted house without requiring ghosts. Karl Burns's manic drumming and the collision of punk aggression with reggae-inflected rhythms create the impression of a domestic argument conducted inside a collapsing dancehall.

Yet the alternatives are scarcely more reassuring. On My Own promises independence, but its synthetic atmosphere suggests isolation rather than liberation. One Day offers future resolution, although its distorted vocals imply the message has been transmitted through a damaged CB radio. Even the affectionate Entitled turns intimacy into a dispute over boundaries.

There is a Beckettian quality to these relationships. People wait for explanations, recognition or reconciliation that never quite arrive. In Waiting for Godot, existence becomes an endless sequence of postponements. The Fall offer a provincial variation in which Godot has gone to the pub, borrowed somebody's money and failed to return. Smith rarely treats these situations with straightforward despair. If family identity is unstable, romantic love suspicious and independence sounds like a defective synthesiser, perhaps the sensible response is to laugh at the entire structure. Or write an unpleasant song about it.

IV. THE ART OF GETTING IT WRONG

One misconception about popular music is that technical improvement necessarily produces artistic improvement. The Fall spent decades disproving this proposition. Their recordings contain elements conventional production wisdom would remove: distorted voices, badly balanced instruments, repetitive structures, awkward transitions and performances apparently assembled during an argument. Yet these defects often become the most memorable features.

Consider the contrast between the synthetic textures of On My Own and the ramshackle spectacle of The Legend of Xanadu. The former approaches the language of 1990s dance music, suggesting Underworld and Detroit techno, while mysteriously withholding the drums that might make the enterprise function. Somebody has constructed an elaborate discotheque but forgotten to install the dance floor.

The latter takes a spectacular piece of 1960s theatrical pop and reduces it to something resembling an outsider-art reconstruction. The original by Dave Dee, Dozy, Beaky, Mick & Tich is a monument to dramatic orchestration and exotic fantasy. The Fall's version sounds like an amateur theatrical society reproducing it with a malfunctioning tape machine and a man impersonating a laser gun.

Comparisons with Daniel Johnston, Jandek and Wesley Willis illuminate the fascination of expressive awkwardness. But Smith was too knowing and culturally literate to be an innocent outsider. His achievement was making professionalism itself suspicious. The delicate Entitled demonstrates the other side of this principle. Its charm evokes R.E.M., the Velvet Underground and even the Housemartins, until Smith's vocal introduces the necessary disorder. The straightforward garage attack of One Day achieves force through precisely the roughness respectable musicians might eliminate.

Marcel Duchamp placed a urinal in an exhibition and challenged the definition of art. The Fall placed a cheap sound effect inside a 1960s pop cover and challenged the definition of a worthwhile afternoon. Both gestures distressed respectable people.

V. FOOTBALL IS THE CONTINUATION OF POLITICS BY OTHER MEANS

Carl von Clausewitz described war as the continuation of politics by other means. British football supporters have spent generations exploring the reverse proposition. Theme from Sparta F.C. inhabits a world where collective identity, tribal aggression and communal celebration are impossible to separate.

Its glam-rock stomp recalls Slade and T. Rex, but the atmosphere is more threatening. The terrace becomes a miniature nation-state, complete with rituals, enemies, heroic mythology and the occasional desire to inflict grievous bodily harm. Its connection with Pay Your Rates is closer than it seems. Both involve people confronting institutions that define their place in society. One expresses resentment through economic obligation; the other transforms belonging into collective aggression. The distinction is largely one of volume.

Mikhail Bakhtin's carnivalesque offers a framework: established hierarchies temporarily overturned through bodily excess, grotesque humour and collective disorder. But Smith's carnival is never innocent. The crowd may challenge authority or merely reproduce its stupidity with greater enthusiasm. The same ambiguity haunts the domestic accusations of Entitled and the aggressive romantic posturing of One Day. The person shouting loudest may be least certain of his position.

Football violence becomes glam rock. Economic protest becomes psychobilly. Domestic confusion becomes punk-reggae. The Fall treat genres as costumes that can be worn badly, exchanged without warning or stolen from somebody else's dressing room. This is cultural vandalism conducted with intelligence.

VI. THE MYSTICAL PROPERTIES OF DEFECTIVE MACHINERY

Machines promise efficiency, precision and control. Smith's recordings employ them to produce uncertainty, repetition and disorder. The electronic atmosphere of On My Own suggests a future beyond conventional rock instrumentation. Unfortunately, the future appears to have been programmed on a cheap keyboard during somebody's lunch break.

Slippy Floor offers something more ambitious. Its shifting structure, accumulating rhythms, distorted guitars and disembodied speech suggest several separate musical organisms welded together. The recording begins in recognisable rock territory before expanding into increasingly strange environments. A snare flourish becomes an event of almost religious significance.

William S. Burroughs's cut-up technique offers one analogy. By disrupting conventional sequences, Burroughs sought hidden relationships and an escape from habitual thought. The Fall achieve something comparable through musical construction. Fragments collide. Voices interrupt. The listener must reconsider what sort of recording is being heard.

The transitions also suggest theurgy: attempted ascent beyond ordinary existence through ritual engagement with divine powers. The music accumulates intensity until repetition resembles transcendence. This is where Sparta F.C. and Slippy Floor unexpectedly converge. One employs the collective football chant; the other repetition and studio construction to suggest a journey beyond ordinary consciousness. Both transform individual experience through rhythmic intensity. The football supporter and mystical initiate are closer relatives than either would admit. Both seek release from ordinary identity, participate in rituals involving shouting and peculiar clothing, and may eventually require medical attention.

H. P. Lovecraft's cosmic horror imagines humanity confronting incomprehensible forces. Smith's universe offers a bureaucratic variation in which the eldritch presence comes with telephone messages, hospital references and the possibility that somebody slipped on the floor. The universe may be infinite and indifferent, but somebody must complete the accident report.

VII. THE DEMOCRACY OF BAD TASTE

There is something revealing about the difficulty of deciding which Fall recordings are good. Conventional criticism depends on relatively stable criteria: melody, arrangement, performance, originality and emotional impact. The Fall undermine them all.

A technically awkward cover may become irresistible after repeated listening. A melodic recording provokes suspicion because it sounds too accomplished. A crude garage performance achieves greater force than an elaborate electronic experiment. Even the distinction between favourite and best collapses.

The multiple incarnations of Sparta F.C. demonstrate this instability. A song can be a muscular anthem, a glam-inflected assault or a ponderous trudge depending on the version. The supposedly fixed artwork becomes a moving target. Xanadu's appeal depends on whether one hears a failed cover, comic performance or outsider-style pop. On My Own can be an underdeveloped dance experiment or an intriguing refusal of dance convention. One Day may seem ordinary or gloriously direct.

These disagreements are not obstacles to understanding The Fall. They are central to it. The music invites listeners to establish standards of judgement and immediately provides evidence those standards are inadequate. It is a democracy in which every voter has misunderstood the question.

VIII. THE SNARE RUSH AT THE END OF HISTORY

What connects these recordings is a refusal to let anything remain in its proper category. The state behaves like a criminal conspiracy. The family becomes an unreliable historical document. Romance resembles a disciplinary hearing. Football turns into warfare. Pop music becomes controlled failure. Electronic technology produces primitive ritual.

And the snare drum, normally employed to maintain rhythmic order, becomes an object of metaphysical fascination.

This is the Fall paradox. The music appears chaotic, yet its worldview is consistent. Across changing personnel, production methods and fashions, Smith returns to the suspicion that civilisation is a fragile arrangement maintained by people who have no idea what they are doing. The minor absurdities of daily life are not distractions from the great philosophical questions. They are the great philosophical questions.

Kafka's bureaucracy, Freud's unstable family, Bakhtin's carnival, Burroughs's sabotage and Lovecraft's indifferent cosmos find counterparts in provincial Britain. The Fall resolve none of these contradictions. They make them dance, stumble, shout and occasionally produce a decent melody before ruining it.

Somewhere a man is composing a letter about his rates. A family rearranges its genealogy. A football crowd prepares for battle. A cheap synthesiser impersonates Underworld. A telephone message arrives from another dimension. In the distance, a snare drum begins ascending the celestial spheres.

Nobody knows where it is going. Nobody knows when it will stop.

But somebody is already complaining that it has gone on too long.
