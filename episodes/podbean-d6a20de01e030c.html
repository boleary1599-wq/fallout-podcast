THE CHRISTMAS THAT ATE PRESTWICH

On Mark E. Smith, defective festive rituals, astrology, eldritch horror, the industrial manufacture of goodwill and the unreasonably profound significance of a kazoo.

By Professor C. R. Scanlon, Department of Seasonal Hostility

I. THE MOST WONDERFUL TIME OF THE YEAR, APPARENTLY

Christmas is the annual British experiment in compulsory happiness. Shops acquire a soundtrack of emotional blackmail, public houses fill with people pretending to enjoy one another's company, and the calendar becomes an administrative threat. For two weeks the country agrees to behave as though the simple passage of time constitutes a collective achievement. Mark E. Smith, who devoted much of his career to exposing the fraudulence of consensus, was therefore an unusually appropriate Christmas commentator.

In a 1990 interview with Simon Reynolds, Smith offered an almost suspiciously reasonable position: he did not mind Christmas itself; what irritated him was the way it dragged on and brought the country to a halt. This is a subtler distinction than the cartoon of Smith as a permanently furious man might suggest. He was not opposed to celebration. He objected to celebration becoming a compulsory institution, and especially to institutions demanding his participation on their own terms.

The distinction runs through The Fall's music. These records often contain the basic ingredients of communal enjoyment: dancing, choruses, repetitive hooks, jokes, drinking songs and bursts of almost indecent enthusiasm. What they refuse is the obligation to package those pleasures as reassurance. Their rhythms invite you inside; the voice asks who let you in. In this respect The Fall might be the most authentically festive band in Britain, provided one's definition of festivity includes the possibility of being told to leave.

Mikhail Bakhtin's account of carnival in *Rabelais and His World* provides a useful starting point. Carnival briefly overturns the normal social order through laughter, excess, bodily grotesquery and mockery of authority. The trouble with modern Christmas is that the overturning has itself become official. The revolution has a sponsorship arrangement and finishes at six. Smith's art restores a little genuine disorder to the ceremony, like an uninvited relative explaining the family's financial history over the trifle.

II. THE UNRELIABLE MAGIC OF CHRISTMAS

Consider the unlikely convergence of a natal astrology chart, a Christmas party game and a reading of H. P. Lovecraft. These are not merely eccentric amusements. Each offers a different system for converting the bewildering into something comprehensible. Astrology arranges a life according to the positions of celestial bodies; a guessing game converts music into clues and answers; supernatural fiction insists that the universe contains patterns too vast or hostile to understand. The Fall's own aesthetic operates in the space between these propositions.

An anonymous astrological reading of Smith's birth chart produces a collection of traits that sound plausible enough: iconoclasm, artistic ability, a forceful personality, possible preoccupation with health, difficulties with anger and the prospect of significant achievement. The problem, as with so much astrology, is that the description could apply to several musicians, three neighbours and a particularly difficult geography teacher. The Barnum effect — the tendency to recognise ourselves in sufficiently general personality descriptions — is a useful defence against the apparent precision of the exercise.

Yet the exercise also demonstrates something important about Smith's public persona. His biography is so densely populated with confrontations, pronouncements and bizarre incidents that nearly any interpretive system can claim to have discovered its secret. Class analysis, psychoanalysis, occultism, music journalism and the language of professional wrestling all seem intermittently convincing. Smith was both unusually legible and impossible to pin down. He encouraged the construction of myths while remaining stubbornly resistant to the people constructing them.

His lyrics perform the same manoeuvre. A phrase may sound like an instruction, a prophecy, an overheard conversation or the title of an unpublished science-fiction paperback. Meaning is offered with great confidence and then withdrawn. The listener becomes a kind of amateur astrologer, discovering significance in the arrangements of verbal stars.

III. WHEN THE KAZOO BECOMES THE TEXT

There is something magnificently revealing about trying to identify Fall songs from kazoo performances. Strip a composition of Smith's voice, the production, the instrumental timbre and much of its original rhythm, and what remains? Sometimes a recognisable melodic structure. Sometimes a sequence of pitches that could be mistaken for a small animal experiencing a medical emergency.

The kazoo is a democratic and therefore dangerous instrument. It requires no conventional embouchure, offers almost no tonal dignity and reduces the distinction between virtuosity and enthusiasm to a narrow strip of vibrating membrane. In the hands of a child it can expose the melodic skeleton of a song with greater honesty than an expensive guitar solo. It can also expose the alarming possibility that a song's skeleton was never especially sturdy.

This is a serious issue for The Fall, whose greatest recordings often derive their power from elements that standard musical transcription cannot adequately capture. The opening of “How I Wrote 'Elastic Man'” is not simply a collection of notes. It is a rhythmic arrangement, a performance of irritation, an argument between guitar and voice, a comic drama in which the very act of making a record seems to be questioned. “No Xmas for John Quays” similarly depends on its nervous attack and ugly momentum. Reproduce the melody without the aggression and the result is a peculiar nursery rhyme.

The question recalls John Cage's insistence that music is not confined to conventional instrumental expression. But where Cage made an aesthetic proposition out of attention to sound, the Fall made a practical joke out of the gap between musical composition and musical event. A record is not the score of a song; it is what happened when those particular people played it in that particular room, with all the mistakes and antagonisms included.

The kazoo is therefore a tiny instrument of critical destruction. It turns rock mythology into a parlour game. It forces the allegedly serious listener to confront the possibility that the great art of the twentieth century can be reduced to a child blowing through a piece of plastic. One suspects Smith might have appreciated this more than a respectful orchestral tribute, provided he was allowed to insult the orchestra afterwards.

IV. A CHRISTMAS CAROL, WITH SOMEONE MISSING

The peculiar charm of The Fall's Christmas material lies in its refusal to produce the expected sentiment. “Xmas With Simon”, sometimes rendered “Christmastide”, introduces recognisably seasonal imagery into an atmosphere of lyrical mischief and musical unease. “No Xmas for John Quays” turns the holiday into an occasion for exclusion, a phrase whose very grammar seems to have been left outside in the cold. Even “We Wish You a Protein Christmas” makes the conventional seasonal blessing sound like a dietary instruction issued by a malfunctioning government department.

The latter title is especially instructive. Christmas songs depend upon the repetition of familiar phrases: peace, goodwill, snow, home, children, bells. Smith's language interrupts this circuitry with a word that belongs to another system altogether. Protein belongs to nutrition, chemistry and bodily maintenance. Insert it into a festive formula and the whole mechanism becomes visible. Suddenly the ritual sounds less like an expression of affection than a set of prescribed inputs.

The move has something in common with the détournement practised by the Situationists, who repurposed existing cultural material to expose its assumptions. It also resembles the comedy of Spike Milligan, in which a tiny alteration to familiar language can produce an entirely new and unreasonable universe. Smith was not necessarily pursuing either programme consciously. His achievement was to make the techniques feel native to the northern vernacular rather than imported from an avant-garde handbook.

The phrase “Christmas for junkies” likewise carries a bleak comic charge. Dickens built *A Christmas Carol* around the possibility of redemption through seasonal recognition; The Fall's Christmas is less willing to promise that the vulnerable will be remembered. The holiday's supposed universality makes its exclusions more obvious. Who is entitled to the celebration? Who is left outside? Who gets the warm house, and who gets a song with a hostile title?

This is not an argument that Smith was a sentimental social critic in disguise. He could be as cruel as the systems he mocked. But his best writing exposes the violence concealed in supposedly harmless expressions. A Christmas greeting, after sufficient Fall treatment, can become a warning.

V. THE FALLEN WOMEN, THE BAUBLES AND THE COMMODITY FORM

Once a band becomes a cult, the cult begins manufacturing objects. A Christmas tree decorated with portraits of former Fall members is a particularly beautiful expression of this tendency. It transforms a notoriously unstable personnel history into a domestic display of continuity. Each departure, dismissal, return and feud becomes a small festive ornament. A band famous for refusing permanence is commemorated by objects designed to be stored in a box and brought out at precisely the same time each year.

Walter Benjamin's essay on art in the age of mechanical reproduction is an obvious reference, though a painted bauble complicates the argument by being both an individually made object and a souvenir of endlessly reproduced cultural images. There is also a wonderfully absurd echo of religious reliquaries: a material token through which the faithful maintain contact with an absent figure. A bauble bearing Karl Burns or Marcia Schofield is not merely decoration. It is a miniature shrine to a particular configuration of musical hostility.

The phenomenon becomes stranger still when Fall-related Christmas cards and novelty products enter the picture. “Hit the North Pole” is a pun of such dreadful inevitability that it seems to have existed since the invention of Santa Claus, patiently waiting for a graphic designer to discover it. Here the cultural industry demonstrates its extraordinary ability to transform any oppositional identity into merchandise. Even Smith's suspicion of sentiment can be sold back to the faithful as a sentimental object.

The Fallen Women, an all-female Fall tribute project, offer a more interesting form of commemoration. A cover band can reproduce the sounds, but it can also reopen the songs to new performers, new audiences and new social meanings. The Fall's identity was never reducible to a single voice, however dominant that voice became. The accumulated work of musicians across decades makes the catalogue available for appropriation, affectionate distortion and argument.

The distinction matters. A Christmas card fixes a cultural identity into a recognisable image. A performance puts it back into motion. One can hang a portrait of Smith on the tree; one cannot hang the actual experience of a Fall gig without significant damage to the branches.

VI. THE COLOUR OUT OF PRESTWICH

The most revealing festive tradition may be the British Christmas ghost story. Long before the television adaptations of M. R. James became a seasonal institution, winter storytelling offered a socially acceptable opportunity to introduce terror into the domestic circle. The family gathered around the fire and listened to evidence that the world beyond the curtains was not necessarily benign.

A reading of Lovecraft's “The Colour Out of Space” makes a curious but entirely defensible addition to this tradition. The story describes an incomprehensible presence that contaminates the familiar world, transforming landscape, living things and perception itself. It is not a Christmas story, but its governing principle is ideally suited to The Fall: the ordinary environment has been invaded by a force that refuses explanation.

Lovecraft's cosmic horror and James's antiquarian ghost stories differ in scale. James generally discovers the dreadful in old documents, ecclesiastical buildings and neglected objects; Lovecraft imagines a universe in which human categories are pitifully inadequate. Yet both depend upon the corruption of the familiar. Smith's finest lyrics operate on a similar boundary. Streets, houses, shops and pubs acquire the density of occult sites. An apparently routine statement becomes a message from a system whose rules are unavailable.

There is a temptation to describe Smith as a Lovecraftian writer with a rhythm section. This is only partly useful. Lovecraft's narrators tend to be overwhelmed by their discoveries; Smith sounds more inclined to complain about the organisation responsible for them. His cosmos is hostile, certainly, but it is also badly managed. If an unspeakable entity appeared in Prestwich, one suspects the first response would concern its punctuality.

The Christmas ghost story restores to the holiday something the advertising industry works hard to remove: the knowledge that celebration and dread can occupy the same room. It is a useful reminder that the ancient winter festival was never solely about comfort. Darkness, death, misrule and uncertainty have always been part of the package.

VII. A PROTEIN-PROTECTION CHRISTMAS

The Fall's relationship with Christmas ultimately illuminates a larger contradiction in the band's work. Smith was a ferocious critic of conformity, yet he repeatedly returned to the structures of collective ritual: repeated riffs, shared chants, familiar covers, seasonal formulas and the simple pleasure of people making a racket together. He wanted the energy of the crowd without surrendering to its stupidity. He wanted the celebration without the sentimental coercion.

This may explain why his Christmas material feels more enduring than much of the music manufactured specifically for the season. It recognises that festivities are made of contradictory things: affection and resentment, generosity and exhaustion, memory and embarrassment, genuine delight and the occasional desire to murder a relative over the washing-up.

The Fall did not resolve these contradictions. They amplified them, then occasionally interrupted the amplification to tell a joke.

Somewhere, a child is playing a Fall song on a kazoo. Somewhere else, an astrologer is confidently identifying the character of a man who spent much of his life refusing to be identified. A bauble bearing a former drummer hangs from a branch. A Lovecraftian colour seeps into the garden. The Christmas cards have arrived, and none of them is particularly good.

There are worse ways to celebrate the season. At least nobody has put on “Wonderful Christmastime”.
