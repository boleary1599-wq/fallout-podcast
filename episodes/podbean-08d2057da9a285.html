THE MACHINERY OF CONSOLATION

The Fall Against the Comfort of Meaning

By Dr. M. Hexen, Senior Correspondent for Unlicensed Metaphysics

I. THE RIGHT TO BE UNHAPPY There is something profoundly suspicious about a song that tells you everything will be all right. The reassurance arrives before the evidence, like a government minister announcing that an industrial accident is entirely under control while the neighbouring cattle begin to glow.

Popular music has built an enormous industry around this sort of emotional fraud. Things get better. Love conquers all. Tomorrow belongs to you. Even the most desolate singer eventually discovers a silver lining, usually accompanied by a string section and a sudden improvement in the weather.

Mark E. Smith was having none of it.

Yet Life Just Bounces is among the strangest attempts at consolation in The Fall's catalogue. Its title suggests the philosophy of a motivational speaker employed by a moderately unsuccessful insurance company. Life is unpredictable, certainly, but the important thing is to remain cheerful, maintain perspective and perhaps purchase additional cover for accidental damage.

The music offers an equally questionable form of encouragement. Its descending and ascending figures have the synthetic optimism of an early-nineties television ident. The guitar and bass pursue one another in a peculiar chromatic game, like two badly programmed electronic table-tennis bats. There is something almost touching about the persistence of the arrangement. It keeps trying to be cheerful. It cannot quite remember why.

The song's relationship with Elton John and Kiki Dee's Don't Go Breaking My Heart makes the whole enterprise more perverse. The original belongs to the great British tradition of pop reassurance: two wealthy entertainers promising emotional dependability while dressed as the entertainment at an unusually expensive wedding reception. The Fall borrow the basic sensation of romantic buoyancy and replace its foundations with institutional weirdness.

Drug companies, chemists, professional misconduct and disturbing allegations enter a song apparently designed to make everyone feel better. Smith delivers these fragments with the distracted satisfaction of a man reading an exceptionally peculiar local newspaper while waiting for his prescription.

Albert Camus might have appreciated the arrangement. The absurd is not merely the discovery that life has no satisfactory explanation; it is the spectacle of human beings continuing to demand one. Smith's answer is neither despair nor conventional optimism. Life bounces because it does. No warranty is provided.

Across the band's catalogue, happiness is rarely impossible. It is simply never allowed to become respectable.

II. A NATION OF WRONG PLACES The Fall's England is an extraordinary country. Its landscape contains industrial estates, forgotten kingdoms, sinister entertainers, dubious institutions, mysterious officials and the occasional man who appears to have escaped from a seventeenth-century woodcut.

In Wrong Place, Right Time, geography becomes an ideological problem.

The song is built around an enormous, stubborn bass figure, with the guitars and drums providing an almost militaristic insistence. It does not so much develop as occupy territory. There is a swagger to the arrangement that recalls a political procession whose organisers have neglected to establish precisely what they are celebrating.

William of Orange appears in the lyrical landscape, dragging the song towards the contested history of British sovereignty. This is typical Smith: a historical figure wanders into a rock song and suddenly the entire business sounds like a constitutional crisis conducted in a pub car park.

The title itself is a miniature philosophical trap. Being in the wrong place at the right time suggests either extraordinary luck or an administrative catastrophe. The categories are unstable. The same event might constitute a triumph, a disaster or a scheduling error.

The Fall repeatedly exploit this confusion. Their music occupies the territory between English national mythology and the ordinary indignities of living in England. The grand historical narrative is constantly interrupted by the sound of someone trying to start a van.

Consider Frightened, where the youthful Smith surveys his surroundings from a position of simultaneous superiority and alarm. Its repetitive keyboard figure, abrasive guitar and sharply propelled drumming establish a world in which confidence and anxiety are virtually indistinguishable.

The narrator is outside the action, looking in, but also terrified that the people inside might notice him. It is existentialism with a bus pass.

Camus's outsider stands apart from society because its conventions have become incomprehensible. Smith's outsider stands apart because he has inspected those conventions and found the participants to be complete idiots. The distinction is subtle but important.

Frightened possesses the insolence of early punk without quite sharing punk's faith in liberation. Its protagonist does not want to overthrow society. He wants society to stop looking at him.

By Wrong Place, Right Time, the outsider has apparently developed imperial ambitions. The nervous observer has become a territorial claimant. Yet the basic musical method remains remarkably consistent: repetition as an instrument of psychological pressure.

The Fall's great political achievement may have been to reveal that Englishness is less a stable national identity than an endlessly recurring argument conducted by people who have forgotten what started it.

III. FOLK MUSIC FOR PEOPLE WHO DISLIKE FOLK MUSIC British folk music has suffered terribly at the hands of its admirers.

There is the worthy historical reconstruction, the lovingly restored instrument, the carefully researched regional dialect, the earnest gentleman explaining the significance of a seventeenth-century agricultural dispute before beginning a song that lasts eleven minutes.

There is also the opposite tradition: music that survives because somebody remembers approximately how it goes, changes the words, gets the rhythm wrong and passes the resulting mutation to another equally unreliable individual.

The Fall belong emphatically to the second tradition.

Stephen Song approaches folk music from an unexpected direction. Its rolling, almost processional rhythm, unusual structural turns and Gavin Friday's theatrical vocal presence give it the atmosphere of a ceremonial performance whose original purpose has been lost.

There are suggestions of medieval pageantry, sea shanties and the slightly dubious paganism of British progressive folk. One can imagine a recorder being introduced, followed shortly afterwards by an argument about whether the recorder is historically authentic.

But The Fall's relationship with folk is far more interesting than the question of whether they can reproduce its familiar sounds.

The group were a modern folk band in the sense that their music continually absorbed rumours, overheard conversations, local characters, historical fragments and material of uncertain provenance. Their songs resemble stories passed between people who are not particularly interested in preserving the original version.

This is also why their apparent incompetence can be so productive.

The guitars on Frightened scratch and collide with the keyboard figure, threatening to become excessively busy without ever entirely escaping the song's primitive architecture. The music is simultaneously disciplined and amateurish. Its confidence seems to depend upon nobody noticing the contradictions.

Compare that with Stephen Song, where the more consciously arranged folk atmosphere risks becoming an aesthetic costume. The distinction is not between sophistication and simplicity. It is between music that has inherited a tradition and music that appears to be impersonating one.

The folk revival, at its most solemn, sometimes resembles a museum in which visitors are forbidden to touch the exhibits. The Fall would steal the exhibits, put them in the wrong rooms and insist they had always belonged there.

Their records also participate in a peculiarly British anti-design tradition. The stark presentation of Live at the Witch Trials appears almost hostile to the potential purchaser. Even its title has the mischievous power to persuade an unsuspecting buyer that a studio album must be a concert recording.

It is an aesthetic of deliberate discouragement. Before the music begins, the sleeve has already asked whether you are absolutely certain you want to get involved.

IV. THE COVER VERSION AS INDUSTRIAL SABOTAGE Most cover versions perform one of two functions. They honour the original or they demonstrate the performer's ability to improve upon it.

The Fall frequently attempt a third possibility: making the original song regret having been written.

Stay Away (Old White Train) is an especially strange case. The source material, associated with Johnny Paycheck, comes equipped with the familiar apparatus of American country and rockabilly mythology: travel, departure, the open road, the train as a symbol of freedom or escape.

The Fall's version offers a considerably less glamorous transport service.

Its train effects sound like something discovered in a school drama cupboard. Carl Burns's lead vocal approaches Iggy Pop territory without acquiring the expected glamour. The arrangement has an oddly functional quality, as though the group were filling time while Smith attended an urgent meeting with a bookmaker.

Yet this apparent awkwardness reveals something important about the band's aesthetic.

A conventional cover version invites the listener to admire a transformation. The Fall's versions often expose the mechanics of transformation itself. The borrowed song remains recognisable, but its original cultural authority has been removed.

The train is no longer a romantic symbol of escape. It is a train noise, inserted into a recording because somebody has decided the song requires one.

This is the logic of British absurdist comedy, from The Goon Show to Vic Reeves Big Night Out: take an established convention, reproduce its external features with excessive literalism and allow the entire structure to collapse under the weight of its own stupidity.

The same principle animates Life Just Bounces. Elton John's emotional machinery survives, but it has been connected to the wrong electrical supply.

The results are not necessarily beautiful. Indeed, Stay Away can sound genuinely underpowered beside the monstrous propulsion of Crop-Dust. But beauty is not always the point.

The Fall treat popular music as a collection of reusable industrial components. A bass line here, a historical reference there, an American country song retrieved from a skip. Everything can be dismantled, reassembled and returned to the public without a certificate of safety.

V. A HOLE IN TIME, SOMEWHERE NEAR THE INDUSTRIAL ESTATE *Crop-Dust* presents perhaps the most complete demonstration of Smith's peculiar imaginative universe.

Its rhythmic foundation is insistent and hypnotic, while the guitar introduces an exotic, modal quality that recalls both the more adventurous end of sixties garage rock and the European experimental tradition. There are filtered textures, abrupt changes in emphasis and an atmosphere of permanently suspended catastrophe.

The song seems to occupy a landscape where time has become physically unreliable.

Film crews, extraordinary weather, damaged buildings, military figures and historical apparitions coexist in a narrative that refuses to distinguish between science fiction and ordinary observation.

It is tempting to invoke J. G. Ballard, whose fiction repeatedly turns modern landscapes into laboratories for psychological disaster. Yet Smith's method is less clinically controlled. Ballard might explain how a motorway interchange reveals the collapse of Western civilisation. Smith would report that the interchange had been visited by an unidentified German soldier, a television cameraman and somebody's former landlord.

The effect is closer to a damaged broadcast from a parallel Britain.

The music does not illustrate the story in the conventional sense. It establishes a pressure system in which the fragments become temporarily plausible. Repetition supplies continuity where narrative refuses to do so.

This is why The Fall's relationship with krautrock matters. The motorik principle, in its broadest sense, offers a method of moving forward without the emotional signposting of conventional rock. Progress becomes a mechanical condition rather than a dramatic achievement.

But Crop-Dust is not simply a motorik exercise. Its strange modal guitar gestures suggest another geography altogether: the imagined East of psychedelic garage records, where a few unfamiliar intervals could conjure an entire fictitious continent.

The Fall combine these traditions without resolving them. European repetition meets American garage primitivism, while Smith supplies an English apocalypse assembled from fragments of the wrong centuries.

The result is an extraordinary form of musical archaeology. Every layer suggests a different civilisation. None can be trusted.

And somewhere beneath the ruins, the rhythm continues.

VI. SPIDERS, WELLIES AND THE LAST GREAT REFUSAL By the time of *Kinder of Spine*, The Fall had reached an extraordinary stage of artistic development: the point at which almost everything that might conventionally be regarded as deterioration could also be understood as an aesthetic resource.

Smith's voice had become an increasingly unstable instrument, liable to interrupt, smear or dismantle whatever arrangement surrounded it. The band could still generate enormous momentum, but the relationship between musical structure and vocal performance was no longer remotely democratic.

Kinder of Spine is a splendidly ridiculous example.

The song's garage-rock framework has the primitive enthusiasm of something discovered on a forgotten Pebbles compilation. The vocal performance, however, belongs to a different dimension. Words become noises, noises become characters, and the suggestion of spiders produces a miniature bestiary of linguistic confusion.

It is possible to imagine a respectable singer approaching this material and asking what the lyrics are supposed to mean. Smith's response, one suspects, would have been to invent another spider.

Against it, Venice with the Girls offers a comparatively recognisable form of pleasure: an engaging, driving indie-rock arrangement with a straightforward melodic appeal. Yet even here the apparent accessibility is complicated by Smith's peculiar delivery and the sense that some private joke has escaped into the recording.

Venice itself is a useful symbol. The city represents centuries of cultivated European beauty, artistic sophistication and expensive tourism. The Fall turn it into another location in their endless catalogue of suspect destinations.

The comparison recalls the band's fascination with marginal figures and unofficial local mythology. Every British town possesses characters whose biographies survive almost entirely through anecdote: the eccentric wanderer, the habitual shouter, the person who becomes famous without ever doing anything conventionally noteworthy.

Such people are living folk songs.

Their stories accumulate details, contradictions and embellishments until the distinction between biography and legend becomes impossible to maintain. The Fall's music operates by precisely the same principle.

The late Smith increasingly resembled one of these figures himself: a performer whose authority derived not from technical perfection but from the sheer persistence of an unmistakable personality.

There is something profoundly moving about that refusal to become dignified.

Rock music generally expects its veterans to achieve one of two acceptable conditions: graceful maturity or heroic retirement. Smith chose neither. He continued to sound difficult, damaged, amused, irritated and occasionally magnificent.

His late performances did not conceal the passage of time. They weaponised it.

VII. NO REFUNDS, NO EXPLANATIONS What connects these recordings is not a single musical style but a systematic refusal of the promises that styles normally make.

Pop promises emotional reassurance. Folk promises continuity with the past. Rockabilly promises escape. Psychedelia promises expanded consciousness. Garage rock promises primitive authenticity. Political music promises that somebody, somewhere, understands what is going on.

The Fall accept the instruments, rhythms and surface characteristics of these traditions while rejecting their guarantees.

Frightened transforms youthful confidence into paranoia. Life Just Bounces turns reassurance into a bureaucratic absurdity. Wrong Place, Right Time makes national history sound like a bass-driven territorial dispute. Crop-Dust discovers that even time itself cannot be relied upon to behave properly.

The covers and late-period recordings extend the argument. A train need not take you anywhere. A folk melody need not preserve anything. A spider need not possess the elementary characteristics of a spider.

This is not nihilism. It is a form of artistic freedom.

Smith understood that the world was already sufficiently strange without requiring musicians to manufacture additional profundity. The trick was to pay attention to the absurdities everyone else had learned to ignore.

A textile chemist, a seventeenth-century monarch, a malfunctioning train whistle, a vanished civilisation, a man shouting at pigeons: each possesses the potential to become the centre of a small and entirely unreasonable universe.

The Fall did not offer escape from reality. They made reality considerably more difficult to escape.

And that, rather than happiness, may be the closest thing to consolation their music ever intended to provide.
