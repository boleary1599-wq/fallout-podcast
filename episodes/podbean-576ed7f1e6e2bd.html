A Cold Glass of Something Unpleasant

Ivor Cutler, Mark E. Smith and the peculiar British art of making nonsense sound like a threat.

By Dr. C. R. Unthank, Institute of Unnecessary Disturbances

A Tomato Sandwich on a Housemaid’s Knee

Ivor Cutler understood something that most rock musicians spend their lives trying to forget: there is no particular reason why a song should behave like a song. It might consist of a few sentences, a wheezing harmonium, a peculiar image and a melody that seems to have arrived by mistake. It might end before anybody has established what it is about. It might be funny without containing a joke, or frightening without making any obvious threat. Most importantly, it need not explain itself. Explanation, after all, is what happens when somebody has failed to appreciate the original absurdity.

Cutler’s miniature compositions inhabit a world in which household objects acquire personalities, ordinary conversations become quietly sinister, and the distinction between childhood innocence and adult anxiety is permanently unstable. His performances suggest a kindly schoolteacher who has developed an alarming interest in the contents of the stationery cupboard. The voice is gentle, the arrangements modest, the subject matter frequently ridiculous. Yet the effect can be deeply disconcerting. A nonsense rhyme repeated often enough begins to resemble an incantation. A familiar domestic object becomes suspicious simply because somebody has described it with excessive precision.

There is a direct connection between this method and the work of Mark E. Smith, although their musical personalities could scarcely have been more different. Cutler approached the microphone as though apologising for disturbing the room. Smith generally sounded as though the room had disturbed him and would shortly be receiving a formal complaint. Nevertheless, both were fascinated by the instability of language, the peculiar musical possibilities of ordinary speech, and the capacity of repetition to transform apparently meaningless material into something memorable.

Cutler’s appearance on Robert Wyatt’s Rock Bottom provides a particularly useful point of contact. The album’s concluding “Little Red Robin Hood Hit the Road” moves through a dreamlike landscape in which conventional musical narrative gradually dissolves. Cutler’s recitation arrives like a visitor from a different programme, introducing an entirely separate imaginative universe without bothering to explain the connection. The effect is neither parody nor conventional surrealism. It is the sound of an artist insisting upon his own peculiar logic in the middle of somebody else’s carefully constructed work.

Smith would spend much of his career doing something remarkably similar.

The Detective Instinct Always Pays Off in Retrospect

The Fall’s early music frequently sounds like an attempt to discover how little information a song can provide before it ceases to be recognisable. A bass line might repeat for several minutes, the drums might offer only the most rudimentary rhythmic assistance, and Smith might contribute a collection of observations whose relationship to one another remains deliberately obscure. The listener is given just enough material to establish a pattern, then invited to become increasingly suspicious of it.

“Detective Instinct”, from Room to Live, is an especially effective example. Its sparse arrangement creates an atmosphere of cavernous uncertainty, with percussion so understated that the rhythm initially appears almost absent. The recording possesses something of the oppressive spaciousness of dub, although the usual pleasures of bass-heavy immersion have been replaced by a more anxious form of listening. There is a sense of something happening at the far end of a badly lit corridor. The instruments do not so much accompany Smith as provide occasional evidence that other people are present.

The song’s title offers a misleading promise of rational investigation. Detective fiction traditionally proceeds through the accumulation of clues, the correction of mistaken assumptions and the eventual restoration of order. Smith appears considerably more interested in the mistaken assumptions. His observations suggest a narrator whose confidence in his powers of deduction far exceeds the available evidence. The effect resembles a provincial detective who has spent several hours interrogating a coat stand and is now preparing to make an arrest.

This is where the relationship with Cutler becomes particularly interesting. Both artists employ the language of everyday certainty while constructing situations in which certainty is impossible. Cutler’s domestic absurdities are presented with the calm assurance of somebody reading the shipping forecast. Smith’s paranoid observations arrive with the authority of a man who has uncovered an international conspiracy involving the local bus service. Neither offers the listener much assistance in distinguishing genuine significance from nonsense.

“What You Need”, from This Nation’s Saving Grace, demonstrates another possibility. Its rolling bass line, steady pulse and carefully placed instrumental details belong to a more expansive musical vocabulary. The rhythm has something of Can’s hypnotic propulsion, particularly the group’s ability to make minimal changes within a repetitive structure feel like major events. Rather than building towards a conventional chorus, the arrangement establishes a musical environment in which small alterations acquire unusual importance.

The difference between these recordings is not simply one of sophistication. “What You Need” is more polished, but “Detective Instinct” uses its apparent deficiencies as expressive resources. One offers the pleasure of being drawn into a groove; the other makes the listener increasingly aware of the empty spaces surrounding it. Both depend upon repetition, but they employ it to opposite ends. One produces immersion. The other produces suspicion.

Everything Is Better Through a Vocoder, Except When It Isn’t

By the beginning of the nineties, The Fall had acquired a reputation for incorporating contemporary musical developments without displaying much concern for the conventions attached to them. Their engagement with dance music was neither a straightforward conversion nor a cynical attempt to reach a new audience. The group’s rhythmic obsessions had always placed them closer to certain strands of electronic and experimental music than their post-punk reputation suggested. The difference was that new technology made those connections more conspicuous.

“Cloud of Black”, associated with the Shift-Work period and later collected on The Twenty-Seven Points, occupies an uneasy position within this development. Its electronic textures and sluggish rhythmic movement suggest the early-nineties crossover between independent rock and dance culture, but the result is strangely inert. The atmosphere is neither fully hypnotic nor sufficiently abrasive to become compelling through resistance. It sounds like a recording that has been instructed to relax and has interpreted the instruction as permission to stop making an effort.

There is something fascinating about The Fall’s capacity to make fashionable musical developments sound faintly disreputable. Where other groups embraced dance production as a means of achieving greater rhythmic precision, Smith’s records could make electronic music sound as though it had been recovered from a damaged cassette in a municipal car park. The effect was occasionally magnificent. At other times, the machinery simply exposed the weakness of the material.

The Funeral Mix offers a more explicit encounter with remix culture. The involvement of Coldcut promises the pleasures of studio manipulation, rhythmic reconstruction and the transformation of familiar material into something unexpected. A vocoder applied to Smith’s voice ought, in theory, to produce a glorious collision between technological artificiality and one of rock music’s least cooperative vocal personalities.

The difficulty is that theoretical possibilities do not necessarily translate into enjoyable records. A vocoder can transform the human voice into a strange electronic instrument, but the transformation itself is not enough. The underlying musical material still requires direction, tension or some recognisable purpose. Without those qualities, technological novelty quickly becomes a substitute for invention.

This is an important qualification to the idea that The Fall could make anything interesting. Smith’s voice was an extraordinary resource, but it was not a magical ingredient capable of improving every arrangement into which it was inserted. The group’s finest experiments depended upon the friction between incompatible musical elements. When that friction disappeared, the result could be surprisingly ordinary.

The same problem applies to Cutler’s apparent simplicity. His work was not compelling merely because it was peculiar. It depended upon timing, vocal inflection, restraint and an exceptionally precise understanding of how much information to withhold. Nonsense is easy. Convincing nonsense is considerably harder.

Five Years in a P.C. Camp

“The Joke (Five Years in a P.C. Camp)” reveals another aspect of Smith’s peculiar relationship with musical structure. Its aggressive garage-rock arrangement recalls the primitive energy of the Sonics, the Stooges and other groups for whom distortion and rhythmic insistence were not unfortunate consequences of inadequate equipment but essential features of the music.

The Fall had been drawing upon this vocabulary since their earliest recordings, yet the song demonstrates how much could still be accomplished with familiar materials. A blunt guitar figure, a driving rhythm and an obstinately repeated vocal phrase become the foundations of something that is simultaneously catchy and unpleasant. Smith’s talent for extracting a hook from almost nothing is especially apparent. A few words, delivered with sufficient conviction and repeated at the right moment, acquire a musical identity that more elaborate melodies might struggle to achieve.

The effect recalls the relationship between punk rock and advertising. Both rely upon short phrases, immediate recognition and the relentless repetition of simple messages. Smith’s genius was to employ those techniques while making the message itself unstable. His slogans often resemble fragments from a political campaign whose organisers have forgotten what they were supposed to be campaigning for.

This ambiguity becomes particularly uncomfortable when the subject matter approaches politics. Smith’s provocations could be genuinely sharp, but they could also collapse into the familiar resentments of a man shouting at the television. His refusal to adopt a respectable political position did not automatically make every utterance insightful. Sometimes the discomfort was artistically productive. Sometimes it was simply discomfort.

The musical achievement of “The Joke” lies in its ability to make that uncertainty part of the recording’s character. The rhythm is direct, the performance committed, and the repeated vocal phrase becomes increasingly difficult to separate from the instrumental arrangement. What begins as a statement becomes a noise, then a hook, then something approaching a compulsive verbal tic.

“DIY Meat”, from The Light User Syndrome, approaches rock music from a more disciplined direction. Its tight guitar playing, busy drumming and aggressive rhythmic movement suggest a band operating with considerable technical confidence. There is a rockabilly-derived directness to the arrangement, but the performance is sufficiently forceful to prevent the familiar ingredients from becoming nostalgic.

The contrast illustrates one of the group’s most persistent contradictions. The Fall could produce exciting music through apparent incompetence, but they could also benefit enormously from musicians who knew exactly what they were doing. Smith’s anti-professional reputation sometimes obscures the extraordinary instrumental skill of the people who worked alongside him. The band’s finest recordings were rarely the achievements of one man imposing disorder upon an otherwise passive group. They were the results of musicians negotiating, resisting and occasionally exploiting his peculiar demands.

Clasp Hands, Then Wash Them

The late-period Fall present a different problem. By the middle of the 2000s, Smith had established a musical language so distinctive that almost any combination of repetitive guitar, driving percussion and abrasive vocals could be recognised as his work. The danger was that recognisability might become a substitute for invention.

“Clasp Hands”, from Fall Heads Roll, occupies this uncertain territory. Its abrupt shifts and borrowed garage-rock vocabulary suggest a band capable of generating excitement almost automatically. Yet the recording also raises the possibility that some of its effects have become habitual. The group can change direction suddenly, but the surprise is diminished when sudden changes have become part of the expected routine.

“986 Generator”, associated with Your Future Our Clutter, offers a more rewarding example of late-period persistence. Its blues-derived stomp, abrasive guitar textures and percussive interruptions create a recording that seems to discover additional energy as it proceeds. Rather than relying entirely upon Smith’s established mannerisms, the arrangement develops its own momentum. The instruments generate a physical excitement that allows the voice to function as one disruptive element among several.

This distinction matters because Smith’s later performances are too easily discussed in terms of deterioration. His changing voice and increasingly unpredictable delivery certainly altered the group’s sound, but they did not eliminate the possibility of musical invention. The question was whether the musicians could construct arrangements that made productive use of those qualities rather than merely accommodating them.

In this respect, The Fall’s final decades resemble the work of an artist who has developed an unmistakable handwriting and must continually resist the temptation to produce signatures instead of sentences. The familiar elements remain available, but their presence no longer guarantees anything. A repeated riff might become hypnotic or merely repetitive. A strange vocal intervention might transform the recording or simply interrupt it. The difference depends upon the particular musical circumstances.

The comparison with Cutler returns us to the fundamental question of artistic personality. Both performers possessed voices that could make almost any material immediately recognisable. Yet their greatest achievements depended upon far more than recognition. Cutler’s carefully measured absurdities required an exact understanding of silence and timing. Smith’s apparently uncontrolled performances depended upon the musicians, arrangements and recording circumstances surrounding him.

There is a temptation to regard both men as lovable eccentrics, national treasures whose peculiarities can be safely celebrated from a comfortable distance. This is particularly misleading in Smith’s case. His music was often hostile, abrasive and genuinely difficult, while his public personality could be thoroughly unpleasant. To reduce him to a collection of amusing sayings is to remove much of what made the records unsettling.

Cutler’s gentle voice could conceal disturbing images. Smith’s aggressive delivery could make ridiculous statements sound threatening. Both understood that the boundary between comedy and anxiety is extraordinarily thin, and that repetition can wear away the distinction altogether.

A tomato sandwich sitting on a housemaid’s knee is a funny image. Repeat it often enough, with sufficient seriousness, and it begins to suggest a world operating according to rules that nobody has explained. The same transformation occurs throughout The Fall’s catalogue. A phrase becomes a rhythm. A rhythm becomes an obsession. An obsession becomes a song.

And somewhere in the process, the ridiculous begins to sound entirely reasonable.
