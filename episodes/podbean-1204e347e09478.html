The Fall and the Lost Art of Finding Things

By C.R. Eep

Punk rock, pirate radio, dub mutations and forgotten B-sides: how The Fall made the margins of popular music feel more important than its centre.

There was a time when discovering The Fall required a certain amount of physical effort. You had to go somewhere. A record shop, preferably one with an unhelpful proprietor and an arrangement of stock that suggested several generations of archaeological disturbance. You had to look through boxes, examine sleeves, wonder whether an unfamiliar title represented a lost masterpiece or another compilation of material you already owned three times. You might spend an entire Saturday finding nothing.

Then, occasionally, you would find something extraordinary.

Episode Eight begins with guest Leon remembering precisely this experience. Having discovered The Fall through John Peel, he describes the peculiar excitement of buying records without knowing quite what they contained. An unfamiliar copy of Grotesque might turn up in a shop, and there was no immediate way to establish where it fitted into the group's history. The music existed as a collection of objects, rumours and possibilities rather than an immediately accessible catalogue.

This was not simply an inconvenience imposed by primitive technology. It changed the way people listened.

A record that had taken three months to find was unlikely to be dismissed after thirty seconds. You had invested money, time and a considerable amount of emotional energy in its acquisition. You were going to listen to the bloody thing whether you liked it or not.

The Fall were particularly well suited to this peculiar economy of attention. Their catalogue was full of unlikely B-sides, Peel Session versions, abandoned directions and recordings that seemed determined to conceal their virtues. They were a band whose least promising material could suddenly reveal itself as essential, while a supposedly important release might turn out to be a considerable waste of everyone's time.

The four pairings in Episode Eight offer a miniature history of this disorderly abundance. They move from the group's earliest punk recordings through their encounters with dub, the uneasy professionalism of the 1990s and the damaged machinery of their later years.

What connects them is not a consistent musical style.

It is the sense that The Fall's real history was always happening somewhere slightly to the side of the official one.

1. Punk Rock Was Already Too Small

The opening of Crap Rap 2 / Like to Blow is one of the most wonderfully unpromising declarations in British popular music.

“We are The Fall.”

It ought to be a statement of arrival, the sort of introduction delivered by a group convinced that history is waiting to receive them. Instead, Mark E. Smith sounds as though he is announcing an administrative inconvenience. The band has turned up. There will be some music. Everybody will simply have to put up with it.

Then the guitars begin.

The recording belongs unmistakably to the punk era, but its relationship with punk is already faintly antagonistic. The guitar sound is abrasive, the drums attack the beat with considerable enthusiasm, and the whole performance seems to be travelling at a speed slightly beyond its ability to remain properly assembled.

Yet Smith's delivery refuses the usual theatrical conventions of punk rebellion. He doesn't sound like someone demanding that society be overturned. He sounds like someone who has already decided society is ridiculous and is now explaining the situation to a roomful of people who have failed to notice.

This distinction matters.

Punk's great rhetorical gesture was the declaration that things could be different. Smith's was the suspicion that things were already much stranger than anybody had admitted.

The music of the earliest Fall can sound relatively conventional beside the records that followed. The group had not yet fully developed its obsession with repetition, nor discovered how to turn a limited instrumental vocabulary into a self-contained musical language. But even here, the essential contradiction is apparent: an apparently primitive rock band fronted by someone whose relationship with rock music was fundamentally suspicious.

Against it, Mess of My suggests how quickly the group began moving away from the assumptions of its original musical environment.

The Peel Session context is important. A BBC session offered musicians a peculiar combination of opportunity and restriction: professional recording facilities, limited time and the knowledge that the resulting performance would reach an audience already predisposed towards the unusual.

For The Fall, these sessions became an alternative recording history. They were neither simply live performances nor conventional studio productions. They captured songs at particular moments in their development, sometimes before their eventual album versions had established what those songs were supposed to be.

This gave the group permission to be provisional.

The distinction between the two recordings is therefore larger than a question of which song is better. One represents the group announcing its existence within an established musical movement. The other belongs to a process through which The Fall increasingly escaped the need to belong to any movement at all.

The most interesting thing about early Fall punk is not how aggressively it embraces punk's limitations.

It is how quickly the group begins looking for ways out.

2. Lee Perry, Kimble and the Dub That Got Away

The Fall's relationship with reggae and dub is one of the most fascinating and insufficiently examined aspects of their music.

This is partly because their borrowings rarely announce themselves with the obvious stylistic gestures associated with white British musicians discovering Jamaican records. The Fall did not generally attempt to become a reggae band. Instead, they absorbed certain principles of reggae production and performance into a musical language that remained stubbornly their own.

The most important of these was the idea that repetition need not imply stasis.

In dub, a rhythm can remain fundamentally unchanged while the space surrounding it undergoes extraordinary transformations. Instruments disappear, echoes extend into unexpected distances, fragments of vocal material acquire new significance, and the recording studio becomes an instrument in its own right.

This offered The Fall a particularly useful way of thinking about music.

Their songs were frequently built from obstinate rhythmic patterns, but the apparent simplicity of those patterns concealed considerable possibilities. A bassline could become the organising principle of an entire performance. A guitar might contribute almost nothing in conventional harmonic terms while altering the music's texture completely.

Kimble makes the connection explicit.

The group's engagement with Lee Scratch Perry is significant not merely because Perry was an extraordinary producer, but because his work offered a radically different conception of what a recording could be.

Perry's productions often treated the studio as a place where music could be dismantled and reassembled according to a logic closer to dream, ritual or private obsession than conventional arrangement. Sound effects, fragments of speech and apparently incidental noises could become as important as melody.

There is something profoundly Smithian about this.

Both artists developed musical worlds in which ordinary objects acquired mysterious significance, and both understood that the personality of a recording might depend upon elements that respectable production techniques would normally remove.

Yet The Fall's relationship with dub was not simply one of admiration or imitation.

Their version of repetition was frequently more abrasive, more claustrophobic and less interested in the pleasures of rhythmic space. Where Perry could make a recording feel as though its boundaries were dissolving, The Fall often seemed intent upon making the walls close in.

Set against Kimble, Guest Informant demonstrates another way in which the group could construct an atmosphere from apparently limited materials.

The late-eighties Fall possessed a formidable rhythmic intelligence. Steve Hanley's bass playing had become central to the group's identity, establishing patterns that were simultaneously muscular and strangely independent of the guitars. The arrival of more polished production did not eliminate this quality. It sometimes made the underlying musical tensions more apparent.

Guest Informant belongs to a period in which the group could sound comparatively assured without surrendering its appetite for oddness.

The title itself suggests one of Smith's favourite social arrangements: a world of observers, witnesses, collaborators and unreliable authorities. Somebody knows something. Somebody is telling somebody else. Whether the information is useful, accurate or entirely invented remains uncertain.

There is a resemblance here to the strange communities that populate Perry's records, where voices appear and disappear as though participating in a conversation whose rules have never been properly established.

But the two artists arrive at their effects through different musical instincts.

Perry frequently transforms music by opening it up.

The Fall often transform it by refusing to let it escape.

Both understand that a recording can contain more than the song apparently being performed.

3. The B-Side as an Act of Sabotage

By the middle of the 1990s, The Fall had survived long enough to become something they were never supposed to be: an institution.

This was a dangerous development.

Institutions acquire expectations. Their audiences begin to recognise characteristic gestures, and those gestures gradually become obligations. The very qualities that once made a group distinctive can harden into a formula.

For a band whose artistic identity depended upon disruption, the prospect of becoming reliably Fall-like must have been particularly unpleasant.

Secession Man belongs to the uneasy period in which these contradictions became increasingly apparent.

The Light User Syndrome is an album of considerable energy and instability, a record that can sound both professionally assembled and permanently on the verge of coming apart. Its guitar-driven arrangements possess a weight that would have been difficult to imagine in the group's earliest years, yet Smith's vocal performances frequently seem determined to undermine whatever coherence the musicians have established.

The title Secession Man is wonderfully suggestive.

Secession implies withdrawal from an established order, the deliberate refusal to remain part of a larger organisation. It is a political term that becomes peculiarly appropriate when attached to Smith, a performer whose career was marked by an almost pathological resistance to stable membership, musical consensus and the ordinary compromises of collective activity.

But there is an important distinction between making music about independence and creating music that actually sounds independent.

The Fall's mid-nineties recordings sometimes struggle with this problem. Their arrangements can sound comparatively conventional even when Smith's words and delivery remain unmistakably peculiar. The familiar tension between group discipline and individual disruption is still present, but it does not always produce something new.

Against it stands Happy Holiday, a recording from the margins of the group's catalogue.

The title is almost aggressively cheerful.

In ordinary pop music, a happy holiday might promise escape, pleasure or temporary liberation from the obligations of everyday life. In the world of The Fall, such a promise immediately becomes suspicious. One begins to wonder who is organising the holiday, what it is going to cost and whether everybody involved will return safely.

This is not merely a matter of Smith's famously abrasive personality. The Fall had a remarkable ability to make apparently innocent cultural language sound faintly sinister. Titles, slogans and familiar expressions were removed from their normal surroundings and exposed as peculiar little constructions.

The B-side was an ideal place for such experiments.

Historically, the B-side occupied an ambiguous position in popular music. It was simultaneously an official release and something of secondary importance, a space where record companies and audiences might tolerate material considered unsuitable for the main attraction.

For many groups, this meant leftovers.

For The Fall, it could mean freedom.

The group's enormous catalogue of non-album material repeatedly undermines the assumption that artistic importance corresponds to commercial prominence. A song might be buried on the reverse of a single and still contain an idea more interesting than anything on the accompanying album.

This is why collecting The Fall could become such a peculiar obsession. The ordinary hierarchy of releases was unreliable. Albums, singles, Peel Sessions, compilations and B-sides all competed for attention, and the supposedly peripheral material was frequently central to understanding the group.

The Fall did not simply produce an extensive discography.

They produced a discography that argued with itself.

4. The Bad Stuff Is Part of the Good Stuff

There is a peculiar irony in the title All Leave Cancelled.

By the time of the Wise Ol' Man EP, The Fall had been recording for nearly four decades. The ordinary narrative of rock history would have suggested that Mark E. Smith ought to be approaching retirement, preferably after a series of dignified farewell performances accompanied by respectful newspaper articles.

Instead, here was a group announcing that everybody's leave had been cancelled.

The phrase sounds like an instruction issued by an especially unpleasant factory supervisor, or perhaps a minor military official whose authority has become inseparable from his personal unhappiness.

It is an almost perfect late-Fall title.

Smith had spent much of his career transforming the language of ordinary institutions into something grotesque. Schools, offices, hospitals, government departments and workplaces recur throughout his songs as environments in which petty systems of control acquire enormous psychological significance.

All Leave Cancelled suggests that even escape has become an administrative impossibility.

The later Fall were frequently described in terms of decline, and there is no reason to pretend that this description was always unfair. Smith's voice deteriorated, performances could become chaotic, and some recordings seemed to survive through sheer stubbornness rather than any obvious artistic necessity.

Yet decline is an inadequate framework for understanding what the group was doing.

The late Fall were not simply a diminished version of the early Fall. They were operating under different physical, musical and psychological conditions. Smith's increasingly damaged voice could produce effects unavailable to the younger singer. His delivery had become thicker, more fragmented and sometimes almost completely resistant to ordinary comprehension.

This could be frustrating.

It could also be extraordinary.

The Bad Stuff makes an appropriately unpromising companion.

The title almost invites the listener to dismiss the recording before it begins. It seems to acknowledge the possibility that some of the group's output might be less than essential, an admission that devoted Fall listeners have occasionally found difficult to make.

There is a longstanding temptation to defend every Fall recording as though the group's entire artistic achievement depends upon the absence of failure.

It does not.

Some Fall records are bad. Some are boring. Some sound like ideas that ought to have been developed further, while others sound like ideas that probably ought never to have been developed at all.

But the failures are important because they reveal the conditions under which the successes became possible.

Smith was not interested in producing a carefully managed sequence of respectable artistic statements. The Fall recorded, toured, changed personnel, experimented, abandoned ideas and began again. Their catalogue contains the evidence of this activity in all its untidy abundance.

The group was not a monument.

It was a process.

And processes produce waste.

The remarkable thing is how frequently The Fall managed to transform that waste into something valuable.

A fragment of a rehearsal might become a song. An unlikely cover version might expose a previously unsuspected musical connection. A B-side might reveal an aspect of the group that the accompanying album had failed to capture.

This is what makes the enormous, contradictory Fall catalogue so compelling.

It refuses to behave like the collected works of a respectable artist.

Instead, it resembles an archive of activities: attempts, accidents, arguments, successes, failures, obsessions and peculiar little triumphs.

The experience of listening to it is therefore not entirely unlike the experience Leon describes at the beginning of Episode Eight.

You go looking for something.

You don't know exactly what you are going to find.

Sometimes you discover a masterpiece. Sometimes you discover an incomprehensible piece of rubbish. Sometimes the rubbish turns out to be the masterpiece, although it may take several years to realise it.

And sometimes the record you spent months trying to find is simply not very good.

That, too, is part of the pleasure.

The modern availability of music has made The Fall easier to investigate than at any previous point in their history. Their recordings can be located, compared and placed in chronological order with a degree of precision that would have astonished the listeners who once spent their Saturdays searching through second-hand record shops.

But something has been lost in the process.

Not the music itself. That remains as awkward, contradictory and occasionally magnificent as ever.

What has diminished is the uncertainty surrounding its discovery.

The Fall were a group for whom uncertainty was not merely an obstacle to understanding.

It was one of the principal reasons to keep listening.

And perhaps that is why the forgotten B-sides, the peculiar Peel Sessions and the recordings that nobody quite knows what to do with remain so essential.

They remind us that the most interesting music does not always arrive where we have been told to look for it.

Sometimes it is sitting in a cardboard box at the back of a shop, waiting for somebody with too much time, insufficient money and a frankly unreasonable level of curiosity.

The Fall made an entire artistic philosophy out of that unreasonable curiosity.

And the best way to listen to them may still be to approach every unfamiliar record as though it has just been discovered.
