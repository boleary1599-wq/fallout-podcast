THE VOLLMEDIAKRIEG AND THE DEATH OF THE LAST RAILWAYMAN

On Knut Hamsun, William Burroughs, the Glorious Revolution, musical sabotage, the murder of Simon Spencer and the peculiar business of making a railway station sound like the end of civilisation.

By Professor J. Temperance, Department of Applied Grotesquery

There is a persistent temptation to regard Mark E. Smith as a surrealist, principally because this relieves the listener of the obligation to understand what he is talking about. An incomprehensible lyric becomes a triumph of the unconscious; a peculiar vocal inflection becomes an avant-garde strategy; a reference to a Norwegian novelist is filed alongside a mention of a Curly Wurly as evidence of the artist's magnificent disregard for meaning.

This is convenient, flattering and frequently wrong.

Smith's imagination was not so much surrealist as catastrophically overconnected. Literature, political history, popular music, local geography, advertising, science fiction and the contents of the nearest newsagent were subjected to the same peculiar process of recombination. Nothing was too important to be trivialised, and nothing too trivial to acquire cosmic significance.

The eight musical artefacts here provide a particularly useful demonstration. They begin in the psychological wilderness of Knut Hamsun, pass through the linguistic terrorism of “Slang King”, discover an unexpectedly moving account of time in “Futures and Pasts”, examine the relationship between ballet and bad trumpet synthesisers, and eventually arrive at Victoria railway station, where the final years of The Fall appear to have been reduced to the dimensions of a particularly unpleasant public announcement.

The result is a history of modernism as told by somebody who has been banned from the library.

I. KNUT HAMSUN AND THE PSYCHOPATHOLOGY OF THE NORTHERN RAILWAY

Knut Hamsun is a useful starting point for any examination of Mark E. Smith's intellectual ancestry, not least because he represents the uncomfortable possibility that literary innovation and moral repulsiveness are entirely compatible.

Hamsun's Hunger (1890) is among the foundational documents of psychological modernism. Its narrator wanders through Kristiania, now Oslo, experiencing poverty, hunger, pride, hallucination and increasingly erratic mental states. The novel's importance lies less in what happens than in how consciousness itself becomes the subject of literature.

Smith's affinity with this kind of writing is obvious. His narrators frequently inhabit states of extreme psychological agitation without supplying the reassuring framework of conventional storytelling. Events are reported, identities shift, grievances accumulate and the relationship between objective reality and subjective distortion becomes impossible to establish.

Hamsun's later support for Nazi Germany complicates any attempt to present him as a straightforward literary hero. Ezra Pound provides an equally disagreeable example. Artistic radicalism and reactionary politics have shared accommodation more often than polite cultural histories care to acknowledge.

Smith's interest in Hamsun also produces an extraordinary anecdote in Renegade: the supposed therapeutic properties of railway travel in relation to tuberculosis. Whether the story is medically or biographically reliable is another matter. Its symbolic appeal is considerable. The train becomes a machine for rearranging consciousness.

This is an unexpectedly appropriate image for The Fall. Their music repeatedly establishes a rhythm that appears capable of continuing indefinitely, while Smith's voice wanders through the carriage making increasingly alarming observations about the passengers. Marinetti regarded technological velocity as a means of destroying the past. Smith regarded the past as something that could be made considerably more irritating by being played over a drum machine.

II. SLANG KING: WILLIAM BURROUGHS GOES TO THE TUCK SHOP

“Slang King”, from The Wonderful and Frightening World of The Fall, is among the group's most compelling demonstrations of language functioning simultaneously as communication, camouflage and assault.

Its musical architecture is deceptively straightforward. A tight, propulsive rhythm section supports guitar figures that alternate between clipped insistence and nervous embellishment. The keyboards supply an almost absurdly jaunty counterpoint, while Smith's vocal performance treats the arrangement as a platform from which to deliver a succession of increasingly unstable linguistic proclamations.

The song's rhythmic discipline is crucial. The band does not attempt to imitate the fragmentation of the lyric. Instead, it constructs a remarkably coherent musical environment in which the fragmentation can become increasingly outrageous. The music's apparent disorder often depends upon somebody playing with extraordinary precision.

“Slang King” is also a song about the possession of language. Its central figure appears to be a man whose vocabulary has been acquired from cheap journalism, fashionable discourse and second-hand cultural authority. His words are not entirely his own.

The idea connects with William S. Burroughs's conception of language as a virus, an external system capable of colonising consciousness. In The Ticket That Exploded, Burroughs's experiments with tape manipulation and cut-up prose attack the apparent naturalness of linguistic sequence. Smith's method is less systematic but no less disruptive.

The recurring phrase Voll Media Krieg suggests a total media war, although its linguistic construction is deliberately unstable. It sounds authoritative without quite behaving like ordinary German. This is the language of propaganda stripped of its administrative competence.

The reference to a cheap bottle of Brut introduces an entirely different cultural register. The grandiose language of ideological conflict is interrupted by the smell of a supermarket toiletries aisle. And then there is the Curly Wurly.

The incident involving three little girls and insufficient money to purchase a chocolate bar is one of the great moments of miniature social tragedy in The Fall's catalogue. Walter Benjamin might have located in this scene the melancholy of commodity capitalism. Roland Barthes could have constructed an elaborate semiology of the chocolate bar. Pierre Bourdieu would doubtless have examined the relationship between confectionery preference and class distinction. Smith simply makes the children put it back.

Joyce's Finnegans Wake offers another comparison. But where Joyce constructs a vast mythological system from the sediment of world languages, Smith operates on a smaller and more malicious scale. His vocabulary comes from advertisements, pubs, cheap magazines and whatever peculiar expression somebody happened to use on the bus. Joyce with a bottle of Brut and £2.50.

The composition repeatedly threatens to become a straightforward dance record before Smith reasserts the authority of incomprehensibility. It is a song about the corruption of language that succeeds by making corrupted language extraordinarily pleasurable.

III. FUTURES AND PASTS: THE METAPHYSICS OF HAVING BEEN A BIT SHIT

“Futures and Pasts”, from Live at the Witch Trials, occupies an entirely different emotional territory. The early Fall were not yet the formidable rhythmic institution they would become. Their arrangements could be sparse, brittle and awkward, with the musicians apparently discovering the possibilities of their instruments through a process of mutual irritation.

Yet “Futures and Pasts” possesses an unusual emotional directness. Its brisk, almost punk-like momentum conceals a lyric concerned with the impossibility of reconciling past experience with future expectation. The narrator appears trapped between remembered disappointment and anticipated failure, unable to inhabit the present without reference to what has already happened or might happen again.

This brings us, somewhat improbably, to Henri Bergson. His conception of durée, or lived duration, rejects the idea that consciousness experiences time as a sequence of neatly separated units. The past persists within the present, shaping perception and expectation. “Futures and Pasts” offers a rather less optimistic version of this arrangement. Memory is not a reservoir of creative possibility but an accumulation of grievances.

Samuel Beckett's Krapp's Last Tape provides a particularly suggestive parallel: a man confronted with recordings of his younger self, discovering that the past is simultaneously familiar, alien and profoundly embarrassing. The Fall's version is considerably faster and involves more guitar.

The rhythm has an urgent, almost impatient quality, while the guitar figures resist the expansive melancholy that a more conventional songwriter might have employed. There is no grand catharsis. The song simply keeps moving. The lyric is concerned with psychological entrapment, but the music is animated by forward propulsion. It is the sound of somebody attempting to outrun their own history and discovering that history has bought a faster pair of trainers.

IV. AUTO TECH PILOT: JOHN CAGE IN A LOCK-UP GARAGE

“Auto Tech Pilot”, a B-side from the Mr. Pharmacist period, is a particularly useful example of The Fall's capacity to turn apparent musical incompetence into something more intriguing.

The recording possesses an unusually spacious, almost provisional quality. Its guitar sounds are detuned, scraped and occasionally resistant to conventional pitch, while the rhythm section provides a relatively stable foundation beneath the disorder. The instruments seem physically present, their strings rattling and their amplifiers producing noises that a more fastidious engineer might have attempted to remove.

John Cage's experiments challenged the assumption that music must be organised around the composer's intention. Everyday sound, chance operations and the acoustic properties of instruments became legitimate musical materials. The Fall were not conducting Cagean experiments in any systematic sense. Their approach was considerably more practical and less philosophically decorous.

Nevertheless, Craig Scanlon's guitar playing often occupied a related territory. His contribution could involve deliberately awkward voicings, abrasive textures and sounds that seemed to exist somewhere between a conventional guitar part and an accident.

The comparison with Derek Bailey is instructive. Bailey's free improvisation rejected established harmonic and rhythmic vocabularies in favour of the guitar's immediate physical possibilities: scraping, striking, bending and producing sounds that did not necessarily resemble guitar playing. Scanlon's work was more disciplined and tied to song structure, but he shared an interest in unexpected sonic events.

There are echoes of the Velvet Underground's more abrasive recordings and of later American noise rock, particularly Sonic Youth. Yet The Fall's version remains distinctively unheroic. They simply seem to have discovered that a guitar can make an interesting noise if treated with insufficient respect. This is a perfectly legitimate aesthetic philosophy.

V. KURIOUS ORANJ: THE GLORIOUS REVOLUTION WITH SYNTHETIC TRUMPETS

“Kurious Oranj” presents one of the strangest episodes in The Fall's history: the transformation of a post-punk group into the musical accompaniment for an experimental ballet concerned, among other things, with William of Orange.

Michael Clark's I Am Curious, Orange combined contemporary dance, political history, theatrical absurdism and the music of The Fall in an enterprise that might reasonably have been expected to collapse under the weight of its own improbability. Instead, it became one of the group's most remarkable cultural achievements.

The historical background involves the Glorious Revolution of 1688, the deposition of James II and the accession of William III and Mary II. But the ballet was hardly a conventional historical pageant. The title refers to Vilgot Sjöman's I Am Curious (Yellow), the controversial Swedish film whose combination of politics, sexuality and experimental form had made it an international cause célèbre.

This creates a marvellous cultural chain: seventeenth-century constitutional conflict, 1960s Swedish cinema, 1980s British experimental dance and Mark E. Smith. The whole thing sounds like a university course assembled by a committee whose members have never met.

The song's synthetic brass figures possess an almost comically artificial quality, while the rhythm section provides a buoyant, danceable foundation. The keyboards have the unmistakable character of late-eighties technology attempting to imitate instruments that would have been considerably more expensive to hire.

The synthetic trumpet does not sound like a trumpet. It sounds like the idea of a trumpet being remembered by a computer. Jean Baudrillard might have described this as a simulacrum: a representation that no longer requires an original. The more obvious explanation is that somebody had bought a synthesiser.

Smith's delivery sounds simultaneously ceremonial and bewildered, as though he has been appointed master of ceremonies at an event whose purpose has not been adequately explained. The orange becomes an unstable symbol: fruit, colour, political dynasty, theatrical prop and possibly a giant anthropomorphic object wandering through the production.

The song demonstrates The Fall's ability to move between cultural registers without treating any of them as inherently superior. A seventeenth-century constitutional settlement can coexist with a cheap synthesiser trumpet. The Glorious Revolution has become a disco.

VI. SPENCER MUST DIE: THE DEATH OF THE PRODUCER AND THE BIRTH OF THE NONSENSE MACHINE

“Spencer Must Die”, from Levitate, belongs to the period when The Fall's recordings began to resemble archaeological sites containing the remains of several incompatible bands. The late-nineties group was undergoing considerable personnel instability, and the music reflects an increasingly fragmented approach to composition and production.

Yet “Spencer Must Die” is unexpectedly delicate. Its arrangement possesses a restrained, almost pastoral quality, with a gentle rhythmic movement and a vocal performance that seems less concerned with confrontation than with the peculiar pleasure of assembling words.

The title invites an interpretation involving producer Simon Spencer. The coincidence of the title with Spencer's later death is disturbing, but it should not be confused with evidence of prophecy or causation. Smith's titles were frequently provocative, and their relationship to actual events was rarely straightforward.

The song's apparent nonsense resembles fragments extracted from an Edward Lear poem, a damaged children's book or overheard conversations. Lewis Carroll's Jabberwocky demonstrates that invented language can possess emotional and narrative force even when individual words resist conventional interpretation. But Carroll's nonsense operates within carefully controlled grammatical structures. Smith's language is more erratic, its syntax frequently interrupted by the demands of performance.

Where Vaughan Williams might have discovered an ancient folk melody in the countryside, Smith seems to have found a damaged cassette containing somebody else's account of a countryside he has never visited. The result is strangely beautiful.

The song demonstrates that The Fall's commitment to disruption did not always require aggression. Sometimes the most effective disturbance was the introduction of an inexplicable image into an otherwise gentle musical environment. The world remains recognisable, but something has gone subtly wrong.

VII. NEW FORMATION SERMON: THE SKIFFLE APOCALYPSE

“New Formation Sermon”, from Are You Are Missing Winner, is a far less delicate proposition. Its rhythmic foundation suggests skiffle, country music and the more primitive reaches of rockabilly. The arrangement possesses a loose, almost amateurish quality, with the instruments apparently more interested in maintaining a collective atmosphere than in achieving technical precision.

Before the Beatles, the Rolling Stones and the great explosion of British rock music, skiffle provided an inexpensive means for young musicians to participate in popular culture. Lonnie Donegan's success helped establish the idea that a guitar, a washboard and sufficient enthusiasm could constitute a legitimate musical enterprise.

The Fall's relationship with this tradition was rarely acknowledged in the same terms as their connection with Krautrock or garage punk, but it was arguably just as important. Smith's music repeatedly returned to the idea of rock and roll as something that could be made without the approval of professional musicians.

“New Formation Sermon” sounds like a country-and-western performance being delivered through a damaged public-address system. Its vocal style suggests a particularly belligerent American cowboy. The lyrics appear as snapshots: images, declarations and fragments of narrative that resist coherent organisation. The title suggests religious authority, but the music is too scruffy to support anything resembling a conventional sermon. The preacher has arrived at a village hall with a borrowed amplifier and a grievance.

The comparison with “Spencer Must Die” is revealing. Both songs employ fragments and apparent nonsense, but their musical environments produce entirely different effects. One is strangely beautiful. The other is stubbornly earthbound. Nonsense is not a genre. It is a relationship between language and sound.

VIII. VICTORIA TRAIN STATION MASSACRE: THE LAST TRAIN TO PRESTON

“Victoria Train Station Massacre”, from New Facts Emerge, is a late-period Fall miniature whose title promises considerably more violence than its brief running time can reasonably accommodate. The phrase suggests a sensationalist newspaper headline, a true-crime documentary or a particularly unfortunate incident on the British railway network.

Yet the music is surprisingly accessible. Its rhythm has a glam-rock quality, recalling the heavy, stomping simplicity of T. Rex or Slade. The guitar arrangement is direct and forceful, while the overall production possesses the compact efficiency of a band that knows exactly what it is doing.

The railway station is a place of departure, arrival, waiting, surveillance, public announcements and minor bureaucratic humiliation. It is a space in which large numbers of strangers briefly share a common environment without forming anything resembling a community.

Marc Augé's concept of the non-place is relevant here. Airports, motorways and railway stations are environments defined less by historical identity than by movement, transaction and temporary occupation. The traveller becomes a passenger, a ticket holder, a customer, a number.

Yet the song's violence remains curiously abstract. The massacre is announced, but its details are elusive. The effect resembles a tabloid headline detached from the article it was intended to introduce. Guy Debord might have recognised the operation: the spectacle of catastrophe replacing the catastrophe itself. Or perhaps somebody simply missed the train.

The song's greatest achievement is its economy. It establishes a convincing rhythmic identity, introduces a disturbing image and departs before the listener has had time to decide what has happened. It is a miniature act of musical vandalism.

IX. SEGUE: THE ART OF NOT QUITE BEING A SONG

“Segue”, also from New Facts Emerge, presents the final problem: what constitutes a Fall song when the singer's presence has become almost entirely incidental?

Its brevity, fragmentary construction and unusual vocal treatment make it difficult to evaluate according to conventional songwriting criteria. Its musical material appears almost transitional, as though the group has recorded something intended to connect two more substantial pieces and then decided that the connecting material deserves an independent existence.

Brian Eno's early ambient experiments challenged the assumption that music must possess a clearly defined beginning, middle and end. The interstitial pieces scattered across experimental rock albums similarly suggest that fragments can acquire significance through placement rather than through autonomous development. But “Segue” is too restless, abrasive and obviously connected to the peculiar circumstances of The Fall's final recordings to be ambient music.

The song's title is almost aggressively literal. It announces itself as a transition. A transition between what and what? The answer is less important than the sensation of incompletion.

There is something poignant about this in the context of New Facts Emerge, the group's final studio album. The Fall had spent four decades producing music that frequently sounded unfinished, interrupted or deliberately sabotaged. At the end, even the idea of a complete song seems to have become negotiable.

One might invoke Theodor Adorno's conception of the fragment as a form capable of resisting the false completeness of conventional culture. One might equally suggest that the group had simply run out of time. Both interpretations possess a certain melancholy plausibility. “Segue” is not a grand farewell. It is the sound of a door being left ajar.

X. THE LAST WORD BELONGS TO THE TICKET INSPECTOR

The extraordinary distance between “Futures and Pasts” and “Segue” provides a useful measure of The Fall's artistic trajectory. The former belongs to a young group discovering the possibilities of repetition, psychological disturbance and amateur musical aggression. The latter emerges from a veteran ensemble whose technical abilities were considerable but whose singer seemed increasingly determined to undermine the ordinary conventions of performance.

The Fall did not become progressively more sophisticated. Nor did they simply decline from early greatness into late-period eccentricity. They moved between sophistication and primitiveness with a freedom that made both categories unreliable.

The group's greatest achievement was perhaps its ability to treat the entire cultural landscape as available material. Knut Hamsun could coexist with a Curly Wurly. William Burroughs could be translated into the language of cheap deodorant. John Cage could find an unlikely disciple in Craig Scanlon. William of Orange could become a synthetic trumpet. Edward Lear could be dragged into the wreckage of Levitate. A railway station could acquire the atmosphere of a national catastrophe.

The Fall's imagination was not surrealist in the conventional sense. It was encyclopaedic, associative and aggressively democratic. Everything was connected, but nobody had been supplied with a map.

There is something almost heroic about this refusal to recognise cultural hierarchy. The same attention could be devoted to an obscure modernist novelist and an item of confectionery, to a constitutional revolution and a cheap keyboard preset. The ordinary world was not beneath artistic consideration. It was the primary source of artistic disturbance.

The train keeps moving. The passengers change. The announcements become less comprehensible. Somewhere in the carriage, a man is shouting about a Curly Wurly. And nobody knows whether he has a ticket.
