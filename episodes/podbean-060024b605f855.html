The Empire Never Ended, but the Tape Ran Out

Faust, The Fall and the fine line between inspired disorder and a terrible idea left running.

Dr. C. R. Hexen, Department of Industrial Folklore

The German Beatles Never Arrived

Faust were once presented as a possible answer to the Beatles. It was an unfortunate sales pitch, though perhaps a magnificent practical joke. Where the Beatles had turned the recording studio into an instrument and then filled the resulting records with recognisable songs, Faust often appeared to have turned the recording studio into an instrument and then mislaid the instructions. Their music could be ferociously repetitive, abruptly pastoral, mechanically brutal or unexpectedly lovely. Sometimes all four conditions obtained before the listener had finished looking at the sleeve.

The usual shorthand, “Krautrock”, suggests a tidy national movement, a procession of serious Germans staring at synthesizers and constructing the future. Faust were far less accommodating. Their recordings took in noise, collage, drones, abrupt edits, rough-hewn rock, untrained enthusiasm and the cheerful destruction of conventional structure. They were capable of making a tune of extraordinary delicacy and then interrupting it with the musical equivalent of somebody emptying a toolbox down a flight of stairs. The point was not that they lacked discipline. It was that discipline could be applied to an entirely different set of priorities.

The Fall belong in this conversation not because Mark E. Smith attempted to reproduce German experimental rock but because his group approached repetition as a way of upsetting musical hierarchy. A guitar figure need not support a vocal melody; a bass line need not announce where the chorus begins; the singer need not behave as though the song belongs to him. The resulting records could be as hypnotic as Can, as disobedient as Faust and as stubbornly provincial as a bus timetable pinned to the wall of a closed railway station.

There is a useful difference between influence and resemblance. The Fall did not require a direct line from a particular Faust recording to a particular Fall recording for the kinship to matter. Both groups understood that amateurism could be a creative resource, that apparent mistakes could become the centre of a piece, and that a record might become more compelling the longer it refused to develop in the expected manner. Yet Faust could sound expansive and utopian even at their most chaotic. The Fall generally sounded as though utopia had been refused planning permission.

Exit This Roman Shell

“Leave the Capitol”, from the Slates period, is among the clearest demonstrations of the band's ability to turn musical economy into a complete imaginative environment. Its bass line moves with a near-motorik certainty, while the guitars establish interlocking patterns that seem both intricately designed and one minor accident away from collapse. The arrangement is lean without being empty. Small details—a rhythmic accent, a change in guitar texture, the entrance of a harmonica—register as significant events because the underlying pulse is so persuasive.

Smith's vocal inhabits the song like a hostile tour guide. The capital is not simply a city but a diseased historical organism, full of bad air, compromised institutions and spectral inhabitants. The images evoke the British tradition of urban disgust, from Blake's London to the Wyndham Lewis of BLAST, without settling into a coherent political manifesto. A line can sound like a public-health notice, a curse or an instruction to flee. Smith makes the categories difficult to distinguish.

That is why the song's demand to depart feels more urgent than an ordinary anti-London complaint. Its geographical target becomes a state of mind: the capital as a place where history has accumulated so thickly that the present can scarcely breathe. The rhythm offers an exit route, but it also traps the listener inside a repeating mechanism. This is the classic Fall contradiction. The music insists on movement while refusing to travel very far.

Against it, “Vixen” possesses a different kind of appeal. Its droning stretches recall the Velvet Underground and the long-form patience of Spacemen 3; its more conventional passages offer a recognisable alternative to the obsessive architecture of “Leave the Capitol”. Brix's presence introduces another sensibility, more open to melodic release and pop structure, though not every passage achieves the same authority. The difference is not between experimental virtue and commercial compromise. It is between a record whose elements seem inevitable and one whose charms remain somewhat detachable.

The strange achievement of “Leave the Capitol” is that its details cannot easily be removed. The bass, the clipped guitar, the voice and the diseased-city imagery operate as a single system. This is what happens when a band stops accompanying a singer and begins constructing a place for him to haunt.

The Department of Unreliable Atmospheres

The Fall's relationship with atmosphere was rarely straightforward. Plenty of groups have made ominous records by slowing down, adding echo and inviting the listener to imagine something lurking in the dark. Smith's bands could do this, but they were also liable to introduce an element that sounded borrowed from a completely different song. A threatening groove might suddenly encounter a rockabilly flourish, a synthetic horn or an arrangement that seemed to have wandered in from the rehearsal room next door.

“Chicago Now!” operates on precisely this unstable boundary. Its murky, noirish textures are attractive, and the recording has a layered density that distinguishes it from some of the more bluntly rendered material around the Extricate period. But its changes of pace threaten to puncture the atmosphere they have established. The sudden appearance of more familiar rock gestures can sound like an attempt to reassure listeners who were enjoying the discomfort perfectly well.

This is a recurring problem in experimental rock. Contrast is not automatically dramatic. A loud section after a quiet one, or a conventional riff after a long stretch of abstraction, may simply demonstrate that the musicians possess two ideas rather than one fully developed idea. The most compelling Fall recordings do not always make their contrasts comfortable. Sometimes they force the incompatible parts to remain in the same room until something interesting happens.

“Service”, from The Infotainment Scan, has its own questionable furnishings: an almost house-like piano figure, synthetic orchestral touches and an atmosphere poised somewhere between nightclub melancholy and a detective programme shown at an unreasonable hour. The production can initially sound cheap, but cheapness is not the same as failure. The arrangement's slightly disreputable textures create a setting in which Smith's words begin to operate with unexpected force.

His references to Wolverines, ordinary clothing and obscure threats suggest a story that has lost its opening pages. The music never quite establishes whether we are hearing a lament, a warning or the testimony of an unreliable witness. That uncertainty is its chief asset. Where “Chicago Now!” occasionally abandons its own atmosphere for a more familiar musical vocabulary, “Service” allows its dubious materials to become the atmosphere.

The distinction is worth defending. The Fall were never a band whose records could be divided cleanly into the properly produced and the badly produced. A technically awkward sound could be exactly the right sound. What mattered was whether the awkwardness produced tension, character and momentum, or merely got in the way.

Walking Down the Street Again

“The Crying Marshal” belongs to the period when The Fall's music could sound as though it had been assembled from the debris of several different contemporary genres. Its enormous beat and restless textures bring the late-nineties big-beat boom into collision with Smith's increasingly peculiar narrative habits. The result can resemble a Chemical Brothers record discovered in the possession of somebody who has been banned from every nightclub in Greater Manchester.

The musical scale is almost comically disproportionate to the apparent subject matter. A figure leaves town, returns, and becomes involved in a series of mundane movements that Smith treats with a degree of urgency more usually reserved for international emergencies. The contrast can be hilarious. It can also be infuriating, especially when the vocal seems less interested in developing an idea than in occupying the available space.

The suspicion that Smith might simply be describing another journey down the street is not an unreasonable one. His work often mocked the banalities of other performers while repeatedly returning to similar fragments of everyday movement. Yet the banality was sometimes the point. In Smith's universe, ordinary errands are susceptible to historical interference, supernatural intrusion and administrative catastrophe. One cannot safely walk to the shops without risking an encounter with the nineteenth century.

“Ol' Gang” is more compelling because the instrumental disorder has a sharper purpose. The piano seems determined to pursue a separate argument from the rest of the band; the rhythm maintains its insistence while the textures scrape against one another. The result approaches the productive instability of Faust. Rather than simply placing odd noises on top of a conventional song, the arrangement makes incompatibility part of its engine.

There is a temptation to treat this sort of music as an invitation to suspend all critical judgment. If a recording is chaotic, perhaps every apparent weakness can be explained as an experiment. That is a convenient argument, particularly for musicians who have forgotten to finish something. The more useful test is whether the disorder becomes memorable, whether the listener can return to it and discover relationships that were not apparent at first. “Ol' Gang” rewards that attention. Its crooked movement has an internal life that cannot be reduced to the presence of strange sounds.

Faust's finest work performs a similar trick. Collage is not merely the act of sticking incompatible fragments together. It is the creation of a context in which those fragments begin to communicate. Sometimes they do so by refusing to agree.

The Empire Never Ended, But the Riff Might

“Coach and Horses” returns to one of Smith's richest territories: historical time behaving badly. The song's imagery suggests an ordinary location becoming contaminated by another century, with Victorian clothing and improbable prohibitions intruding upon the present. The supernatural is not located in a ruined castle or a distant wilderness. It has taken up residence just outside the front door, where it can interfere with the licensing arrangements.

This is the point at which Smith's reading of weird fiction becomes more useful than the usual descriptions of him as a surrealist. The logic resembles the uncanny disturbances of Arthur Machen, in which the familiar city contains older, stranger realities that occasionally become visible. It also recalls the temporal unease of Philip K. Dick: the possibility that what appears to be the present is only one unstable version of it. The Fall's music repeatedly turns this literary anxiety into a rhythmic one. The groove continues, but the world around it is no longer reliable.

Unfortunately, an intriguing imaginative premise does not guarantee a distinguished recording. “Coach and Horses” has its pleasures, yet its musical construction can feel oddly uncommitted. A serviceable riff may provide a framework for Smith's observations without generating much excitement of its own. This is the difficulty with a group whose vocabulary is so recognisable. A few familiar ingredients can produce something that sounds like The Fall without necessarily achieving very much.

“Assume”, by contrast, is an exercise in making apparently ridiculous language work through sheer rhythmic persistence. Its guitar figure is not beyond criticism: it can sound like the sort of provisional riff that a band intends to replace once the proper idea arrives. But the percussion is forceful, the bass has physical weight, and Smith's verbal games transform the recording into something stranger than its initial materials promise.

The repeated manipulation of the word “assume” is the kind of schoolboy linguistic trick that ought to exhaust itself almost immediately. Smith worries it until it becomes a chant, a piece of mangled philosophy and an accusation. The record's roughness is part of its appeal, though it is not exempt from the charge of being unfinished. The important question is whether the roughness generates energy. Here, at least, the rhythm section gives the words something substantial to collide with.

This is where The Fall's kinship with Faust becomes most persuasive. Neither band was consistently great because it rejected professionalism. Their finest recordings demonstrate a more difficult proposition: that music can remain alive to uncertainty even when its constituent parts are painfully simple. Two drummers, a battered piano, an obstinate guitar or a voice pursuing an absurd phrase may be sufficient. The arrangement must still discover why those things belong together.

It is easy to romanticise the sound of people doing things wrong. It is harder to explain why one wrong note changes the world and another merely sounds wrong. Faust and The Fall spent decades investigating the distinction, sometimes with extraordinary results and sometimes with the air of men attempting to repair a washing machine using a bicycle.

At their best, they leave the listener with a peculiar sense of liberation. The song need not develop, the singer need not sing, the instruments need not agree and the lyrics need not provide a solution. Yet something must happen. A groove becomes a place. A phrase becomes a threat. An ordinary street opens onto another century. The machinery continues to turn, and for a few minutes its defects seem like the only sensible way it could possibly work.
