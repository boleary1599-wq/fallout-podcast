The Empire Never Ended, but the Tape Ran Out

Faust, The Fall and the uncomfortable distinction between inspired disorder and music that simply hasn't worked.

By Dr. C. R. Hexen, Department of Industrial Folklore

The German Beatles and Other Administrative Errors

The attempt to market Faust as a German answer to the Beatles remains one of the more entertaining misunderstandings in the history of popular music. The Beatles had transformed the recording studio into an instrument, certainly, but they had also retained a sentimental attachment to melodies, choruses and the general proposition that a record should contain something resembling a song. Faust regarded these things as optional. A recording might consist of two drummers, an untrained musician, a sustained drone, an abrupt edit and somebody doing something unpleasant to a piece of electrical equipment. It might also contain a melody of astonishing beauty. There was no particular obligation to announce which version of the group had turned up.

This unpredictability distinguished Faust even from their German contemporaries. Neu! could establish a rhythmic trajectory and pursue it with almost supernatural conviction. Can could transform collective improvisation into an extraordinarily disciplined form of hypnotic music. Faust were less interested in establishing a recognisable method. Their records seemed to regard the very idea of consistency as a suspiciously bourgeois arrangement. A gorgeous pastoral passage might be followed by an industrial accident. A rhythmic experiment might end before it had properly begun. The next track could sound like somebody had discovered the Beach Boys while dismantling a refrigerator.

The connection with The Fall is therefore more interesting than the familiar business of identifying a German influence. Mark E. Smith's group shared Faust's refusal to accept that musical competence, formal coherence and artistic achievement were necessarily the same thing. But they also shared something less frequently acknowledged: the ability to be absolutely dreadful when the experiment failed.

The Fall's catalogue is not a sequence of triumphant assaults upon conventional musical taste. It is an enormous, uneven body of work in which some recordings achieve greatness through apparently unsuitable methods, while others demonstrate that unsuitable methods can produce unsuitable records. The distinction matters. Without it, the group's entire history becomes an exercise in explaining why every mistake was secretly brilliant.

There is a considerable difference between creating music that sounds as though it might fall apart and creating music that has actually fallen apart.

The Machine That Refuses to Go Anywhere

Repetition is the central technology of this music. Not the comforting repetition of a familiar chorus, but the obstinate continuation of a musical idea long after ordinary songwriting would have demanded something else. The riff becomes a machine. The machine begins to acquire a personality. Eventually the listener can no longer decide whether it is moving forward or simply making an impressive amount of noise while remaining in the same place.

“Leave the Capitol” is an almost perfect demonstration of this principle. Its bass line establishes a propulsion that feels simultaneously mechanical and physical. The guitars operate in interlocking patterns rather than conventional chordal accompaniment, introducing small variations whose significance grows with repeated listening. There are moments of rhythmic and instrumental detail that would barely register in a more elaborately arranged song. Here they seem enormous.

The music has the disciplined forward motion associated with Neu! and the hypnotic repetition of Can, but its character remains unmistakably The Fall. There is something cramped and argumentative about the arrangement. It moves like a vehicle whose occupants are refusing to agree upon the destination.

This is what makes the apparently simple distinction between a good riff and a bad riff so difficult. “Assume” is built around material that can sound almost provisional, the sort of guitar figure a band might play while waiting for somebody to arrive at rehearsal. Yet the rhythm section supplies enough weight and insistence to make the recording physically convincing. The drums bash away, the bass gives the track a stubborn centre, and Smith worries at a verbal idea until its ridiculousness begins to acquire musical force.

Elsewhere, the same approach produces diminishing returns. “Coach and Horses” has an intriguing imaginative premise, but its musical framework can feel oddly inconsequential. The riff does its job without ever establishing why it deserves to continue. Repetition alone cannot create hypnosis. It can just as easily create boredom.

The distinction is not a matter of technical sophistication. The apparently ramshackle “Ol' Gang” generates extraordinary tension because its parts seem to resist one another. The piano pursues a line that refuses to settle comfortably against the other instruments. The rhythm presses forward regardless. The resulting discordance is not merely decoration; it becomes the engine of the performance.

That is where Faust's example is most useful. Their collage methods did not succeed simply because incompatible sounds were placed together. They succeeded when the incompatibility itself generated an unexpected musical relationship. A piece could be chaotic without being arbitrary.

The Fall's finest recordings operate according to the same difficult logic. The musicians need not agree. But their disagreement must be worth hearing.

The Empire Never Ended

Smith's imagination repeatedly returns to the possibility that history has not actually finished happening. The past is not safely contained in museums, schoolbooks or respectable historical dramas. It remains active, interfering with ordinary streets, buildings and conversations. The present is an unstable arrangement that might be withdrawn without notice.

“Leave the Capitol” turns London into a diseased historical organism. Its imagery of Victorian corruption, polluted atmosphere and oppressive institutions suggests a city whose accumulated past has become physically toxic. The demand to leave is more than a complaint about the capital. It is an attempt to escape a system of historical contamination.

This places Smith within a tradition of British urban unease extending from Blake to Wyndham Lewis and Arthur Machen. In these writers, the familiar city can become threatening through a change of perspective. A street corner may contain an older reality. An apparently ordinary building may conceal an entirely different order of existence. The metropolis is not simply a collection of places but a repository of unfinished disturbances.

The same imaginative mechanism appears in the time-displacement fantasies of “Coach and Horses”. Victorian dress and nineteenth-century conditions intrude upon a recognisable everyday environment. The absurdity lies partly in the casualness of the intrusion. There is no grand science-fiction apparatus, no elaborate explanation of temporal mechanics. The world has simply gone wrong, and somebody must now negotiate the inconvenience.

Philip K. Dick provides another useful parallel. His famous anxiety that the empire never ended was not merely a historical proposition but a suspicion that the apparent present concealed an alternative structure of reality. Smith's writing often produces a similar sensation through much smaller means. A phrase, an image or an ordinary encounter suggests that the official version of events cannot be trusted.

The extraordinary thing is how frequently this metaphysical unease is attached to trivial activity. Someone leaves town. Someone returns. Someone walks down a street. Someone discovers that the circumstances of an ordinary journey are not what they appeared to be.

“The Crying Marshal” turns such mundane movement into a grotesquely inflated musical event. Its big-beat production gives an almost absurd degree of importance to a narrative that seems determined to resist dramatic significance. The enormous rhythmic machinery suggests a major revelation. The words offer a series of movements and observations that may or may not amount to anything.

This is both the joke and the risk. Smith's fascination with ordinary activity can transform banality into something uncanny, but it can also become merely banal. His impatience with other musicians describing everyday journeys sits rather awkwardly beside his own repeated accounts of people walking around.

Yet that contradiction is essential to the music. The Fall are forever promising access to some hidden order while supplying evidence that the hidden order may consist largely of somebody having a particularly irritating afternoon.

Cheap Furniture in the House of Modernism

There is a tendency to discuss experimental music as though it must sound expensive, serious and intellectually respectable. Faust's reputation can encourage this misunderstanding. The combination of German modernism, tape manipulation and avant-garde techniques suggests an austere cultural enterprise, a world of laboratories and manifestos.

In practice, the music is frequently funny, scruffy and absurd. Its experiments often possess the character of practical jokes undertaken by people who have become genuinely fascinated by the results.

The Fall brought this sensibility into collision with the cheaper materials of British popular culture. Garage rock, music-hall grotesquerie, second-hand electronic keyboards, rockabilly, pub-band rhythms and television soundtracks could all enter the same musical environment. Smith's group did not always elevate these materials into high art. Sometimes it dragged high art down to their level, which was considerably more entertaining.

“Service” is an especially revealing example. Its synthetic orchestral touches and peculiar piano arrangement initially suggest something hopelessly unfashionable, as though an unsuccessful television detective had commissioned his own theme tune. Yet the recording gradually acquires a strange emotional authority. The artificial textures establish a nocturnal atmosphere in which Smith's observations become increasingly unsettling.

The result demonstrates that musical cheapness can possess expressive value. A synthesizer does not need to reproduce a convincing brass section if the unconvincing imitation creates a more interesting atmosphere. The important question is whether the sound belongs to the imaginative world of the recording.

“Chicago Now!” raises the opposite problem. Its dense, noirish textures are compelling, but the introduction of more familiar rock gestures can puncture the atmosphere rather than intensify it. The contrast feels insufficiently integrated. A record can contain two individually attractive ideas without discovering a convincing relationship between them.

This is why the familiar celebration of The Fall's incompetence is inadequate. Some of their most successful recordings depend upon musicians of exceptional precision and inventiveness. The disciplined guitar interplay of the Slates period, the rhythmic intelligence of the group's finest line-ups and the ability to sustain a difficult arrangement all demonstrate forms of musicianship that are too easily overlooked.

Smith's apparent indifference to conventional professionalism should not obscure the work required to make his records function. There is a considerable difference between an artist rejecting virtuosity as an aesthetic value and a band lacking the ability to play.

The same distinction applies to humour. The most convincing musical absurdities depend upon timing, restraint and an understanding of exactly how long an idea can survive before it becomes exhausting. Les Dawson's deliberately wrong piano notes worked because he understood perfectly well where the right notes belonged. Frank Sidebottom's apparently primitive performances operated within a carefully constructed comic universe.

The Fall could occasionally achieve something comparable: music that sounded catastrophically wrong while displaying a remarkably exact understanding of its own wrongness.

They could also produce records that might genuinely have benefited from Les Dawson taking over the piano.

A Walk to the Shops, Followed by Damnation

By the late nineties, Smith's relationship with contemporary musical fashion had become particularly strange. The enormous beats and studio manipulations associated with dance culture offered new possibilities for a group already obsessed with repetition. But the resulting music rarely embraced the optimistic communal pleasures of the dance floor.

Instead, the technology often seemed to magnify the most antisocial elements of Smith's personality.

“The Crying Marshal” can resemble a big-beat anthem whose singer has misunderstood the purpose of an anthem. Its pounding arrangement suggests celebration or release, while the vocal remains preoccupied with peculiar grievances and inconsequential movements. The disparity is genuinely funny. It is also musically unstable.

“Ol' Gang” approaches a similar period of experimentation from another direction. Its abrasive instrumental relationships and stubborn momentum create a far more convincing sense of danger. The music does not simply provide an energetic background for Smith's performance. It threatens to overwhelm him.

That possibility is crucial. One of The Fall's great achievements was to create situations in which Smith was not entirely in control of the recording, despite his overwhelming personality. The instruments could challenge him, interrupt him or continue with magnificent indifference to whatever he happened to be saying.

In less successful recordings, the opposite occurs. The band supplies a competent framework while Smith contributes familiar vocal gestures. Everything sounds recognisably Fall-like, but the relationship between the elements has ceased to be interesting.

This is the occupational hazard of developing an unmistakable musical identity. Once the vocabulary becomes familiar, the artist can begin reproducing the signs of invention without necessarily inventing anything.

Faust avoided this problem partly by making instability itself a central principle. Their records could change direction so dramatically that the listener was denied the comfort of knowing what constituted a typical Faust performance. The Fall possessed a more recognisable sound, but their finest music maintained a comparable sense that the arrangement might unexpectedly escape its own rules.

That is the real value of disorder. It prevents the music from becoming a demonstration of a method already perfected.

The Sound of Something Nearly Working

The most revealing question to ask of The Fall is not whether a recording is strange, abrasive, primitive or technically imperfect. It is whether those qualities create something that could not have existed otherwise.

“Leave the Capitol” achieves a remarkable unity through apparently limited means. “Service” transforms questionable production choices into an unsettling atmosphere. “Ol' Gang” makes instrumental disagreement a source of energy. “Assume” discovers rhythmic force in a riff and a verbal conceit that might have been dismissed as hopelessly slight.

None of these achievements proves that every rough recording is a masterpiece. They prove almost the opposite: that roughness is a material requiring imagination, judgment and sometimes considerable discipline.

Faust's experiments suggest a world in which music might be constructed from almost anything. The Fall's catalogue demonstrates what happens when that possibility is pursued for decades by a succession of musicians under the direction of an exceptionally difficult singer. Some combinations are inspired. Some are ridiculous. Some are both. Others are simply not very good.

The failure is part of the history, but it is not automatically part of the achievement.

There is something profoundly liberating about a record that makes the listener reconsider what constitutes a song. A bass line can become an entire landscape. An unsuitable piano part can transform a groove into a nightmare. A cheap keyboard can evoke a world more convincingly than an expensive orchestra. A phrase repeated beyond all reasonable limits can cease to function as language and become a rhythmic object.

But these transformations do not happen by magic. They happen when musicians discover a relationship between sounds that initially appear to have no business occupying the same recording.

The Fall's greatest music does not merely celebrate things going wrong. It reveals how wrongness can become a form of composition.

And somewhere between the relentless bass line, the uncooperative piano, the collapsing historical timeline and the man who has apparently gone for another walk down the street, the machine begins to work.

Not properly, of course.

That would spoil everything.
