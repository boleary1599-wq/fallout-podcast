THE SIXTH CIRCLE HAS A TWO-DRUMMER POLICY

The Stooges, Nietzsche, Victorian children, failed rockabilly and the difficult business of being a gang.

By Professor C. R. Scanlon, Institute of Hostile Aesthetics

The art of making a room dangerous

The Stooges did not invent repetition, volume, theatrical aggression or the notion that a singer might become a public-health emergency. What they did was arrange these ingredients so that rock music briefly ceased to resemble entertainment and began to resemble a disturbance in the natural order. Iggy Pop was not merely singing over a band; he was changing the temperature of the room. In the more frightening performances, the difference between stage and audience appeared negotiable. The music was both the mechanism and the excuse.

The MC5, emerging from the same Detroit ecology, proposed a different sort of insurrection. Their revolutionary rhetoric was explicit, their virtuosity often flamboyant. The Stooges could be political by sounding as if the institutions that made politics possible had already collapsed. That is one reason The Fall could inherit so much from them while sounding almost nothing like them. Mark E. Smith did not require a shirtless Dionysian exhibitionist. He required a group that could sustain the disturbance while he wandered around it, describing the peculiar things he claimed to have seen.

Nietzsche's distinction in *The Birth of Tragedy* between Apollonian form and Dionysian intoxication provides a tempting shorthand. But the best Fall records do not simply choose frenzy over order. They need the order: the immovable bass figure, the unsmiling drum pattern, the structure sufficiently sturdy to make the sabotage audible. The musicians are not attendants at Smith's private séance. They are the people who have built the room in which the séance can go wrong.

This matters especially when the band plays a riff for so long that it ought to become ridiculous. “Neighbourhood of Infinity” proceeds with a grim, economical certainty, its guitar abrasion and paired percussion giving the impression of several industrial processes being carried out simultaneously in an insufficiently ventilated factory. The sound is not quite the Stooges' primitive assault, nor Can's elastic trance, nor the Velvet Underground's drugged insistence. It is an argument conducted by machinery. Its brutality depends on everybody knowing what they are doing.

A Victorian child enters the factory

The Fall's imagination was often described as surreal, but that word can conceal the precision of its source materials. Smith liked objects and institutions that had already acquired a little historical grime: municipal offices, pubs, northern streets, cheap horror, educational relics, archaic language. The “Spoilt Victorian Child” is an especially useful specimen. Victorian childhood carries the contradictory baggage of sentimentality and punishment, lace collars and industrial labour, the nursery and the workhouse. A spoiled child ought to be indulged; a Victorian child ought to be disciplined. The phrase is an argument before the music starts.

The recording's slightly destabilising rhythmic movement gives this imaginary creature an unsteady gait. The riff is memorable without becoming comfortable. It is the kind of music that seems to have been taught to dance by a person with a serious grievance against dancing. Where “Neighbourhood of Infinity” uses repetition to exert pressure, “Spoilt Victorian Child” makes an awkward figure move across a room full of sharp furniture.

M. R. James understood the usefulness of antiquated things. In his ghost stories, manuscripts, engravings and archaeological curiosities become instruments through which the past makes an unreasonable demand upon the present. Smith's own antiquarianism is noisier and funnier, but the mechanism is related. The old object is not merely decorative. It has come back to interfere.

This also explains the oddly compelling atmosphere of “R.O.D.”, a piece whose lyrics seem to open onto a half-understood landscape of dusk, overheated brains and inexplicable social prohibitions. The transcript's discussion drifts towards Robert E. Howard and Lovecraft, an illuminating pairing. Lovecraft often constructs dread through the immense and unknowable; Howard tends to make it physical, sweaty, urgent, a menace with boots. The Fall sit somewhere between the two. Their cosmic horror is liable to arrive in a pub car park and demand to know who moved the van.

The cover version as controlled demolition

Few bands have been so ready to dismantle their own property. “Big New Priest” is not simply a performance of “New Puritan” under a different title; it exemplifies The Fall's refusal to regard a recorded song as a fixed object. Its thudding bass, ruthless rhythmic continuity and guitar work turn the material into something closer to a public ceremony. An appearance on Tony Wilson's television programme, with the musicians dressed for an entirely different sort of show, sharpens the contradiction. They look capable of providing light entertainment at a provincial function while generating music that would have the licensing committee evacuated.

There is a principle here more radical than the familiar rock injunction to play loud. The Fall could make their own compositions behave like covers: things to be recovered, reconfigured, misremembered, pushed into the wrong decade. A song was a set of usable materials rather than an item in a museum. Walter Benjamin's essay on mechanical reproduction is concerned with the changing status of the artwork under technological replication. The Fall propose a less orderly problem: what if the artwork itself refuses to remain identical from one encounter to the next?

The technique resembles a theatrical company reviving a play and discovering that half the cast have rewritten their parts, the stage has been moved to another town and the director has decided the second act should be played twice as fast. Yet the essential thing survives. Sometimes it becomes stronger.

The opposite danger appears in “White Line Fever”, where rockabilly's familiar signifiers can sound too cleanly assembled for the menace promised by the title. A loping rhythm and backing vocals may evoke a genre without reproducing the instability that once made it dangerous. The comparison with Memphis garage revivalists such as 68 Comeback is useful: scuffed surfaces and unhinged performances are not automatically more authentic, but polish can remove the very friction a song needs. An insult song performed as respectable wedding entertainment is still an insult song; the question is whether anybody believes it.

Antidotes, cans and the horror of competence

Late-period Fall records often present an almost theological problem for rock criticism. The group could sound technologically adventurous and musically lumpen in adjacent passages, sometimes within the same recording. “(Jung Nev's) Antidotes” puts electronics and heavy guitar into uneasy proximity. The beat suggests an awareness of the Chemical Brothers and the possibilities of post-rave rock, while the guitar insists on maintaining an older and considerably less fashionable argument.

Combining those elements is not inherently experimental. It is perfectly possible to produce a sort of beige-brown musical emulsion: the rock band plays its rock parts, the electronic equipment provides electronic decoration, and nobody is made uncomfortable. The more interesting Fall recordings allow the components to disagree. The result can be exhilarating or disastrous, but at least the disagreement is audible.

“Kick the Can” offers the inverse problem. Its ridiculous deadpan vocal and eventual rockabilly movement can feel like an extended joke that has forgotten to arrange a punchline. Yet comedy has always been integral to The Fall's practice, from Smith's malicious portraits of cultural types to his delight in phrases that sound as though they were overheard in the wrong queue. The band were not a comedy act; that would have required their audience to agree about what was funny. They were capable of making humour feel like an accusation.

Theodor Adorno would probably have been appalled by much of this, which is an excellent reason to imagine him being made to review “Kick the Can” for the *New Musical Express*. His suspicion of standardised popular music meets its natural enemy in a group that repeatedly takes a standardised form and then plays it as if the instructions have been translated through several hostile languages.

The relationship between repetition and musical sophistication is especially treacherous here. A rhythm can be technically simple but psychologically intricate, just as an elaborate arrangement can communicate almost nothing. Tony Williams could dismantle a metre from within; the two-drummer Fall could achieve a comparable sense of instability by letting different accents rub against each other. The important unit is not the individual beat but the changing relationship between beats. This is why a three-chord assault may sound more adventurous than a piece full of expensive harmonic decisions. Complexity is not the same as difficulty, and difficulty is certainly not the same as interest.

The Fall understood another unfashionable truth: a deliberately awkward performance need not be an incompetent one. The distinction is audible in the bass figures that continue with mechanical confidence while the guitars seem to be attempting to escape. It is audible, too, in the contrast between a singer who appears to disregard the bar line and a band that knows exactly where it is. To mistake this for a lack of musicianship is like accusing Buster Keaton of being unable to walk because he keeps falling over.

The group that would not become a solo career

“Recovery Kit” suggests a more serious question about the later Fall. By the 2000s, Smith's position was so dominant that the group could be mistaken for a vehicle carrying a single irreplaceable passenger. Personnel changes had become part of its mythology. The temptation is to interpret the musicians as interchangeable components and the voice as the sole source of identity.

But listen to the way a relatively functional arrangement changes once Smith begins to inhabit it. His phrasing creates new stresses and implications; the music pushes back by continuing on its own terms. This is not a man delivering a monologue over anonymous accompaniment. It is an unstable collaboration. The fact that some collaborators lasted longer than others does not make their contributions incidental.

There is something touching, and faintly absurd, about the persistence of the group name. Smith could have pursued the obvious route of becoming a solo artist with a rotating backing band. Instead, The Fall remained The Fall: a gang, an argument, a workplace, a travelling institution with poor industrial relations and an improbably durable sign above the door. In a culture that increasingly treats the front person as the brand, this was a stubborn commitment to collective friction.

The Stooges offer a revealing precedent. Iggy Pop's solo career produced remarkable work, but the original group's music depends on the specific force generated by Ron and Scott Asheton, Dave Alexander and their singer together. No amount of personal magnetism can retrospectively replace that chemistry. Similarly, The Fall's identity lies partly in the distance between Smith's commands and the musicians' responses. If everyone obeyed perfectly, the records might have been much less interesting.

That is the paradox at the centre of this extraordinary, frequently exasperating catalogue. Smith cultivated the mythology of absolute authority, yet the music was most alive when it sounded as though authority might be contested. The bass line keeps going. The drums refuse to be impressed. The guitar threatens to knock down a wall. The singer announces an obscure grievance from somewhere behind the amplifiers.

Nietzsche's Dionysian impulse has not conquered the Apollonian. It has rented a room above it, failed to pay the deposit and started a second band.

The sixth circle of Hell, reserved in Dante for heretics, would be an appropriate destination for this enterprise, except that the occupants would almost certainly object to the rehearsal schedule. The Fall's real heresy was simpler: a rock group need not develop towards technical refinement, commercial maturity or a stable final form. It could keep rebuilding itself around the same antagonistic idea, sometimes brilliantly and sometimes with results that ought to be investigated by environmental health.

A professional pan-pipist would never have stood a chance.
