The Idiot Joy of Being Miserable

The Fall, Captain Beefheart, dead children, bad comedians and the peculiar genius of Mark E. Smith's music-hall apocalypse

By Professor Cuthbert J. Arsewangle

There are two Mark E. Smiths. One is the celebrated post-punk visionary, a working-class modernist whose gnomic utterances have been subjected to the sort of exegetical scrutiny ordinarily reserved for the Dead Sea Scrolls. The other is a horrible little man in a working men's club, making unpleasant observations about somebody's trousers before performing a Tommy Cooper impression and falling off a stool.

The remarkable thing about The Fall is that these are not competing interpretations. They are the same man, frequently within the same song.

Episode 14 of the F.A.L.L.O.U.T. podcast provides an unusually persuasive demonstration of this duality. Across eight recordings, the group moves from rural paranoia to theatrical satire, from the ghost of a child killed at Hiroshima to a rambling catalogue of insults delivered over what sounds suspiciously like somebody failing to operate a keyboard.

It is tempting to call this range extraordinary. It is also tempting to call it bloody ridiculous. Both descriptions are correct.

The Beefheart problem

The episode begins, appropriately, with Captain Beefheart, whose relationship to The Fall has become one of the great critical commonplaces of post-punk scholarship. To mention Smith without invoking Don Van Vliet is almost to risk having one's membership of the Rock Critics' Guild revoked.

But the comparison is frequently misunderstood.

Beefheart's music, particularly the extraordinary interlocking guitar figures of Trout Mask Replica and the later, more economical brutality of Doc at the Radar Station, was the product of elaborate construction. Its apparent disorder concealed arrangements of sometimes punishing exactitude. The musicians were not simply wandering around a studio making peculiar noises. They were performing compositions that made peculiar noises sound inevitable.

The podcast's opening discussion usefully concentrates on the instrumental relationships: the guitars working against and across one another, the rhythmic structures that initially seem incompatible, the sense of several musical arguments proceeding simultaneously.

This is where the comparison with The Fall becomes meaningful. Not in Smith's alleged eccentricity, nor his reputation for being difficult, but in the peculiar relationship between repetition and disruption.

Yet there is an important difference. Beefheart was frequently attempting to impose an extraordinarily detailed musical vision upon his musicians. Smith's methods could be considerably more arbitrary. The Fall's arrangements often seem to have emerged from an ongoing negotiation between what the musicians wanted to play and what their employer was prepared to tolerate.

Both approaches could produce masterpieces. Neither guaranteed them.

The countryside is full of bastards

Hard Life in the Country, from 1982's Room to Live, offers one of Smith's finest excursions into the English pastoral nightmare.

The countryside in conventional popular music is a place of retreat, authenticity and spiritual restoration. In Smith's imagination it is populated by suspicious neighbours, local scandals, encroaching bureaucracies and the lingering possibility that the villagers will surround your house and do something unspeakable.

The podcast detects something of The Wicker Man in its atmosphere. Quite right, too. But where Robin Hardy's film locates its horror in an isolated community's pagan traditions, Smith finds it in the everyday mechanisms of English provincial life: gossip, resentment, class hostility and the surveillance exercised by people who have absolutely nothing better to do.

This is Thomas Hardy rewritten by an angry man from Prestwich who has been refused service in a pub.

The music is crucial. Its grinding, rumbling accompaniment never settles into the comforting patterns of folk-rock pastoralism. Instead, the instrumental parts shift against one another, generating a slow, oppressive momentum.

It is not merely a song about rural paranoia. The arrangement behaves paranoically.

The contrast with Draygo's Guilt is revealing. The latter is a surprisingly accessible piece of mid-eighties Fall pop, with the brisk rhythmic confidence of a group that had learned how to make its peculiarities commercially attractive without necessarily surrendering them.

The podcast even invents a splendidly implausible interpretation involving snooker player Tony Drago and a massacre of nuns in Katanga. This is, in its own way, a perfectly legitimate Fall-critical procedure: begin with a fragment of language, follow it towards an increasingly improbable explanation, and hope that Smith never emerges from the grave to contradict you.

Yet Hard Life in the Country possesses something more substantial than eccentricity. Its atmosphere, arrangement and language operate towards the same end. The absurdity is inseparable from the menace.

Show us your house and show your cock

If Hard Life in the Country reveals Smith as a Gothic novelist of the English provinces, Idiot Joy Showland finds him transformed into a particularly vindictive cultural critic.

The target is the commodification of rebellion: musicians performing authenticity, the entertainment industry selling dissent, and audiences apparently content to consume whatever passes for subversion this week.

This is familiar territory, extending from the Situationists through punk's original rhetoric to the more sophisticated cultural pessimism of Guy Debord. The difference is that Debord never had the good fortune to hear Mark E. Smith abusing a collection of hapless indie musicians.

The podcast describes the song's aggression as almost horizontal: a partially unconscious man armed with a flick knife, casually threatening the entire entertainment industry without bothering to stand up.

It is a magnificent description.

The music, meanwhile, presents its own puzzle. Phil hears something of The Monks, the extraordinary German-American garage group whose primitive rhythms and deliberately confrontational performances helped establish a vocabulary later adopted by punk. The comparison is initially greeted with incredulity before becoming rather more persuasive.

The Monks' importance lies partly in their understanding that repetition can itself become a form of aggression. The Fall understood this instinctively.

But Idiot Joy Showland also produces the episode's most beautiful autobiographical digression.

Brendan recalls playing Manchester's Night and Day Café with Phil in the 1990s. During the soundcheck, they noticed Mark E. Smith sitting at the back with a pint. Naturally, they launched into a Fall-influenced number.

Smith listened for approximately twenty seconds, stood up, abandoned his almost full drink and walked out.

There are more devastating critical reviews, but few so economical.

One might imagine the young musicians dreaming of recognition from their hero, perhaps even a grudging compliment. Instead, they received something considerably more authentic: the full Mark E. Smith experience, compressed into twenty seconds and the sacrifice of a perfectly serviceable pint.

The episode suggests the band eventually split up six years later.

Smith's influence was apparently profound.

The plague and the performance

Van Plague, from I Am Kurious Oranj, introduces another version of The Fall: comparatively restrained, melodic, and shaped by the musical and theatrical possibilities opened up by Brix Smith's presence.

Its origins in the Michael Clark ballet project are important. Here was a group frequently characterised as aggressively anti-art participating in a production involving contemporary dance, historical mythology and the visual language of the avant-garde.

Smith's cultural position was always more complicated than the caricature of the belligerent pub intellectual.

The podcast considers several possible meanings of the plague: sexually transmitted disease, AIDS, the historical William of Orange, or perhaps simply an unpleasant smell emanating from somebody's van.

This range of possibilities is not necessarily evidence of profound literary sophistication. Sometimes a strange phrase is merely a strange phrase.

But Van Plague succeeds because the music refuses to resolve the uncertainty. Its comparatively subdued arrangement and Brix's melodic sensibility create an almost tender setting for Smith's grotesque associations.

It is a curious little masterpiece of controlled understatement, suggesting that The Fall could achieve considerable emotional complexity without making a tremendous racket.

The dead do not grow

Then comes I Come and Stand at Your Door, and the evening takes an abrupt turn towards something approaching genuine tragedy.

The song derives from Nâzım Hikmet's poem about a child killed in the atomic bombing of Hiroshima, widely known in English through its musical adaptation as I Come and Stand at Every Door. Its directness is almost shocking in the context of Smith's usual lyrical strategies.

There is no labyrinth of invented characters, no elaborate linguistic sabotage, no peculiar Lancashire grievance. A dead child speaks.

The podcast divides sharply over the recording. Phil hears an unsuccessful exercise in melancholy, its musical language suggesting Joy Division's Atmosphere or Decades without achieving their emotional force. Al finds the production and percussion actively objectionable.

Brendan, by contrast, is deeply moved, particularly by the recording's placement amid an otherwise ridiculous collection of songs about idiots, insults and grotesque characters.

The discussion turns to Grave of the Fireflies and When the Wind Blows, two works that confront the destruction of children through war and catastrophe.

These comparisons expose an important question: what does it mean for art to make suffering bearable without making it pleasurable?

Theodor Adorno's famous anxiety about poetry after Auschwitz is frequently reduced to a prohibition against representing atrocity. His actual concern was considerably more difficult: whether aesthetic form risks transforming suffering into something consumable.

The Fall's recording approaches this problem from an unexpected direction. Smith's voice is neither conventionally beautiful nor reassuring. His delivery resists the sentimental conventions through which popular music often communicates tragedy.

For Brendan, this becomes the recording's greatest strength. For others, it remains an inadequate musical setting for devastating material.

Both responses deserve consideration. Emotional seriousness does not automatically confer artistic success. Equally, a performance need not be conventionally moving to communicate grief.

What matters is that Smith, the great manufacturer of verbal complications, finds himself confronting language whose power depends on its terrible simplicity.

For once, there is nothing to improve by being clever.

The man who insulted everybody

The final pairing returns us to the gutter.

Mad Mock Goth presents Smith in playground-bully mode, directing his contempt towards an identifiable cultural type: the pseudo-Gothic alternative-rock enthusiast, whose carefully assembled appearance apparently warrants prolonged public humiliation.

The podcast connects the song to the early-2000s festival and alternative-music scene, with its curious combinations of Gothic clothing, sportswear and fashionable alienation.

There is a long tradition of British popular comedy built upon the destruction of pretension. From music hall through Tony Hancock, Peter Cook and the more belligerent corners of Viz, the pleasure often lies in watching somebody's carefully maintained dignity being dismantled by a person with considerably less dignity of his own.

Smith belongs firmly within this tradition.

But Mad Mock Goth demonstrates its limitations. A good insult is not necessarily a good song. Its sparse arrangement, repetitive guitar and increasingly obvious lyrical target produce something entertaining without becoming particularly essential.

Insult Song, from Reformation Post TLC, is another matter entirely.

The instrumental backing is, by the podcast's assessment, frequently dreadful. The keyboard wanders about like an elderly relative attempting to locate the lavatory at a wedding reception. The music appears to have been assembled from whatever remained in the rehearsal room after everybody else had gone home.

And yet Smith is magnificent.

His catalogue of characters, accents, peculiar names and increasingly ridiculous observations becomes an extended exercise in comic improvisation. The podcast hears Tommy Cooper, working men's club humour and the magnificent stupidity of a joke that refuses to acknowledge its own stupidity.

Here Smith is not the great literary modernist of critical legend. He is a man making himself laugh.

The distinction matters.

One of the most persistent errors in writing about The Fall is the assumption that Smith's humour must conceal some elaborate intellectual project. Sometimes it does. Frequently, however, the joke is simply that somebody has said something extraordinarily stupid in an extraordinarily funny voice.

There is a democratic vulgarity to this, even if Smith himself was hardly a model of democratic temperament.

The podcast also hears Insult Song alongside Over! Over! as part of the story of the band's post-2006 reconstruction: an old leader acquiring new musicians and discovering that his authority, his resentments and his capacity for mischief remain intact.

Towards the end, Smith appears to become conscious of the recording time being wasted. The performance simply stops.

It is an ending Samuel Beckett might have admired, had Beckett spent more time listening to drunken Lancastrians abusing one another.

The peculiar business of being Mark E. Smith

What connects these recordings is not merely eccentricity. It is Smith's extraordinary ability to move between registers without acknowledging that any movement has occurred.

The rural Gothic, the political diatribe, the avant-garde ballet, the anti-war lament and the playground insult all belong to the same imaginative territory.

Smith's world is one in which the high and low are not opposing categories. They are neighbours, frequently arguing through the wall.

This is why the standard descriptions of The Fall remain so inadequate. Post-punk, experimental rock, garage music, literary modernism: each captures something, but none explains the peculiar pleasure of hearing an apparently serious artistic statement interrupted by a joke about somebody's trousers.

The episode's most revealing contrast is between I Come and Stand at Your Door and Insult Song. One concerns the annihilation of innocence; the other finds an ageing man entertaining himself with increasingly idiotic observations.

It would be absurd to claim they are artistically equivalent.

But their coexistence reveals something essential about The Fall. Smith never accepted that music had to occupy a single emotional or cultural register. His records could be cruel, tender, profound, childish, sophisticated and embarrassingly stupid, sometimes within minutes.

And perhaps the greatest joke is on the critics, forever attempting to construct a grand theory capable of explaining why a man who could channel the ghost of Hiroshima might subsequently spend several minutes doing a terrible accent.

There is no grand theory.

Sometimes the music is sublime. Sometimes it is appalling. Sometimes the lyrics resemble modernist literature, and sometimes they resemble the conversation outside a Wigan chippy at closing time.

The Fall contain all of it.

And somewhere, in a half-empty Manchester pub, Mark E. Smith is still leaving before the band finishes its first song.
