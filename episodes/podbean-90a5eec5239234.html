THE MAN WHO ESCAPED HIS OWN BAND

Mark E. Smith, the freelance apocalypse, and the curious business of being Mark E. Smith without The Fall

By Dr. M. Hexen, Senior Correspondent for Unlicensed Metaphysics

I. HAVE VOICE, WILL TRAVEL

Mark E. Smith was one of the few singers whose voice could function as an entire artistic identity without a recognisable tune, a conventional lyric or, in extreme circumstances, the rest of his band. Most singers interpret music. Smith frequently behaved as though the music had been hired to interpret him.

Outside The Fall, this became an experiment with unusually expensive consequences for the people operating the mixing desk. Remove the group's familiar bass repetitions and abrasive guitars and the voice survives intact: emphatic consonants, sideways timing, peculiar allegations and the air of a man correcting a municipal planning application. Yet a recognisable voice does not automatically make a record interesting.

Consider the Stooges' I Wanna Be Your Dog, recorded with Clint Boon. The Hammond solo is a splendidly incongruous addition to a song whose original power depends upon brutal economy. Smith's presence ought to make the cover stranger. Instead, it exposes a danger of the guest appearance: an established voice can simply be placed on top of an established song. An organ arrives, the singer is famous, the paperwork is complete. Has anything actually happened?

This is the problem running through the solo recordings and collaborations. Smith was at once extraordinarily adaptable and almost impossible to accommodate. He could appear on industrial metal, acid electronics, spoken-word theatre and something resembling a guided meditation for people barred from ordinary meditation. The strongest recordings do not merely display his personality. They discover something his personality can do to the music.

II. SHOPPING LISTS AND PHANTASMAGORIC FLATULENCE

The Post-Nearly Man and Pander! Panda! Panzer! can be difficult to separate in the memory. Their recitations, fragmentary texts and loose electronic settings sometimes resemble a single sprawling project in which Smith has been asked to read everything on his desk, including the desk itself.

That resemblance is both their charm and their problem. A dislocated phrase becomes sinister when Smith leans on the wrong syllable; a banal observation acquires the force of an accusation. But the trick does not work indefinitely. Sometimes a shopping list remains a shopping list, however much electronic fog is sprayed over it.

Beckett understood that repetition could strip speech of its ordinary purpose. Smith, less fastidious, often sounded like a Beckett character who had been sent out for cigarettes and returned with a detailed grievance about the shopkeeper. The comedy matters: his apparent seriousness is repeatedly sabotaged by ridiculous material.

The early experiments also contain an overlooked physical pleasure: the squelch. Acidic, rubbery synthesiser noises suggest bodily functions as much as futuristic machinery. They belong to an electronic tradition in which the synthesiser is not a polished orchestra replacement but an unruly creature making wet noises in a cupboard. Phantasmagoric flatulence, indeed.

Such textures anticipate the more fully realised electronic collisions of Von Südenfed. Yet the solo material is uneven because the musical environment sometimes appears content merely to exist behind Smith. A vocal style is not a composition. One needs pressure, resistance, another intelligence with which the voice can quarrel.

Even the title Pander! Panda! Panzer! summarises the method: near-repetition, linguistic mutation, and the abrupt arrival of a tank. Nursery-rhyme logic applied to cultural aggression. The music is at its best when it behaves with similar intelligence.

III. A RHINO ENTERS THE DISCOTHEQUE

Von Südenfed solved the accompaniment problem by refusing to accompany Smith politely. Mouse on Mars supplied electronics with their own vigorous personality: elastic bass, acid squiggles, sharp rhythmic grids and synth figures that change shape while you look at them.

Rhinohead is instructive. Its force comes not merely from Smith singing over dance music, but from recognisable song architecture: a four-to-the-floor pulse, a memorable synthetic hook and vocals treated as rhythmic and melodic material. Repeated syllables are processed and inserted into the arrangement rather than left to wander around its perimeter.

That difference is enormous. The studio does not simply capture Smith; it makes new music from the grain of his voice. The chorus offers something almost scandalous in his universe: pleasure that can be located, repeated and danced to.

Serious Brainskin works from a related premise but is no duplicate. Its electronic attack makes Smith's vocal irregularities part of the arrangement's tension. Slow Down Ronnie provides another contrast. These recordings demonstrate that collaboration is not a uniform genre but a succession of negotiations between a singer's habits and a machine's expectations.

Dance music promises a collective body moving to a shared clock. Smith sounds as though he has brought his own clock, which is wrong, and intends to prosecute anyone who points this out.

Dada offers one comparison: rational systems interrupted by unruly language. But the music is no conceptual joke. Its rhythms are too muscular, its hooks too persuasive. Von Südenfed's pleasure lies in hearing the disruptive voice become part of the engine without losing its capacity to jam the gears.

A guest decorates a track. A collaborator changes what the track is.

IV. KEVIN AND THE WORKPLACE APOCALYPSE

Mutation's Relentless Confliction sets Smith against a heavier industrial and metal-derived framework. Its aggression seems an obvious match for his bark, but the results raise an awkward question: does a confrontational singer necessarily improve confrontational music?

The music has weight and forward drive. Smith supplies unmistakable character, yet the attraction sometimes lies more in the collision of reputations than in an integrated composition. The guitars are doing one sort of violence; the voice another. They share a room without always sharing a purpose.

Then there is Kevin the sound man.

The famous studio footage in which Smith addresses Kevin has become a miniature classic of the difficult-artist genre. It is tempting to present the confrontation as heroic evidence that Smith refused compromise. The less romantic interpretation is that a working sound engineer was trying to do his job while the singer behaved like the inspector of an imaginary dictatorship.

Studios are organised around control: microphone positions, levels, takes and technical corrections. Smith's authority depended upon seeming impervious to precisely those procedures. The legendary outsider becomes somebody else's occupational hazard.

This is why the Clint Boon Stooges cover matters too. A faithful rendition with a Hammond flourish is entertaining, but has Smith transformed the song or merely signed it? The original already sounds like a primitive ritual. Adding an irritable Mancunian narrator does not automatically make it more primitive.

The strongest collaborations allow reciprocal disturbance. The musician changes Smith's voice, and Smith changes the arrangement. Otherwise the result is karaoke with an unusually good guest list.

V. LOVECRAFT ON RADIO THREE

Smith's voice was practically designed for horror: a messenger carrying fragments of information nobody requested and that may be dangerous to understand. The Horror in Clay makes the connection explicit, entering the world of H. P. Lovecraft's The Call of Cthulhu.

Lovecraft's cosmic horror depends upon the possibility that scientific discovery will reveal humanity's insignificance. Smith's particular horror often depends upon discovering that humanity is already insignificant because the people running it are idiots.

The performance has the measured theatricality of radio drama, an art form in which a convincing voice can summon an invisible architecture. The accompaniment is relatively stripped back, leaving diction and accumulating dread to do much of the work. One could imagine encountering it late at night on Radio 3, immediately before an experimental bassoon recital.

The adaptation works for reasons more specific than Smith's reputation for strangeness. His speech has a natural uncertainty about its point of origin. Is this an author, a character, an unreliable witness, or somebody who wandered into the wrong session? Lovecraft's nested documents and questionable testimony benefit from precisely that instability.

A recitation is not automatically an artistic transformation. Here the material creates a relationship with the voice: ancient entities, unreliable evidence, catastrophic revelation and the bureaucratic absurdity of explaining the inexplicable.

Smith's apparently unmusical delivery could be intensely theatrical. The rhythm of a sentence, pressure on an unexpected word, refusal to land a phrase cleanly: these are performance decisions even when they sound like accidents.

VI. MOLOCULAR MEDITATION

The most surprising destination is Jan St. Werner's Molocular Meditation, emerging from the earlier Molecular Meditation installation. At around twenty minutes, it offers no easy single-song payoff. Instead, Smith's recorded voice inhabits a composed electroacoustic environment of spectral keyboards, unstable tones and shifting electronic spaces.

This is not random Smith utterances with random bleeps underneath. The music develops, swells, recedes and returns. Ordinary phrases acquire uncanny weight through their placement. Voice and electronics begin to behave as parts of the same material.

Pierre Schaeffer's musique concrète provides a useful comparison. Recorded sound can be detached from its everyday source and treated as a musical object. Smith's voice, burdened with recognisable personality, becomes something more elusive: breath, grain, accent, repetition, memory.

The paradox is striking. The performer most associated with confrontation becomes the centre of an experience that can feel almost serene. Not reassuring, exactly. One would hesitate to book Smith as a mindfulness instructor. But the work asks the listener to surrender to a changing sound world rather than hunt for a chorus, punchline or decipherable accusation.

Its scale is not the bombastic epic of a rock group adding strings and announcing maturity. It achieves breadth through the patient arrangement of small events. A phrase is heard, altered, surrounded by new sound and returned in a different light.

The contrast with the solo albums is decisive. Where some recitations place music behind Smith, this composition makes the vocal recording itself musical. The voice is neither decorative guest nor dominating frontman. It is an instrument that can be bent, recontextualised and made unexpectedly tender.

For an artist whose every syllable seemed to carry the stamp of ownership, this surrender of control is remarkable. It may be the most generous thing a collaborator could have done for him.

VII. THE POST-NEARLY MAN

The phrase Post-Nearly Man captures Smith's position outside The Fall. He was nearly a poet, nearly a comedian, nearly a narrator, nearly an electronic musician and, in certain circumstances, nearly a professional workplace grievance. He was not quite any of them.

The range from Rhinohead to Relentless Confliction, from Lovecraft to Molocular Meditation, shows why these recordings cannot be dismissed as curiosities. They are experiments in what happens when a recognisable voice is removed from the machinery that made it famous.

Some fail. The voice becomes a celebrity stamp pasted onto material that would otherwise proceed unchanged. Others succeed because the music resists him, reorganises him or discovers a new use for his peculiar timing.

The difference is not between noisy and quiet music, or serious art and popular entertainment. A dancefloor hook can transform Smith as radically as an electroacoustic installation. A faithful rock cover can leave him oddly stranded.

The Fall themselves were never merely a backing band for a difficult singer. They were a system of tensions: repetition against interruption, groove against accusation, discipline against sabotage. The finest outside collaborations reproduce that argument without reproducing The Fall's sound.

The question was never whether Mark E. Smith could sing over absolutely anything. Plainly he could, and occasionally did. The question was whether anything happened to the music as a consequence.

When the answer was yes, the results were extraordinary. When it was no, someone had merely booked a famous voice and hoped the legend would do the rest.

The finest collaborators built a machine around Smith, switched it on and discovered that, for once, the machine had an opinion of its own.
