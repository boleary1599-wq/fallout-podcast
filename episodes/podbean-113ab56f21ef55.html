The Fall and the Uses of Unreason

By Barmy McGurk

Hexes, shopping bags, chemical enlightenment and a man called Ronnie: how Mark E. Smith turned the ridiculous into a method of understanding the world.

Hexes, History and the Machinery of Belief

The Fall were never particularly interested in the distinction between the profound and the stupid. Mark E. Smith could invoke the occult, European history, working-class Manchester, speculative fiction and the contents of somebody’s shopping bag without suggesting that any one of these subjects deserved more serious treatment than the others. His was a world in which the supernatural and the mundane occupied the same cramped room, frequently arguing over the furniture. This was not surrealism in the conventional sense. Smith rarely seemed interested in transporting the listener into a dream. His achievement was stranger: he made the ordinary world sound as though it had been inadequately explained.

“Hexen Definitive / Strife Knot” belongs to the period when The Fall discovered that repetition could do something more interesting than produce momentum. It could alter the listener’s sense of time. On Perverted by Language, guitar figures recur without the usual promise of resolution. Rhythms continue past the point at which a conventional rock arrangement would introduce a change. Smith’s voice appears to exist simultaneously inside and outside the performance, issuing observations without assuming responsibility for explaining them.

The title does considerable work. A hex suggests an act of supernatural interference; a knot suggests something bound, tangled or deliberately made difficult to undo. The words offer an impression of occult significance without providing a usable system of meaning. Smith understood that mystery becomes less interesting when its mechanism is explained.

Set against it, “Gut of the Quantifier” sounds almost outrageously sociable. The rhythm is springy, the guitar has a rough-edged swagger, and the performance possesses the pleasure of musicians who know exactly how to make a simple idea move. Yet the title is every bit as peculiar. A quantifier belongs to logic, measurement and classification. Giving it a gut transforms an abstraction into something bodily, vulnerable and faintly disgusting. One sounds like a ritual whose purpose has been forgotten. The other sounds like a party attended by people who have brought the wrong equipment. Both depend on the same principle: language should disturb the music rather than merely explain it.

The Supernatural Contents of a Shopping Bag

“Carry Bag Man” sounds like a minor character from a particularly miserable children’s television programme, a local eccentric identified entirely by the object he carries, or a superhero whose powers are too embarrassing to discuss. The recording has an unusual sense of space. Its repetitive foundations create room for the instruments to operate without constantly competing for attention. The rhythm holds things together while the guitars and vocal performance establish an atmosphere of persistent, low-level unease.

Smith’s characters rarely possess the psychological completeness expected of literary protagonists. They arrive as fragments: names, occupations, habits, grievances, bits of overheard speech. He offers enough information to make them memorable and withholds enough to prevent them becoming reassuringly familiar. The carry bag becomes an emblem without ever becoming a symbol that can be satisfactorily decoded.

“Twister” approaches the same period from a different direction. Its emphatic drums, forceful arrangement and sense of forward motion demonstrate how thoroughly the late-eighties Fall could exploit the resources of a comparatively polished recording. The spacious, reverberant drum sound suggests a scale considerably larger than the cramped world of “Carry Bag Man”. The difference exposes a useful misconception: The Fall’s achievement was not dependent upon sounding cheap. They could make extraordinary use of sophisticated recording techniques, provided those techniques were not allowed to impose conventional good taste. Production was another opportunity to make the group sound more like itself.

Chemical Enlightenment and the Failure of Meaning

By the time of “Ketamine Sun”, The Fall had developed an extraordinary capacity for making records that appeared to contain several incompatible songs. Paired with “Octo Realm” on The Unutterable, the track moves through a succession of musical environments rather than developing a single idea in the conventional manner. Smith’s voice occupies a peculiar space between comic monologue, spoken-word performance and the ravings of somebody who has mistaken his private thoughts for public announcements. Then the music opens out.

The keyboards establish an unexpectedly beautiful psychedelic atmosphere, and the recording begins to resemble a damaged version of the expansive alternative rock fashionable during the preceding decade. There are moments when the melodic movement seems almost embarrassingly lush by Fall standards. Yet the beauty arrives surrounded by rubbish. Julia Nagle’s keyboard work helped expand the group’s vocabulary beyond guitar-driven arrangements. Rather than decorating songs, electronic textures could change their atmosphere, introducing melancholy, artificial grandeur and disorientating beauty.

“Symbol of Mordgan” is a much smaller, more deliberately fragmentary experiment. Spoken fragments, references to football, instrumental gestures and apparently incidental noises coexist without developing into a conventional song. John Peel’s familiar broadcasting authority becomes another piece of material to be manipulated. The result recalls tape experiments associated with Faust, although The Fall’s version is more casually irreverent. Not every fragment acquires significance by being placed beside another. Sometimes the collision produces a revelation; sometimes it produces a peculiar little noise. “Ketamine Sun” transforms its discontinuities into a compelling journey; “Symbol of Mordgan” seems content to leave them as discontinuities.

The Right to Make a Terrible Record

There is something almost heroic about a group releasing a recording called “I’m Ronney the Oney”, although heroism is probably not the first quality that comes to mind while listening to it. The track presents relatively straightforward garage-rock instrumentation. The guitar has a rough, familiar insistence, the drums maintain a functional rhythm, and the whole thing sounds rather like a rehearsal promoted beyond its abilities.

For a band whose greatest achievements frequently depend upon repetition, this presents an interesting problem. Why does repetition sometimes produce extraordinary music and sometimes merely produce more of the same? The finest Fall recordings contain productive disagreement between their elements. A rhythm continues too long. A vocal phrase refuses to fit the arrangement. A guitar introduces a noise that makes the rest of the music seem briefly inadequate. Without that friction, the group’s characteristic simplicity can become merely simple.

“Jam Song” is more successful because it allows the listener to hear musical ideas taking shape. The recording begins with uncertainty, its components gradually finding a relationship rather than announcing a finished arrangement. The keyboards have an agreeably ungainly quality, and the rhythm possesses a loose, slightly mechanical character. Smith enters this unstable environment without entirely resolving it. Its structure suggests the music might have gone somewhere else.

There is a temptation among devoted Fall listeners to treat every apparent failure as evidence of some deeper strategy. If a recording sounds unfinished, perhaps that was the intention. If a performance is incompetent, perhaps it exposes the absurdity of competence. Such arguments can become a means of refusing to listen critically. The Fall made bad music. They made tedious music. Acknowledging this does not diminish their achievement. It makes it more remarkable.

Smith’s artistic method depended upon proceeding without knowing whether the results would justify the effort. A hex could become a musical structure. A shopping bag could become a mystery. A chemically dislocated monologue could open into unexpected beauty. An ordinary jam could briefly reveal the process by which a band invents itself. The Fall’s world was not magical because Smith possessed supernatural powers. It was magical because he refused to accept that the familiar world had exhausted its possibilities. Sometimes he was wrong. The remarkable thing is how often he wasn’t.
