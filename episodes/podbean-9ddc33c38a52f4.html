THE GOSPEL ACCORDING TO KING TUBBY'S BROKEN CHRISTMAS TREE

On media enemies, imaginary children, suburban fascism and the troubling possibility that the Beach Boys were a conspiracy against the working classes.

By Professor C. R. Scanlon, Institute of Hostile Aesthetics

I. THE INFORMATION UNDERGROUND

A culture can be judged by the peculiarities it chooses to preserve. Somewhere between the industrial archaeology of *Re/Search* magazine, the outsider electronics of Jean-Jacques Perrey and the exquisitely petty grudges of Mark E. Smith lies an alternative history of postwar popular music: not the victorious procession of great albums and innovative producers, but an inventory of cranks, failed utopias, discarded technologies and men who should probably have been asked to leave the premises.

The Fall belonged to this tradition in spirit, if not always in practice. The extraordinary *Re/Search* enterprise, emerging from the San Francisco punk fanzine *Search & Destroy*, treated J. G. Ballard, William S. Burroughs, industrial culture and the outer limits of recorded music as parts of the same intellectual ecology. That ecology helps explain a Fall catalogue in which a song about a man pushing a pram can sit quite naturally beside a bitter argument with the music press. Each subject is evidence that the world is stranger than the public relations department would have us believe.

The title *Shit King Tubby!* is a joke with a serious ancestry. King Tubby's Jamaican dub experiments made the mixing desk into an instrument, and demonstrated that the apparent leftovers of a recording—echo, empty space, a stray fragment of voice—could become its principal content. The Fall's method was less technically elegant, but similarly hostile to the idea that a recording must be a complete, polished statement. On *Dragnet*, *Perverted by Language* or the later electronic records, apparently disposable fragments become the source of the trouble. The studio is not a place to tidy things up. It is where the evidence is disturbed.

II. JOURNALISM IS NOT A SUBCULTURE

The most revealing Fall records are often about the production of cultural authority. “Printhead”, a vicious little *Dragnet* song, attacks the music press with the special irritation reserved for people who think they are more interesting than the things they cover. Its scratchy guitars, compact punk rhythm and confrontational vocal delivery leave little room for compromise. This is not a sophisticated theory of media institutions. It is a man pointing at an editor and making a noise that suggests the editor has just stood in something.

Yet the grievance has a larger historical resonance. In the late 1970s, the British music weeklies were not merely reporting on punk and post-punk: they were helping decide what those movements meant. The critic could become a minor celebrity, the magazine a gatekeeper, the review a miniature exercise in social control. Smith's contempt is understandable precisely because The Fall were dependent upon this machinery even as they ridiculed it.

Pierre Bourdieu's account of cultural distinction is relevant here. Taste is never simply personal preference; it is also a means of establishing position, superiority and membership. “Printhead” hears this mechanism grinding away and responds with the social tact of a brick through the office window. The song is a particularly effective corrective to the comforting fantasy that the underground is automatically virtuous. It can manufacture its own bureaucrats in leather jackets.

“Wings”, meanwhile, seems to escape such institutional boundaries altogether. Its driving rhythm, repetitive guitar figure and Smith's astonishing time-travel narrative produce an alternative kind of cultural history, one in which chronology is less a sequence than a pile-up. The song's fractured episodes and abrupt changes of perspective resemble the cut-up procedures associated with Burroughs, although the actual effect is more like a half-remembered adventure serial being transmitted from a malfunctioning public house. Its famous video, with its pub imagery and deliberately unglamorous presentation, turns the heroic conventions of rock performance into local theatre. Where “Printhead” attacks the people who explain music, “Wings” creates a world that resists explanation in the first place.

III. THE NATIVITY PLAY IS A SECURITY RISK

There is something profoundly English about the desire to ruin Christmas through excessive attention to detail. “Xmas with Simon” and “Overture from *The Curious Orange*” approach seasonal ritual from opposite directions. The former's comic unease and peculiar backing vocals make the festive occasion seem like a gathering of people who have been warned not to discuss the incident. The latter belongs to The Fall's theatrical collaboration with Michael Clark, where religious narrative becomes a grotesque collision of pageantry, dance and rock performance.

The distinction is instructive. One record reduces Christmas to the social embarrassment of being trapped in a room; the other expands it into a public spectacle. Neither offers much in the way of peace on earth. The British Christmas tradition has always had a disturbing underside, from the supernatural tales of M. R. James to the masked disorder of older seasonal customs. The modern Nativity play is a sanitised descendant of rituals that were not necessarily designed to be comforting.

Smith's Christmas songs work because his voice refuses the usual emotional contract. Sentimentality requires a performer who appears to believe in communal warmth. Smith sounds as though he has just found the communal warmth blocking his driveway. His irritation becomes a means of rescuing the season from its own compulsory cheerfulness. If Charles Dickens invented the modern Christmas as a moral economy of charity and ghosts, The Fall restored the possibility that the ghosts might be drunk and the charity might have an ulterior motive.

The theatrical setting of *The Curious Orange* also raises the question of who, exactly, owns a Fall song. In Michael Clark's world, choreography, costume, historical symbolism and Smith's voice coexist without necessarily agreeing on the meaning of anything. The spectacle becomes an argument between disciplines. This is one of the band's most valuable capacities: to collaborate without becoming accommodating.

IV. A RECIPE WITHOUT A KITCHEN

“Recipe for Fascism” sounds like an accusation assembled from damaged household appliances. Its abrasive, fragmented construction is a long way from the tidy political messaging that the title might imply. Rather than present a programme or a denunciation, the recording makes authoritarianism feel like a process: repetition, pressure, distortion, a voice half-buried in its own machinery.

This is where the Fall's experimentalism becomes more than a decorative commitment to being difficult. Theodor Adorno's warning about the standardisation of popular culture is frequently deployed against uncomplicated commercial music. But the relationship between form and obedience is rarely so simple. A recording that refuses easy consumption does not thereby become politically emancipatory. Equally, an accessible tune does not necessarily make its listener a willing participant in the culture industry. The Fall inhabit the awkward space between these certainties, turning the question into a practical problem of rhythm, texture and vocal authority.

“My Ex-Classmates' Kids” provides the necessary counterweight. The subject is apparently negligible: the offspring of people the narrator once knew. Yet the song locates an entire social world in that trivial encounter. Childhood, ageing, embarrassment and the lingering violence of school hierarchies emerge from the simplest observation. Alan Bennett's monologues sometimes operate in a similar fashion, allowing a tiny domestic incident to disclose an entire system of resentments. Smith has less interest in sympathy. His characters do not need to be understood, only noticed.

This attention to minor social disturbances explains why the grand political vocabulary of “Recipe for Fascism” can coexist with the domestic absurdity of an old acquaintance's children. Both concern the small, habitual arrangements by which people exercise power over one another. The Fall's political imagination is not confined to parliament, protest or ideology. It is also present in the queue, the classroom, the newspaper office and the suburban front window.

V. BEACH BOYS, BAD MEN AND THE PRAM

The extraordinary “Mike's Love Xexagon” turns a Beach Boys reference into something closer to a coded warning. Its unexpected harmonies and elaborate lyrical play suggest a fascination with the friction between immaculate pop craftsmanship and the dubious personalities who may inhabit it. Brian Wilson's melodic architecture was built upon a nearly impossible ambition: to make popular music that sounded like the interior of a troubled mind while retaining the seductive surface of a hit single. The Fall's response is not to imitate *Pet Sounds*, but to vandalise the idea of California harmony with a set of jokes that seem to have escaped from a sinister children's book.

The contrast with “Stout Man” could scarcely be more pronounced. Here is a fat man, a pram, an errand, the everyday physical comedy of someone occupying space. The song's aggressive brevity and barked delivery recall the economy of early rock and roll: a character, a rhythm, a repeated phrase, and no obligation to explain what happened afterwards. Yet its power comes from an almost anti-romantic attention to the ordinary. Rock traditionally gives us rebels, lovers, drifters and outlaws. The Fall give us a man going to the shops.

It is tempting to read this as cruelty. Smith's observational comedy can certainly be vicious, and the line between grotesque portraiture and sneering contempt is not always easy to establish. But the more interesting point is how much musical energy is expended upon such an unpromising subject. The ordinary is not simply mocked; it is granted the dignity of a full-scale rock performance. The pram becomes as worthy of artistic treatment as the apocalypse.

This recalls the peculiar democratic impulse of the British comic tradition, from the crowded social worlds of William Hogarth to the malicious little universes of *Viz*. A ridiculous figure is not necessarily an insignificant figure. In the right hands, he can stand for an entire social order, or at least for the queue outside the off-licence.

VI. THE ARCHIVE IS AN ARGUMENT

The most revealing connection among these recordings is not chronological or stylistic. It is the insistence that popular music ought to retain room for the inconvenient. The music press must be attacked, Christmas made awkward, political slogans broken into fragments, California harmonies contaminated, and the most ordinary human encounter treated as a potentially dangerous narrative event.

The Fall were not always right. Their methods could produce dullness, gratuitous nastiness and experiments that were more interesting in conception than in execution. To pretend otherwise would turn an unruly body of work into the kind of respectable cultural monument Smith would have enjoyed kicking over.

But the contradictions are essential. *Re/Search* made a virtue of preserving the material respectable culture preferred to ignore. King Tubby found possibility in the remnants of songs. Smith's achievement was to make the rejected, the trivial and the irritating sound like legitimate subjects for rock music, even when the results were not especially legitimate themselves.

The great Fall archive is therefore not a museum of masterpieces. It is a disorderly repository of possible meanings, a municipal records office in which the clerk has been replaced by a man shouting about his ex-classmates' children. The documents are incomplete. The witnesses are unreliable. The Christmas decorations have caught fire.

Somewhere in the back room, a tape is still running.
