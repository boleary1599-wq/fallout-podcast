THE ART OF NOT AGREEING

Eight Fall songs, four uneasy verdicts, and the limits of a knockout competition

A Fall knockout is a fundamentally suspect enterprise. You are asked to compare records that frequently seem to be disagreeing about what a record ought to be. The four contests in this episode stretch from the wiry early group to its later, more dislocated incarnations. They are not merely exercises in nostalgia. Each pairing asks whether a song's power resides in the riff, the voice, the arrangement, the words, or in the particular accident by which all four refuse to cooperate.

The opening match, ‘I’m Into C.B.’ against ‘Various Times’, is especially cruel because the two records make almost opposite uses of space. ‘I’m Into C.B.’, on Hex Enduction Hour, has the scale and pressure of something being dragged across a factory floor. The guitars scrape and the rhythm section bears down, while Smith turns citizens’ band radio into a vocabulary of private obsession. It is funny in the way a stranger's overheard argument can be funny: the details are absurd, but the intensity is not. The comedy would be much less effective if the band sounded as though it were in on the joke.

‘Various Times’ comes from a different Fall: comparatively exposed, sharp-edged, almost precarious. Its historical and temporal dislocations are not just lyrical curiosities. The music keeps the listener off balance; the song feels less like a story being told than a message arriving through damaged equipment. In the discussion, the players return to the guitar sound, to the almost physical nastiness of early Fall, and to the peculiar way Smith's delivery can lock into the instruments without behaving like a conventional vocal line. Those are better grounds for comparison than the familiar question of which era was ‘best’. One song accumulates weight; the other makes instability its principal weapon.

The second pairing, ‘You Haven’t Found It Yet’ and ‘Australians in Europe’, is less immediately spectacular and therefore more revealing. The former belongs to Shift-Work, an album whose relative clarity is sometimes mistaken for a retreat. A Fall song can be tuneful without being straightforward: the surface can invite you in while the phrasing keeps shifting the furniture. ‘Australians in Europe’ offers the opposite temptation, inviting listeners to overinterpret its title and then refusing to deliver a tidy explanation. The episode's digressions and disagreements matter here. A song that one person hears as hypnotic another hears as merely meandering. Repetition is not automatically compelling. It has to do something to time.

That distinction becomes even sharper with ‘Sons of Temperance’ against ‘Surmount All Obstacles’. The titles sound like proclamations from rival committees, which is a very Fall sort of coincidence. ‘Sons of Temperance’, from The Unutterable, takes an apparently solemn phrase and makes it unstable through insistence and atmosphere. The band's turn-of-the-century sound could be abrasive, electronic, unexpectedly spacious, sometimes all within the same passage. In the conversation, attention moves toward the more expansive, ‘spacey’ portions of the music. What matters is not simply weirdness but the way a track changes its temperature while continuing to insist on its own logic.

‘Surmount All Obstacles’, from Middle Class Revolt, is a particularly good test of how much weight a Fall song's concept can carry. The title resembles the kind of advice printed on a motivational poster; in Smith's mouth it becomes at once command, mockery and threat. But a splendidly peculiar phrase cannot rescue every arrangement. The hosts' objections to the music are useful because they resist a lazy critical reflex: treating every difficult or ungainly Fall recording as evidence of hidden genius. Sometimes the awkwardness is productive; sometimes it is simply awkward. Serious admiration has to permit the second possibility.

The final collision, ‘Cowboy George’ against ‘Irish’, makes that argument harder still. Late Fall records are often described as though they were made by a diminished version of the earlier band. Yet the group's later work could be both more brutally direct and more bizarrely constructed, with riffs that seem to arrive from one room and vocals from another. ‘Cowboy George’ has a knowingly ridiculous title, but the joke is not a substitute for the music. ‘Irish’ likewise asks to be judged by what the band actually does with repetition, momentum and interruption, not by the prestige of its place in a discography. The discussion circles around those competing demands rather than reaching an objective standard that could settle them.

What distinguishes this episode at its best is not consensus but the sound of criteria being made and unmade. One speaker values the attack of a guitar; another remembers the circumstances in which a song first became indispensable; another refuses to reward a promising idea attached to an unconvincing performance. Such disagreements expose a central truth about The Fall. Smith's records invite grand theories—of repetition, class, place, surrealism, Englishness—but they also insist on being heard as particular pieces of music, some thrilling, some irritating, some both within thirty seconds. The bracket produces winners. Its real achievement is making the reasons for winning look so gloriously inadequate.
